### 223–225 (*Iliad* 2.786–787, 2.796–801, 2.807–808)

#### Lines 223–224

> 223 cum pater ad Priamum mittit Saturnius Irim,
>
> 224 quae doceat fortes uenisse ad bella Pelasgos.

At that moment the story moves to a new action, marked by *cum*. A divine presence intervenes as a father, spoken of as *pater*, though who this father is is not yet said. A message is directed toward the king of Troy, sent *ad Priamum*. He dispatches an envoy, told with *mittit*. The father turns out to be the son of Saturn, appearing as *Saturnius*. As the commentary says, Saturn's son is Jupiter. The divine messenger he sends is Iris, closing the line as *Irim*.

The divine messenger is referred to again as *quae*. Her mission is to inform the king, expressed by *doceat*. The approaching enemy is praised as brave, described as *fortes*, though who these warriors are is not yet said. They have already arrived, recorded with *uenisse*. They have come to wage war, directed *ad bella*. Those described as *fortes* are revealed as the Pelasgians, standing at the end of the line as *Pelasgos*. As the commentary says, the Pelasgians are the Greeks.

#### Line 225

> 225 Nec mora: continuo iussu capit arma parentis

There is no delay, told as *Nec mora*. Action follows at once, designated by *continuo*. He responds in obedience to a command, characterized as *iussu*. He takes up what is needed, declared with *capit*, but who takes them up is said only in the next section. What he takes in hand is weapons, entering as *arma*. That command was given by his father, finishing the verse as *parentis*.
