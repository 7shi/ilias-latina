### 132–135 (*Iliad* 2.53–56, 2.76–83)

#### Line 132

> 132 in coetum proceres remque omnibus ordine pandit:

He calls them into a gathering, brought *in coetum*. The ones who assemble are the leading chiefs, spoken of as *proceres*. He turns to what took place, presenting it as *remque*. The account is shared with the whole assembly, directed to *omnibus*. He recounts the message step by step, delivered with *ordine*. He lays it all open before them, proclaimed with *pandit*.

#### Line 133

> 133 cuncti promittunt socias in proelia uires

The chiefs respond with one voice, appearing as *cuncti*. They give their pledge, declared with *promittunt*. Their support is to be united, described as *socias*, though what they offer together is not yet said. This effort is directed toward war, called *in proelia*. What was described as *socias* is revealed as their fighting strength, closing the line as *uires*.

#### Lines 134–135

> 134 hortanturque ducem. Quorum rex fortia dictis
>
> 135 pectora collaudans grates agit omnibus aequas.

The chiefs also spur on their commander, told with *hortanturque*. The one they urge forward is their king, named *ducem*. The poem turns to these loyal chiefs, referred to as *Quorum*. The commander who hears them is the king, appearing as *rex*. Their spirit is praised as steadfast, described as *fortia*, though what is steadfast is not yet said. He addresses them with spoken words, closing the line as *dictis*.

What was described as *fortia* is revealed as their brave hearts, arriving as *pectora*. He speaks highly of them, portrayed as *collaudans*. He expresses his gratitude, offering *grates*. He renders this acknowledgment, marked by *agit*. It is given to every one of them, returning to *omnibus*. The thanks he shares out are equal to each man, closing the verse as *aequas*.
