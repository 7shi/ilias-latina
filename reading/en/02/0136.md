### 136–138 (*Iliad* 2.212, 2.216, 2.213–215, 2.225–242)

#### Lines 136–138

> 136 Hic tunc Thersites, quo non deformior alter
>
> 137 uenerat ad Troiam nec lingua proteruior ulli,
>
> 138 bella gerenda negat patriasque hortatur ad oras

At this moment a new figure steps in, marked by *Hic*. The time of this interruption is fixed as *tunc*. The speaker who enters is Thersites, named *Thersites*. A comparison begins from him, opened with *quo*. That any man could surpass him is rejected with *non*. No other man was more misshapen, described as *deformior alter*. Among all who had made the journey, told with *uenerat*. Their destination had been Troy, specified as *ad Troiam*. His speech was equally unbridled, and that another matched him is excluded by *nec*. No one possessed a more insolent tongue, characterized as *lingua proteruior*. Such insolence belonged to no one else, closing the description as *ulli*.

He turns to the fighting to be waged, naming the conflict *bella gerenda*. He declares that it must not be fought, refused with *negat*. Instead, their thoughts are directed toward what belongs to their homeland, joined as *patriasque*, though what they seek there is not yet said. He presses the men to return, urging them with *hortatur*. What was described as *patriasque* is revealed as the shores of home, reached *ad oras*.
