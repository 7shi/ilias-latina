### 136–138 (*Iliad* 2.212, 2.216, 2.213–215, 2.225–242)

#### Lines 136–138

> 136 Hic tunc Thersites, quo non deformior alter
>
> 137 uenerat ad Troiam nec lingua proteruior ulli,
>
> 138 bella gerenda negat patriasque hortatur ad oras

At this point a man comes in, marked by *Hic*. It is at that time, marked by *tunc*. The man is Thersites, named *Thersites*. Others are measured against him, marked by *quo*. The measure begins with a denial, marked by *non*. It is about one uglier, described as *deformior*. That one would be another man, named *alter*.

No such man had come, told with *uenerat*. Where he would have come is Troy, given as *ad Troiam*. Another denial follows, marked by *nec*. It concerns a more insolent tongue, named *lingua proteruior*. Such a tongue belonged to no one, spoken of as *ulli*.

Now there are wars to be waged, named *bella gerenda*. He says no to them, told with *negat*. Something belongs to their fathers, described as *patriasque*, though what it is is not yet said. He urges them on, told with *hortatur*. What was described as *patriasque* is the shores they are urged toward, given as *ad oras*. What he urges them to do comes only in the next section.
