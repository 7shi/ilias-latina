### 161–166 (*Iliad* 2.484, 2.487, 2.485, 2.493)

#### Lines 161–163

> 161 Vos mihi nunc, Musae - quid enim non ordine nostis? -,
>
> 162 nomina clara ducum clarosque referte parentes
>
> 163 et dulces patrias: nam sunt haec munera uestra.

The poet turns to divine powers, addressing them as *Vos*. They are to speak to the poet himself, who refers to himself as *mihi*. The appeal comes at this moment, marked by *nunc*. The goddesses he invokes are the Muses, addressed as *Musae*. He asks what they could fail to know, opening the question with *quid*. Their all-seeing knowledge explains the appeal, tied in by *enim*. Nothing is hidden from them, the denial stated with *non*. They know everything in its proper sequence, described as *ordine*. Their understanding is complete, told with *nostis*.

He asks them to recount famous names, calling for *nomina clara*. These belong to the leaders of the host, appearing as *ducum*. Renowned figures are also asked for, described as *clarosque*, though who they are is not yet said. The Muses are bidden to recount them, commanded with *referte*. What was described as *clarosque* is revealed as their parents, named *parentes*. Their dear homelands too are added by *et*, appearing as *dulces patrias*. A reason is offered with *nam*. Such telling belongs to them, affirmed with *sunt*. These tasks are pointed to as *haec*. They are the duties of the goddesses, spoken of as *munera*. The work belongs to the Muses themselves, closing the line as *uestra*.

#### Lines 164–166

> 164 Dicamus quot quisque rates ad Pergama duxit
>
> 165 et coeptum peragamus opus, sitque auctor Apollo
>
> 166 aspiretque libens operi per singula nostro.

The poet proposes to tell the story together with the goddesses, urging: *Dicamus*. He asks how many each one brought, beginning with *quot*, though what was brought is not yet said. Each individual commander is included, appearing as *quisque*. What was counted with *quot* turns out to be the ships, named *rates*. The fleet was directed toward Troy's citadel, spoken of as *ad Pergama*. Each leader brought them there, told with *duxit*. The work already begun is joined by *et* as *coeptum*, though what work it is is not yet said. The poet calls for carrying it through to the end, declared with *peragamus*. What was called *coeptum* is this poetic undertaking, named *opus*.

A wish for divine presence also enters, expressed as *sitque*. He should stand as the guide and inspirer, named *auctor*. The god called upon to inspire it is *Apollo*. The god is asked to favor the undertaking, prayed for with *aspiretque*. He is asked to do so with good will, appearing as *libens*. His favor is directed toward the poem as *operi*. The blessing is to guide every detail, described as *per singula*. The labor is the poet's own, closing the line as *nostro*.
