### 120–128 (*Iliad* 2.16–23, 2.26–29)

#### Lines 120–122

> 120 Nec mora: Somnus abit leuibusque per aera pennis
>
> 121 deuolat in thalamos Agamemnonis: ille sopore
>
> 122 corpus inundatum leni prostratus habebat.

There is no delay, told as *Nec mora*. The god of sleep sets himself to the task, appearing as *Somnus*. He departs, marked by *abit*. Something about his flight is light, described as *leuibusque*, though what carries him is not yet said. His course leads through the open sky, directed *per aera*. What was described as *leuibusque* is revealed as wings, closing the line as *pennis*.

He swoops down from above, reported with *deuolat*. He enters the sleeping chamber, arriving *in thalamos*. The room belongs to the king, named *Agamemnonis*. The king himself is brought before us, referred to as *ille*. He is bound in slumber, spoken of as *sopore*, though its quality is not yet said. The rest encompasses his whole frame, given as *corpus*. It is steeped in that rest, described as *inundatum*. What was spoken of as *sopore* is revealed as gentle, shown as *leni*. The king lies stretched out on his couch, portrayed as *prostratus*. He held himself in this quiet state, concluding the scene as *habebat*.

#### Line 123

> 123 Ad quem sic loquitur curarum operumque leuator:

The messenger approaches the sleeping king, directed *Ad quem*. He prepares to address him in this way, indicated by *sic*. He speaks, told with *loquitur*. The burdens he lifts are troubles of the mind, spoken of as *curarum*. To these are added the toils of life, joined as *operumque*. The god who relieves them both is their reliever, closing the line as *leuator*.

#### Lines 124–128

> 124 "Rex Danaum, Atrida, uigila et mandata Tonantis
>
> 125 quae tibi iussa simul delatus ab aethere porto,
>
> 126 accipe: cum primum Titan se emerserit undis,
>
> 127 fortibus arma iube socios aptare lacertis
>
> 128 et petere Iliacos instructo milite campos."

The speech begins by greeting the ruler of the Danaans, hailed as *Rex Danaum*. As the commentary says, the Danaans are the Greeks. He is addressed as the son of Atreus, appearing as *Atrida*. As the commentary says, this son of Atreus is Agamemnon. He is commanded to shake off sleep, urged with *uigila*. A message is joined to this call, bringing the orders of the Thunderer, joined by *et* and described as *mandata Tonantis*. As the commentary says, the Thunderer is Jupiter.

These orders are referred to again as *quae*. They are directed to the king, offered to him as *tibi*. They come as commands, set down as *iussa*. They are brought at this very moment, marked by *simul*. The messenger has descended from on high, described as *delatus*. His journey comes from the upper air, named *ab aethere*. He carries the charge himself, stated with *porto*.

The king is told to take these orders, commanded with *accipe*. The appointed time begins to be set, opening with *cum*. The earliest moment of that time is marked by *primum*. The sun god is named as the sign, appearing as *Titan*. He is to raise himself, expressed with *se*. He will come up out of the depths, told with *emerserit*. The waters from which he rises close the line as *undis*.

The strength of the warriors is noted, described as *fortibus*, though what is strong is not yet said. The weapons they must take up are their armor, named *arma*. The king must give the order, commanded with *iube*. The charge is given for his companions, spoken of as *socios*. They are to fit the gear to their bodies, told with *aptare*. What was described as *fortibus* is revealed as their arms, closing the line as *lacertis*. A further action is added, to advance toward their objective, joined by *et* and urged with *petere*. Their goal belongs to Ilion, described as *Iliacos*, though what lies there is not yet said. The army is to march in battle array, presented as *instructo milite*. What was described as *Iliacos* is revealed as the plains, ending the speech as *campos*.
