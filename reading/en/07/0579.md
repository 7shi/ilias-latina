### 579–584 (*Iliad* 7.161, 7.168, 7.165–166, 7.162, 7.164, 7.167, 7.163)

#### Line 579

> 579 Nec mora: continuo fraudis commentor Vlixes

There is no delay, given as *Nec mora*. It is at once, marked by *continuo*. Deceit comes in, named *fraudis*. Its contriver comes in, named *commentor*. The contriver is Ulysses, who closes the line as *Vlixes*. What he and the others do comes only at *procedunt*, in the next section.

#### Lines 580–581

> 580 et ferus Idomeneus et notus gente paterna
>
> 581 Meriones Graiumque simul dux acer Atrides

Another is joined to him, marked by *et*. He is fierce, described as *ferus*. The fierce one is Idomeneus, named *Idomeneus*. Another is joined, marked by *et* again. Someone is renowned, described as *notus*, though who it is is not yet said. It is for his lineage, named *gente*. The lineage is his father's, which closes the line as *paterna*.

The renowned one is Meriones, named *Meriones*. The Greeks come in, named *Graiumque*. It is with them, marked by *simul*. Their leader comes in, named *dux*. He is valiant, described as *acer*. The leader is Atrides, who closes the line as *Atrides*. As the commentary says, Atrides here identifies Agamemnon, the chief commander of the Greek army.

#### Lines 582–583

> 582 Aiacesque duo \<et> claris speciosus in armis
>
> 583 Eurypylus magnoque Thoas Andraemone natus

The Ajaxes come in, named *Aiacesque*. They are two, given as *duo*. As the commentary says, the two Ajaxes are the son of Telamon and the son of Oileus. Another is joined, marked by *\<et>*. Something is shining, described as *claris*, though what it is is not yet said. Someone is splendid, described as *speciosus*. What was shining is arms, which close the line as *in armis*.

The splendid one is Eurypylus, named *Eurypylus*. Someone is great, described as *magnoque*. Thoas comes in, named *Thoas*. The great one is Andraemon, named *Andraemone*. Thoas is his son, which closes the line as *natus*.

#### Line 584

> 584 quique manum Veneris uiolauit uulnere tristi

Someone else comes in, as *quique*, though who it is is not yet said. A hand comes in, named *manum*. It is the hand of Venus, named *Veneris*. He harmed it, told with *uiolauit*. It was with a wound, named *uulnere*. The wound is grievous, which closes the line as *tristi*. As the commentary says, this is Diomedes, who earlier wounded Venus.
