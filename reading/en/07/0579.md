### 579–584 (*Iliad* 7.161, 7.168, 7.165–166, 7.162, 7.164, 7.167, 7.163)

#### Line 579

> 579 Nec mora: continuo fraudis commentor Vlixes

There was no delay in answering, and that there was not is told with *Nec*. Delay comes in, named *mora*. At once they answer, marked by *continuo*. Deceit comes in, called *fraudis*. Its contriver comes in, spoken of as *commentor*. The contriver is Ulysses, who closes the line as *Vlixes*.

#### Lines 580–581

> 580 et ferus Idomeneus et notus gente paterna
>
> 581 Meriones Graiumque simul dux acer Atrides

Another is joined to him, marked by *et*. He was fierce, called *ferus*. The fierce one is Idomeneus, named *Idomeneus*. Another is joined, as *et*. Someone was renowned, described as *notus*, though who it is is not yet said. It was for his lineage, given as *gente*. That lineage was his father's, which closes the line as *paterna*.

The renowned one is Meriones, named *Meriones*. The Greeks come in, called *Graiumque*. They join together, marked by *simul*. Their leader comes in, spoken of as *dux*. He was valiant, described as *acer*. The leader is Atrides, who closes the line as *Atrides*. As the commentary says, this son of Atreus is Agamemnon.

#### Lines 582–583

> 582 Aiacesque duo \<et> claris speciosus in armis
>
> 583 Eurypylus magnoque Thoas Andraemone natus

The Ajaxes too join them, named *Aiacesque*. They are two, as *duo*. Another is joined, marked by *\<et>*. Something was shining, described as *claris*, though what was shining is not yet said. He was splendid, called *speciosus*. What was shining was in his arms, which close the line as *in armis*.

The splendid one is Eurypylus, named *Eurypylus*. Someone was great, described as *magnoque*, though who was great is not yet said. Another warrior comes in, called *Thoas*. The great one was Andraemon, spoken of as *Andraemone*. Thoas was his son, which closes the line as *natus*.

#### Line 584

> 584 quique manum Veneris uiolauit uulnere tristi

The one who did a great deed is joined, as *quique*. A hand comes in, named *manum*. It was the hand of Venus, called *Veneris*. He injured it, told with *uiolauit*. The harm was with a wound, given as *uulnere*. The wound was grievous, which closes the line as *tristi*. As the commentary says, this warrior is Diomedes.
