### 449–453 (*Iliad* 5.290, 5.294–296, 5.291–293)

#### Lines 449–451

> 449 Tu quoque Tydidae prostratus, Pandare, dextra
>
> 450 occidis, infelix, accepto uulnere tristi,
>
> 451 dextera qua naris fronti coniungitur imae;

The poem turns to someone directly, addressing him as *Tu*. He is drawn in as well, marked by *quoque*. The son of Tydeus comes in, named *Tydidae*, though what of his is meant is not yet said. The one addressed has been laid low, described as *prostratus*. He is Pandarus, called *Pandare*. What was Tydides' is his right hand, which closes the line as *dextra*.

Pandarus falls, told with *occidis*. He is unfortunate, described as *infelix*. Something has been received, given as *accepto*. What he received is a wound, named *uulnere*. That wound is grievous, which closes the line as *tristi*.

Something on the right is struck, described as *dextera*. It is where the blow falls, marked by *qua*. The part on the right is a nostril, named *naris*. It meets the forehead, called *fronti*. The nostril joins it, told with *coniungitur*. It meets the lowest part of the forehead, which closes the line as *imae*.

#### Lines 452–453

> 452 dissipat et cerebrum galeae cum parte reuulsum
>
> 453 ossaque confossa spargit Tydideus ensis.

Someone scatters something, told with *dissipat*. More follows, joined by *et*. What is scattered is the brain, named *cerebrum*. It belongs to a helmet, called *galeae*. It comes with a part of it, given as *cum parte*. The brain has been torn away, which closes the line as *reuulsum*.

Bones come in as well, named *ossaque*. The bones have been pierced, described as *confossa*. The attacker strews them, told with *spargit*. The weapon belongs to Tydides, called *Tydideus*. What does this is a sword, which closes the line as *ensis*.
