# How the example was built

The example (lines 1–8) was written by hand, and the other sections are
written after it. This account goes through how the example was built,
from the division into groups to the making of a sentence for each word,
and quotes every sentence of it in order.

## Aim

The feel of a word is gained from how it appears together with other
words. The aim of this reading is that relations among the Latin words
grow in the reader simply through reading on, in ordinary English that
has Latin mixed into it. It is not the study of a classical language,
looking up its grammar, but reading that lets those relations grow while
the reader follows the story.

- The Latin is embedded in the sentences as loanwords, and the English
  keeps its own grammar. The sentences do not become a broken mixture of
  English and Latin.
- The reading does not take the form of an explanation of words or of
  grammar. The Latin is not talked about as words; a word appears inside
  a sentence that carries the story, as that part of the story itself.
- A Latin word once met is used again in later sentences in place of the
  English. As the same word comes back carrying what came before, the
  relations build up.

## Premises

- The material is the Latin of the section, its translation and the
  commentary.
- The reader knows no Latin and reads beside the translation and the
  commentary. So the reading does not restate the translation; it hands
  over, one at a time and in the order in which the Latin sets the
  words, what has just been said.
- Each sentence goes from the known to the new. At the start of a
  sentence stands what the reader already has (what has been said so
  far, Latin met before), and a Latin word met for the first time comes
  at its end. English, which puts the weight of a sentence at its end,
  suits this.

## The steps

1. Divide the lines into groups.
2. In each group, take the Latin one word at a time from the start, and
   make one sentence (at most two things) with that word. Only these may
   be taken together: an adjective and a noun side by side (*animas
   fortes*), a short pair side by side describing one person (*bello
   clarus*), and the short phrase a word such as *ex* makes with the word
   it leads (*ex quo*). A clause or a run of words is not quoted at
   once, and the meaning of a run is not told first with the run quoted
   after.
3. A word whose partner comes later is held back where it stands ("is
   not yet said") and joined where the partner comes. Hold a word back
   only where something else is read before its partner comes, as with
   the five held back in the example: *Tristia* (waiting for *funera*),
   *miseris* (waiting for *Grais*), *Latrantum* (waiting for *rostris*,
   with *dedit* between), *trahendos* (waiting across the line for
   *artus*) and *summi* (waiting for *regis*, with *sententia* between). A word whose partner comes next is
   not held back: the two are taken as a pair if they stand side by side
   (*animas fortes*), and otherwise joined as content where the partner
   comes.
4. From the commentary, take only what the Latin does not say (whom a
   father's name or a periphrasis means).
5. Reread, and settle the endings of the sentences, repetitions and the
   handling of the connecting words.

## 1. Groups

A line whose sense closes within itself is a group of its own; where a
word of one line is completed only in the next, the lines are put
together.

- Line 1: *Pelidae* and *superbi* stand apart, but within the same line.
  One line, one group.
- Lines 2, 3 and 6: each closes within itself.
- Lines 4–5: what *trahendos* (what is to be torn) in line 4 refers to
  is not known until *artus* in line 5. They are put together.
- Lines 7–8: whose hearts the *discordia pectora* (hearts at odds) of
  line 7 are is known from the two names in line 8. They are put
  together.

## 2. Line 1: from the known to the new

> 1 Iram pande mihi Pelidae, Diua, superbi

**Iram** The reader has nothing yet. So the first sentence gives the
content in English, and the next takes that content up and puts the
word at its end.

> The poem begins by telling of a man's anger. That anger is the opening *Iram*.

The anger the reader gained in the first sentence stands at the front of
the next as the known. The content is said once, and the word is shown
once.

**pande** At the front stands the known *Iram*, in place of the English,
and the new word comes at the end. It is not explained; it is the
poet's own command, given as he speaks it.

> The poet asks for this *Iram* to be laid open and commands: *pande*.

**mihi** The one to whom it is laid open.

> It is to be laid open to the poet himself, who refers to himself as *mihi*.

**Pelidae** How the owner of the anger is called is said as part of the
story.

> The man whose *Iram* it is goes not by his own name; after his father Peleus, he is called *Pelidae*.

Who he is is not said here. His name comes in line 8, and is joined
there.

**Diua** In the order of the words, the one addressed appears here.

> Here the poet reveals whom he is asking. The one he calls upon is a goddess, addressed as *Diua*.

**superbi** It goes with *Pelidae*, earlier in the line. The reader
already has *Pelidae*, so it is put first and joined.

> At the end of the line the poem comes back to *Pelidae* and, judging him a proud man, sets him down as *superbi*.

Last, one sentence may say what the line as a whole has brought
together.

> So the first line alone brings together the theme of anger, the speaker, the man whose anger it is, and the goddess.

## 3. Line 2: holding back and revealing

> 2 Tristia quae miseris iniecit funera Grais

In this line there are two pairs in which the adjective comes first and
the noun later (*Tristia … funera*, *miseris … Grais*), with other words
between. The first word is held back ("is not yet said") and the later
one reveals it.

> From line 2 on, the poem tells what this *Iram* did. It first speaks of the outcome as grievous, calling it *Tristia*, but what is grievous is not yet said.

*quae* is not named by a term of grammar; it is said by what it does in
the story.

> The account runs on, and the *Iram* is referred to again as *quae*.

> The victims, too, are pitied before they are named, and are shown as *miseris*. That the anger cast this upon them is told with *iniecit*.

The two held back are revealed in the order they come. The earlier word
is taken up again in Latin, not turned back into English.

> Only now is the content revealed. What was called *Tristia* is death, and it is named *funera*. Those shown as *miseris* are the Greeks, who close the line as *Grais*.

## 4. Line 3: weaving in a connecting word

> 3 Atque animas fortes heroum tradidit Orco

*Atque* is not given a sentence of its own. It goes into a sentence that
says how this follows on from what came before. The *funera* of the
previous line is used here as known.

> The work of the *Iram* does not stop at *funera*; with *Atque* it goes further.

*animas fortes* is a noun and an adjective side by side, so they are
taken together.

> Next come the souls of the dead, valiant souls, described as *animas fortes*. These souls belong to heroes, spoken of as *heroum*. The anger hands these *animas fortes* over into someone's keeping, and that handing over is *tradidit*. The one who receives them is Orcus, the power of the dead, named *Orco*.

## 5. Lines 4–5: holding back across a line

> 4 Latrantumque dedit rostris uolucrumque trahendos
>
> 5 Illorum exsangues, inhumatis ossibus, artus.

First, where the story moves to is given as known.

> Once the souls have gone to *Orco*, the poem turns to what becomes of the bodies left behind.

*Latrantum* means barking creatures, but what they have to do with it is
not yet known. That it is not known is said as it is.

> Line 4 begins with barking creatures, that is, dogs, called *Latrantum*. What the dogs have to do with it is not yet clear.

*-que* goes into a sentence that says how this follows on.

> A third work of the anger is joined to the others by *-que*. The anger gave something away, and this giving is *dedit*.

When *rostris* comes, the *Latrantum* held back is joined.

> The gift went to mouths, which appear as *rostris*. Only here does it turn out that the *Latrantum* own these mouths.

The Latin words met so far are joined with one another, without turning
them back into English.

> Birds from the sky come to the same mouths, joining in as *uolucrumque*. The *Latrantum* on the ground and the *uolucrum* in the air share the one *rostris*.

What *trahendos* refers to does not come until the end of line 5. It is
held back across the line.

> What is given is doomed to be torn apart, and is declared *trahendos*. But what is to be torn is still not said, even when the line ends.

Line 5 starts from the known (the *heroum* of line 3).

> Line 5 begins by saying whose these are. They belong to the *heroum* of line 3, who are referred to again as *Illorum*. Their bodies have already lost their blood, and are described as *exsangues*. Their bones are left without burial, and that state is *inhumatis ossibus*.

The last word reveals what was held back.

> After this long wait, what was declared *trahendos* is revealed. It is the limbs of the heroes, which stand at the end of the line as *artus*.

## 6. Line 6: a connecting word of reason, and an adjective apart from its noun

> 6 Confiebat enim summi sententia regis,

*enim* goes into the flow of the sentences about the fulfillment, as its
relation (a reason) to the havoc before it.

> All the havoc told so far was in fact part of something being fulfilled, and line 6 opens with that fulfillment, told as *Confiebat*. This fulfillment is the reason for all that havoc, and the two are tied together at once by *enim*.

*summi … regis* stand apart, with *sententia* between them. *summi* is
held back and *regis* joins it.

> The one whose purpose was being fulfilled is set above all others and called *summi*, though it is not yet said what he is. While we wait, his will appears as *sententia*. At the end of the line the highest one turns out to be a king, named *regis*. So *summi* and *regis* come together, with *sententia* between them.

The Latin does not give the king's name. Who is meant is left to the
commentary.

> Yet the king's name is not given. Who is meant is left to the commentary.

## 7. Lines 7–8: the mark of doubt, and the name revealed

> 7 protulerant* ex quo discordia pectora pugnas,
>
> 8 Sceptriger Atrides et bello clarus Achilles.

The *sententia* of the previous line carries the story on as known. The
base text's mark of doubt (`*`) is noted briefly, once, and the word is
read as it stands.

> Next the poem tells when the king's *sententia* began to be fulfilled. First something had been stirred up, told as *protulerant*. The base text marks this *protulerant* with a sign of doubt, but here it is read as it stands.

> This event is the starting point, and the time since then is marked with *ex quo*. It was stirred up by hearts set against each other, described as *discordia pectora*. What these *discordia pectora* stirred up was strife, named *pugnas*.

Line 8 reveals whose were the hearts of line 7. *Sceptriger Atrides* and
*bello clarus* are each taken together, and *et* goes into the sentence
of the second man.

> Line 8 reveals whose hearts these were. The first is the son of Atreus, who holds the scepter as the sign of his kingship; he is called *Sceptriger Atrides*. The second is joined to him by *et*: a man who has made his name in war, described as *bello clarus*. The poem keeps his name for the end, and that name is *Achilles*.

Only what the Latin does not say (who the *Pelidae* of line 1 is) is
taken from the commentary. The identification is made here, where the
name comes, though it looks back to line 1. It names the man in English,
not by the Latin word: the son of Peleus, not *Pelidae*.

> The scepter and the fame won in war, on which each of the two takes his stand, are set side by side. As the commentary says, the son of Peleus in line 1 is this Achilles. The man whose *Iram* opened the poem is called by his name only now.

This section does not say whom *Atrides* means. So when *Atrides* comes
in a later section, it is identified there as Agamemnon from the
commentary.

## 8. Final checks

- **Endings**: spread the endings of the sentences ("commands: *X*", "is
  called *X*", "is named *X*", "is described as *X*", "is spoken of as
  *X*", "is referred to again as *X*", "appears as *X*", "is declared
  *X*", "is told with *X*", "stand at the end of the line as *X*"), and
  do not let one ending follow another. A sentence that ends "… is *X*"
  ("That anger is the opening *Iram*", "that handing over is
  *tradidit*") comes at most once a paragraph, as in the example. "Is shown as *X*" and "is
  revealed" are handy and so pile up; keep them to one or two a
  paragraph.
- **Do not hold back too much**: "what … is not yet said", "is not yet
  clear" are used only where step 3 allows, when something else is read
  before the partner comes. If one group uses them many times, pairs that
  come next to each other are being held back.
- **Do not slot in**: do not put a Latin word in the middle of an
  English sentence in place of an English word ("then, *deinde*, he
  throws the spear", "he runs here, *huc*"). The English grammar breaks.
  Put it in a sentence that says the content: "The throw comes after
  that, told with *deinde*."
- **Do not talk of punctuation or the shape of a sentence**: do not
  explain where a sentence breaks or how it is built ("the phrase runs
  past the comma at the end of the line", "how it is, is told with
  *est*"). If something is carried on to the next line, say what is
  carried on in terms of the content.
- **Do not make a new word the topic or subject**: do not put a Latin
  word met for the first time at the front of a sentence ("*X* tells
  …", "The *X* at the end of the line shows …"). Put it at the end:
  "… commands: *X*", "… calls him *X*". A word met before may stand at
  the front of a later sentence.
- **Do not talk of words**: do not explain the Latin as words ("the word
  for wrath is *Iram*", "the verb *pande*", "*superbi* means proud"), and
  do not quote a gloss. The one exception is the brief note on the mark
  of doubt.
- **Use the words already met**: a word once met is taken up again in
  Latin, not turned back into English (this *Iram*, what was called
  *Tristia*).
- **Say it once**: the sentence that gives the content and the sentence
  that shows the word do not repeat the same meaning.
- **Do not go over it again in closing**: do not close by setting out
  again the Latin just read ("So the *corpora* spread over the field and
  the *sanguine* soaks the *prata*"). Do not add a sentence that adds
  nothing ("The story goes on from here"). "So the first line alone
  brings together …" at the end of line 1 says what the line has brought
  together; it does not go over it again. "So *summi* and *regis* come
  together, with *sententia* between them" in line 6 says that a word
  held back has been joined; it does not go over it again either. But
  that sentence may close only a pair held back as step 3 allows, and
  only once a section. Do not put it after a pair not held back or a
  pair side by side (*more ferarum*, *exsanguia membra*).
- **Do not use terms of grammar**: say what a word does in the story:
  *quae* refers to the *Iram* again, *trahendos* is doomed to be torn,
  *enim* ties the fulfillment to the havoc as its reason.
- **Identifications**: one sentence, "As the commentary says, …", for
  what the Latin does not say, made where the verses bring what is
  needed, and once a book. It names the person in English, not by the Latin word ("the
  son of Peleus in line 1 is this Achilles", not "*Pelidae* is
  Achilles").
- **Connecting words**: *et*, *-que*, *atque*, *enim* and the like are
  not given a sentence of their own; they go into the sentence of what
  they connect. A word with *-que* is shown whole, by its own meaning,
  and the connection melts into the flow of the sentence
  (*uolucrumque*). Where a sentence about the connection is needed of
  its own, *-que* alone may go into it, and the word may be shown
  without *-que* (*Latrantum* and *-que*). In neither case are both set
  in one sentence, as in "together with *-que*, *uolucrumque*".
- **"It", "he", "his"**: do not use them where they could be read as
  pointing to another person ("the girl's father", not "his father",
  right after another man).

## Forms to avoid

These are forms that break the aim, with the form the example uses
instead.

1. **A table of grammar**: the forms and functions of each word set out
   in a table. No explanation of grammar is needed; the example is a
   chain of sentences that name the known by the new.
2. **Saying one thing twice**: "The poem begins with anger. The anger is
   called *Iram*." Once is enough: "The poem begins by telling of a man's
   anger. That anger is the opening *Iram*."
3. **One ending over and over**: "… is called *X*" sentence after
   sentence. Vary the endings.
4. **Apposition**: a gloss set beside the word, as in "the 'wrath',
   *Iram*". Let the meaning melt into the sentence.
5. **A quoted gloss**: "*mihi*, 'to me'", "that 'highest' is *summi*". A
   quoted gloss does the work of apposition even when it is not set
   beside the word. Say the meaning as the content of the sentence: "who
   refers to himself as *mihi*", "set above all others and called
   *summi*".
6. **A new word at the start**: "The next word, *pande*, asks for this
   *Iram* to be revealed." The new comes first. Move the new word to the
   end of the sentence.
7. **The Latin explained as words**: "the word that calls him proud is
   *superbi*", "that word is *Tristia*". This takes the form of a word
   list. Embed the word in a sentence that tells the content: "judging
   him a proud man, sets him down as *superbi*", "speaks of the outcome
   as grievous, calling it *Tristia*".
8. **A list of glosses**: the meanings of the words set out in order,
   with nothing flowing. Keep the order of the Latin and let the content
   and the words both become clear as the reader goes on.
