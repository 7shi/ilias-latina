### 888–891 (*Iliad* 18.587–589, 18.516–517)

#### Line 888

> 888 tondent prata greges, pendent in rupe capellae.

Someone crops something, told with *tondent*. The meadows appear as *prata*. Who crops them is flocks, named *greges*. Someone clings, told with *pendent*. The cliff comes in, given as *in rupe*. Little goats come in, which close the line as *capellae*.

#### Line 889

> 889 Haec inter mediis stabat Mars aureus armis,

Among these comes in, given as *Haec inter*. Something is in the middle, described as *mediis*, though what it is is not yet said. Someone stood, told with *stabat*. Mars comes in, called *Mars*. As the commentary says, Mars is the Roman name for the war-god whom Homer calls Ares. He is golden, described as *aureus*. What is in the middle is arms, which close the line as *armis*.

#### Lines 890–891

> 890 quem diua poesis reliquae* circaque sedebant
>
> 891 anguineis maestae Clotho Lachesisque capillis.

Mars comes back, as *quem*. A goddess comes in, named *diua*. As the commentary says, the verse is damaged after the goddess, given as *poesis*. The others come in as well, described as *reliquae*. The base text marks this *reliquae* with a sign of doubt, but here it is read as it stands. Around comes in as well, marked by *circaque*. They were seated, which closes the line as *sedebant*.

Something is snaky, described as *anguineis*, though what it is is not yet said. They are mournful, described as *maestae*. Clotho comes in, called *Clotho*. As the commentary says, Clotho and Lachesis are two of the Fates, the goddesses who determine the course and duration of human lives. Lachesis comes in as well, called *Lachesisque*. What is snaky is hair, which ends the eighteenth book as *capillis*.
