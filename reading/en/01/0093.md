### 93–96 (*Iliad* 1.517, 1.522–523, 1.531–532)

#### Lines 93–96

> 93 Iuppiter haec contra: "Tristes depone querelas,
>
> 94 magni diua maris, mecum labor iste manebit.
>
> 95 Tu solare tui maerentia pectora nati."
>
> 96 Dixit. At illa leues caeli delapsa per auras

The king of the gods answers by name as *Iuppiter*. What he speaks in reply is designated as *haec*. He speaks in answer to her plea, marked by *contra*. He bids her lay aside sorrow, calling it *Tristes*, though what is sorrowful is not yet said. He tells her to put it away, commanded with *depone*. What was called *Tristes* is revealed as her complaints, appearing as *querelas*.

The domain he mentions is vast, described as *magni*. He addresses her as a goddess, spoken of as *diua*. The realm that is *magni* turns out to be the sea, returning as *maris*. The responsibility will rest with him, told as *mecum*. What he takes upon himself is the burden of redress, named *labor*. The trouble she brought before him is indicated as *iste*. It will stay in his hands, closing the line as *manebit*.

He turns the task back to her, addressing her as *Tu*. He directs her to bring comfort, urged with *solare*. She is to console what is her own, claimed as *tui*. The heart she must soothe is grieving, described as *maerentia pectora*. That grieving heart belongs to her son, who closes his speech as *nati*.

Jupiter concludes his promise, told with *Dixit*. The poem turns now to Thetis, beginning with *At*. The goddess herself is referred to as *illa*. The path she takes is light, described as *leues*, though what is light is not yet said. It belongs to heaven, named *caeli*. She glides downward from the heights, given as *delapsa*. What was described as *leues* is revealed as the breezes, which close the line as *per auras*.
