### 98–105 (*Iliad* 1.536–539, 1.555–559, 4.60–61, 1.540–543, 1.560–569)

#### Lines 98–105

> 98 Offensa est Iuno: "Tantum"que ait, "optime coniunx,
>
> 99 Doride nata ualet, tantum debetur Achilli,
>
> 100 ut mihi quae coniunx dicor tua quaeque sororis
>
> 101 dulce fero nomen, dilectos fundere Achiuos
>
> 102 et Troum renouare uelis in proelia uires?
>
> 103 Haec ita dona refers nobis? sic diligor a te?"
>
> 104 Talibus incusat dictis irata Tonantem
>
> 105 inque uicem summi patitur conuicia regis.

Anger is aroused in someone, described as *Offensa*. Her resentment is complete, confirmed by *est*. The one who takes offense is the queen of heaven, named *Iuno*. She demands to know how great this favor is: *Tantum*. She joins her words to her anger with *-que*. She speaks her indignation, told with *ait*. She addresses him with bitter irony, designated as *optime coniunx*.

She names the mother as *Doride*. Her daughter has such influence, spoken of as *nata*. As the commentary says, this daughter of Doris is Thetis. That daughter prevails so far, told with *ualet*. So much is granted to her, repeated as *tantum*. It is owed to someone, given as *debetur*. The debt is claimed for Achilles, who closes the line as *Achilli*.

The result of this favor follows with *ut*. The slight falls upon Juno herself, pointing to herself as *mihi*. She refers to her own standing, appearing as *quae*. She holds the position of wife, spoken of as *coniunx*. She is called so, told with *dicor*. She is his own, claimed as *tua*. She claims another bond as well, joined as *quaeque*. She holds the title of sister, described as *sororis*, though what belongs to a sister is not yet said.

That title is sweet, described as *dulce*. She bears it, told with *fero*. What was spoken of as *sororis* is revealed as her name, appearing as *nomen*. The Greeks she favors are dear to her, designated as *dilectos*. Jupiter is willing to scatter them in defeat, expressed by *fundere*. Those who are *dilectos* are the Greeks, who close the line as *Achiuos*.

He also aids the other side, joined by *et*. The enemies whose cause he revives are the Trojans, named *Troum*. He would restore them, told with *renouare*. He is willing to do this, asked with *uelis*. He readies them for battle, given as *in proelia*. What he restores to them is their strength, closing the question as *uires*.

She points to these consequences, appearing as *Haec*. She asks if it is in this way, marked by *ita*. These are the gifts he brings, named *dona*. He returns them to her, told with *refers*. He gives them to his wife, speaking of herself as *nobis*. She questions if she is treated in this way, indicated by *sic*. She is loved by him, asked with *diligor*. The love she questions is from him, which closes her protest as *a te*.

She reproaches him with such things, spoken of as *Talibus*, though what kind they are is not yet said. She blames him, told with *incusat*. What was indicated as *Talibus* is revealed as her words, appearing as *dictis*. She is furious as she speaks, described as *irata*. The god she reproaches is the Thunderer, who closes the line as *Tonantem*.

She receives a retort in turn, told as *inque uicem*. The ruler who answers is the highest, described as *summi*. She endures his rebuke, given as *patitur*. What she bears is harsh abuse, appearing as *conuicia*. That abuse comes from the king, who closes the section as *regis*.
