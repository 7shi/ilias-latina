### 74–77 (*Iliad* 1.188–194)

#### Lines 74–76

> 74 At ferus Aeacides nudato protinus ense
>
> 75 tendit in Atriden et, ni sibi reddat honestae
>
> 76 munera militiae, letum crudele minatur,

The robbery brings an immediate response, opening with *At*. Achilles rises in fierce anger, appearing as *ferus Aeacides*. As the commentary says, this descendant of Aeacus is Achilles. His weapon is already bare, described as *nudato*, though what is drawn is not yet said. He bares it without hesitation, told with *protinus*. What was described as *nudato* is his sword, which closes the line as *ense*.

He moves straight forward, told with *tendit*. His advance is directed against the king, given as *in Atriden*. He adds threats of what is to come, joined by *et*. He sets a condition for the king's life, opening with *ni*. He demands that the prize be given back to him, pointing to himself as *sibi*. The king must restore what he took, marked by *reddat*. What was taken was won with honor, described as *honestae*, though what was honorable is not yet said.

What was described as *honestae* is revealed as his rewards, appearing as *munera*. Those rewards were won in war, named *militiae*. If refused, he will inflict a violent end, described as *letum crudele*. This is the vengeance he threatens, which closes the thought as *minatur*.

#### Line 77

> 77 nec minus ille parat contra defendere se ense.

The king does not yield to the threat, given as *nec*. His readiness is no less firm, marked by *minus*. Agamemnon stands his ground, returning as *ille*. He makes ready for the fight, told with *parat*. He turns to face him, indicated by *contra*. He intends to guard his life, expressed with *defendere*. The person he protects is himself, appearing as *se*. His weapon is his sword, which closes the verse as *ense*.
