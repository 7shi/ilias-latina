### 32–43 (*Iliad* 1.37, 1.39–42)

#### Lines 32–33

> 32 "Quid coluisse mihi tua numina, Delphice, prodest
>
> 33 aut castam uitam multos duxisse per annos?

Chryses opens his prayer with a question: *Quid*. He recalls his worship of the god, told with *coluisse*. He asks what good it did him, and points to himself as *mihi*. What he worshipped was the god's divinity, spoken of as *tua numina*. He calls the god by his sanctuary at Delphi, addressing him as *Delphice*. As the commentary says, the Delphic god is Apollo. The good he asks about comes last, as *prodest*.

He sets beside this another claim, joined by *aut*. He has led a pure life, described as *castam uitam*. It went on through many of something, marked as *multos*, though what they are is not yet said. He has led that life, told with *duxisse*. What was marked as *multos* turns out to be the years through which it ran, told as *per annos*.

#### Lines 34–35

> 34 Quidue iuuat sacros posuisse altaribus ignes,
>
> 35 si tuus externo iam spernor ab hoste sacerdos?

A second question joins the first, asked as *Quidue*. It asks what help there is, told with *iuuat*. He recalls something holy, described as *sacros*, though what it is is not yet said. He had set it out, told with *posuisse*. The place was the god's altars, named *altaribus*. What was called *sacros* is now revealed as fires, appearing as *ignes*.

The condition that makes all this worthless comes with *si*. He is the god's own, called *tuus*, though what he is to the god is not yet said. Someone from abroad stands against him, described as *externo*, but who that is is not yet said either. This happens now, marked by *iam*. He is scorned, told with *spernor*. The scorn comes from an enemy, the one called *externo*, given as *ab hoste*. Finally, the one called *tuus* is revealed as the god's priest, who closes the line as *sacerdos*.

#### Line 36

> 36 En, haec desertae redduntur dona senectae?

Chryses calls on the god to look, and cries: *En*. He points to what he has been given as *haec*. Something of his is forsaken, described as *desertae*, though what it is is not yet said. These things are given to him in return, told with *redduntur*. What he pointed to as *haec* are gifts, returning as *dona*. At the end of the line what was called *desertae* is revealed as his old age, which closes the question as *senectae*.

#### Line 37

> 37 Si gratus tibi sum, sim te sub uindice tutus.

He speaks on a condition, marked by *Si*. The condition is that he is dear, described as *gratus*. He is dear to the god, who is addressed as *tibi*. That he is so is told with *sum*. His prayer follows, told with *sim*, though what he prays to be is not yet said. He looks to the god himself, pointing to him as *te*. He wants to be under the god as his protector, told as *sub uindice*. What he prays to be is safe, which closes the line as *tutus*.

#### Lines 38–39

> 38 Aut si qua, ut luerem sub acerbo crimine poenas,
>
> 39 inscius admisi, cur o tua dextera cessat?

Then he turns to the other possibility, joined by *Aut*. Again he speaks on a condition, marked by *si*. It concerns something, spoken of as *qua*, but what was done is not yet told. What it would bring follows, led in by *ut*. It is that he should pay, told with *luerem*. He would pay under a bitter charge, given as *sub acerbo crimine*. What he would pay is the penalty, which appears as *poenas*.

If he has done wrong, it was without knowing, described as *inscius*. What was spoken of as *qua* turns out to be a wrong he has done, told with *admisi*. If so, he asks: *cur*. He cries out to the god with *o*. What he asks about is the god's right hand, spoken of as *tua dextera*. It stays idle, and that idleness is *cessat*.

#### Line 40

> 40 Posce sacros arcus, in me tua derige tela:

The priest bids the god act, commanding: *Posce*. What the god is to call for is his sacred bow, described as *sacros arcus*. The attack is to turn on the priest himself, told as *in me*. What is to be aimed is the god's own, called *tua*. He bids him aim, commanding again: *derige*. What the god is to aim are his arrows, which close the line as *tela*.

#### Lines 41–43

> 41 auctor mortis erit certe deus. Ecce, merentem
>
> 42 fige patrem; cur nata luit peccata parentis
>
> 43 atque hostis duri patitur miseranda cubile?."

He finds something to hold to: there will be a cause of his death, spoken of as *auctor*. What it causes is his death, named *mortis*. This will be so, told with *erit*. At least that much is sure, marked by *certe*. The cause will be a god, closing the thought as *deus*. Then he calls for the god's attention: *Ecce*. He speaks of one who deserves the blow, described as *merentem*, but who that is comes only in the next line.

He bids the god strike, commanding: *fige*. What was described as *merentem* is the father himself, named *patrem*. Then he asks another question: *cur*. It is about his daughter, who returns as *nata*. She pays for something, told with *luit*. What she pays for are wrongs, named *peccata*. These wrongs belong to her parent, spoken of as *parentis*.

Her suffering goes on, joined by *atque*. What follows belongs to a cruel enemy, spoken of as *hostis duri*, though what of his it is is not yet said. She endures it, told with *patitur*. She is to be pitied, described as *miseranda*. What she must endure is the enemy's bed, which closes the prayer as *cubile*.
