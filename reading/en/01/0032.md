### 32–43 (*Iliad* 1.37, 1.39–42)

#### Lines 32–33

> 32 "Quid coluisse mihi tua numina, Delphice, prodest
>
> 33 aut castam uitam multos duxisse per annos?

Chryses opens his prayer by questioning the value of his service, asking what with *Quid*. He recalls that he has long worshipped the god, a devotion told with *coluisse*. He asks what benefit this brought to himself, who appears as *mihi*. The object of his worship was the god's divine presence, spoken of as *tua numina*. He addresses Apollo directly by his sanctuary, calling him *Delphice*. As the commentary says, the Delphic god is Apollo. He asks what good all this has done him, closing the verse with *prodest*.

He sets beside this another claim on the god, joined by *aut*. He has lived an undefiled life in the god's service, described as *castam uitam*. This devoted life went on through a great length of time, marked by *multos*, though the span is not yet named. Such was the life he has led, a continuation told with *duxisse*. He walked that path through the years, advancing with *per*. What was described by *multos* is revealed as the passing years, which close the line as *annos*.

#### Lines 34–35

> 34 Quidue iuuat sacros posuisse altaribus ignes,
>
> 35 si tuus externo iam spernor ab hoste sacerdos?

A second question joins the first, asking what use it is with *Quidue*. He asks how it avails him, told with *iuuat*. He recalls the ritual offerings, described as *sacros*, though what was holy is not yet said. He had set them forth for the god, an offering told with *posuisse*. The place where he offered them was upon the god's shrines, named *altaribus*. What was declared *sacros* is now revealed as the altar fires, appearing as *ignes*.

The condition under which this worship seems worthless begins with *si*. The priest claims his bond to Apollo, claiming to be *tuus*, though who is his is held back. His oppressor comes from an alien land, described as *externo*, with the enemy held back. The humiliation happens even now, marked by *iam*. He finds himself spurned and cast out, an indignity declared with *spernor*. He is rejected at the hands of someone, led in by *ab*. The alien attacker who was called *externo* is named as an enemy, appearing as *hoste*. Finally, the one who claimed to be *tuus* stands revealed as the god's own priest, closing the verse as *sacerdos*.

#### Line 36

> 36 En, haec desertae redduntur dona senectae?

Chryses points to his present plight, calling for notice with *En*. He gestures toward what he has received, pointing to them as *haec*, though the gifts wait. His years are left lonely and abandoned, described as *desertae*, with his age held back. He asks if such are the returns given back to him, told with *redduntur*. What was pointed to with *haec* is called gifts, returning as *dona*. At the end of the line, what was described as *desertae* is revealed as his old age, closing the question with *senectae*.

#### Line 37

> 37 Si gratus tibi sum, sim te sub uindice tutus.

He appeals again to the god's goodwill, opening with *Si*. He asks whether his devotion has found favor, describing himself as *gratus*. The one whose favor matters is Apollo, addressed as *tibi*. He puts his present standing to the test, told with *sum*. If that favor holds, he asks that he may be preserved, praying with *sim*. He looks to Apollo himself, pointing to him as *te*. He seeks shelter beneath the god's power, stepping beneath it with *sub*. The god is called upon to be his defender, named as his *uindice*. Under that defense he asks to remain unharmed, closing the line as *tutus*.

#### Lines 38–39

> 38 Aut si qua, ut luerem sub acerbo crimine poenas,
>
> 39 inscius admisi, cur o tua dextera cessat?

The alternative possibility is introduced with *Aut*. He considers whether he has offended, continuing with *si*. He admits that offenses might have occurred, speaking of any such wrong as *qua*, though the action is not yet told. The purpose of paying for it is led in by *ut*. He acknowledges that he must atone, expressed by *luerem*. He would suffer under an accusation, placed under it with *sub*. The charge brought against him would be severe, described as *acerbo crimine*. What he must pay under that charge is the penalty, which appears as *poenas*.

If he did transgress, it was without knowing, described as *inscius*. Now what was marked by *qua* is completed by his having done it, told with *admisi*. If that is so, he questions why the god delays, asking *cur*. He cries out in bewilderment, calling aloud with *o*. He looks for the striking hand of Apollo, spoken of as *tua dextera*. He asks why that hand does not strike, ending with *cessat*.

#### Line 40

> 40 Posce sacros arcus, in me tua derige tela:

The priest demands immediate punishment from the god, commanding: *Posce*. He bids Apollo take up his holy bow, described as *sacros arcus*. He bids the god turn the attack upon himself, directing it *in me*. The arrows belong to Apollo, signaled by *tua*, with the weapons held back. He urges the god to level them at him, a command told with *derige*. What was signaled with *tua* is now revealed as the god's shafts, closing the line as *tela*.

#### Lines 41–43

> 41 auctor mortis erit certe deus. Ecce, merentem
>
> 42 fige patrem; cur nata luit peccata parentis
>
> 43 atque hostis duri patitur miseranda cubile?."

He finds consolation in having a divine originator for his end, spoken of as *auctor*. What this power brings about is his own demise, named *mortis*. He is assured that this will come to pass, told with *erit*. Such an end would at least be honorable, declared with *certe*. The one who causes his death will be divine, closing the thought as *deus*. He turns again to urge the blow, crying *Ecce*. He presents himself as the one who earned the punishment, described as *merentem*, but who deserves it is kept for the next verse.

He tells Apollo to pierce the target, commanding: *fige*. The victim who was described as *merentem* is revealed as the father himself, named *patrem*. Then his anguish turns to his daughter, asking *cur*. The innocent victim is his child, returning as *nata*. He asks why she must atone, a payment told with *luit*. What she must atone for is the burden of guilt, appearing as *peccata*. Those wrongs belong not to her but to her father, designated as *parentis*.

Her continued suffering is joined to the question by *atque*. She is subjected to a harsh captor, designated as *hostis duri*. Her captivity forces her to undergo this misery, an endurance told with *patitur*. The helpless daughter evokes deep pity, described as *miseranda*. What she is compelled to share with him is his bed, which closes the prayer as *cubile*.
