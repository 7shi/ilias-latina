### 23–26 (*Iliad* 1.22–25, 1.32, 1.112–115)

#### Line 23

> 23 Myrmidones reddique patri Chryseida censent.

The soldiers who were won over by the priest's weeping are now revealed, called *Myrmidones*. As the commentary says, the Myrmidons are here the Greek army at large. They urge that the captive be given back, joining their plea with *reddique*. She is to be restored to Chryses, referred to as *patri*. The daughter whose return they seek is named *Chryseida*. This is what the soldiers propose, closing the line with *censent*.

#### Lines 24–26

> 24 Sed negat Atrides Chrysenque excedere castris
>
> 25 despecta pietate iubet: ferus ossibus imis
>
> 26 haeret amor spernitque preces damnosa libido.

The king stands against the army's wish, breaking in with *Sed*. He flatly refuses to yield, an outright denial told with *negat*. The man who refuses is the leader, named *Atrides*. He turns upon the priest, linking him to his refusal as *Chrysenque*. He demands that the old man depart, an ejection demanded with *excedere*. He is driven away from the Greek camp, designated as *castris*.

In doing this, Agamemnon sets aside all sacred duty, scorning it with *despecta pietate*. This harsh expulsion is his command, told with *iubet*. The desire driving the king is savage, described as *ferus*, though what is fierce is not yet named. That passion has lodged deep within his marrow, penetrating his *ossibus imis*.

It clings fast within him, told with *haeret*. What was described as *ferus* is now revealed as love, appearing as *amor*. This craving rejects all appeal, joined by *spernitque*. What it casts aside are the father's pleas, named *preces*. The appetite that drives him is ruinous lust, described as *damnosa libido*.
