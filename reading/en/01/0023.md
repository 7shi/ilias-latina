### 23–26 (*Iliad* 1.22–25, 1.32, 1.112–115)

#### Line 23

> 23 Myrmidones reddique patri Chryseida censent.

The soldiers who were won over by the priest's weeping are now revealed, called *Myrmidones*. As the commentary says, the Myrmidons here stand for the Greek army at large. Their wish is that someone be given back, told with *reddique*. The one to receive her is her father, named *patri*. The one to be returned is Chryseis, named *Chryseida*. This is what the soldiers urge, and their urging is *censent*.

#### Lines 24–26

> 24 Sed negat Atrides Chrysenque excedere castris
>
> 25 despecta pietate iubet: ferus ossibus imis
>
> 26 haeret amor spernitque preces damnosa libido.

But the king does not agree, and the turn is marked by *Sed*. He refuses, told with *negat*. The one who refuses is the son of Atreus, named *Atrides*. Next comes the priest himself, named *Chrysenque*, though what is to be done with him is not yet said. He is to go out, told with *excedere*. What he must leave is the camp, designated as *castris*.

In this the king holds sacred duty in contempt, which is said as *despecta pietate*. Only here does it turn out that the departure of *Chrysenque* is the king's order, given as *iubet*. Something in the king is fierce, described as *ferus*, though what is fierce is not yet said. It lies deep within his bones, spoken of as *ossibus imis*.

It clings there, told with *haeret*. What was described as *ferus* is love, appearing as *amor*. Something in him also spurns the father, described as *spernitque*. What it spurns are his prayers, named *preces*. What spurns them is ruinous lust, described as *damnosa libido*.
