### 1–8 (*Iliad* 1.1–7)

#### Line 1

> 1 Iram pande mihi Pelidae, Diua, superbi

The poem begins by telling of a man's anger. That anger is the opening *Iram*. The poet asks for this *Iram* to be laid open and commands: *pande*. It is to be laid open to the poet himself, who refers to himself as *mihi*. The man whose *Iram* it is goes not by his own name; after his father Peleus, he is called *Pelidae*. Here the poet reveals whom he is asking. The one he calls upon is a goddess, addressed as *Diua*. At the end of the line the poem comes back to *Pelidae* and, judging him a proud man, sets him down as *superbi*. So the first line alone brings together the theme of anger, the speaker, the man whose anger it is, and the goddess.

#### Line 2

> 2 Tristia quae miseris iniecit funera Grais

From line 2 on, the poem tells what this *Iram* did. It first speaks of the outcome as grievous, calling it *Tristia*, but what is grievous is not yet said. The account runs on, and the *Iram* is referred to again as *quae*. The victims, too, are pitied before they are named, and are shown as *miseris*. That the anger cast this upon them is told with *iniecit*. Only now is the content revealed. What was called *Tristia* is death, and it is named *funera*. Those shown as *miseris* are the Greeks, who close the line as *Grais*.

#### Line 3

> 3 Atque animas fortes heroum tradidit Orco

The work of the *Iram* does not stop at *funera*; with *Atque* it goes further. Next come the souls of the dead, valiant souls, described as *animas fortes*. These souls belong to heroes, spoken of as *heroum*. The anger hands these *animas fortes* over into someone's keeping, and that handing over is *tradidit*. The one who receives them is Orcus, the power of the dead, named *Orco*.

#### Lines 4–5

> 4 Latrantumque dedit rostris uolucrumque trahendos
>
> 5 Illorum exsangues, inhumatis ossibus, artus.

Once the souls have gone to *Orco*, the poem turns to what becomes of the bodies left behind. Line 4 begins with barking creatures, that is, dogs, called *Latrantum*. What the dogs have to do with it is not yet clear. A third work of the anger is joined to the others by *-que*. The anger gave something away, and this giving is *dedit*. The gift went to mouths, which appear as *rostris*. Only here does it turn out that the *Latrantum* own these mouths. Birds from the sky come to the same mouths, joining in as *uolucrumque*. The *Latrantum* on the ground and the *uolucrum* in the air share the one *rostris*. What is given is doomed to be torn apart, and is declared *trahendos*. But what is to be torn is still not said, even when the line ends.

Line 5 begins by saying whose these are. They belong to the *heroum* of line 3, who are referred to again as *Illorum*. Their bodies have already lost their blood, and are described as *exsangues*. Their bones are left without burial, and that state is *inhumatis ossibus*. After this long wait, what was declared *trahendos* is revealed. It is the limbs of the heroes, which stand at the end of the line as *artus*.

#### Line 6

> 6 Confiebat enim summi sententia regis,

All the havoc told so far was in fact part of something being fulfilled, and line 6 opens with that fulfillment, told as *Confiebat*. This fulfillment is the reason for all that havoc, and the two are tied together at once by *enim*. The one whose purpose was being fulfilled is set above all others and called *summi*, though it is not yet said what he is. While we wait, his will appears as *sententia*. At the end of the line the highest one turns out to be a king, named *regis*. So *summi* and *regis* come together, with *sententia* between them. Yet the king's name is not given. Who is meant is left to the commentary.

#### Lines 7–8

> 7 protulerant* ex quo discordia pectora pugnas,
>
> 8 Sceptriger Atrides et bello clarus Achilles.

Next the poem tells when the king's *sententia* began to be fulfilled. First something had been stirred up, told as *protulerant*. The base text marks this *protulerant* with a sign of doubt, but here it is read as it stands. This event is the starting point, and the time since then is marked with *ex quo*. It was stirred up by hearts set against each other, described as *discordia pectora*. What these *discordia pectora* stirred up was strife, named *pugnas*.

Line 8 reveals whose hearts these were. The first is the son of Atreus, who holds the scepter as the sign of his kingship; he is called *Sceptriger Atrides*. The second is joined to him by *et*: a man who has made his name in war, described as *bello clarus*. The poem keeps his name for the end, and that name is *Achilles*. The scepter and the fame won in war, on which each of the two takes his stand, are set side by side. As the commentary says, the son of Peleus in line 1 is this Achilles. The man whose *Iram* opened the poem is called by his name only now.
