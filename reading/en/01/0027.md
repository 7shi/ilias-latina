### 27–31 (*Iliad* 1.33–36)

#### Line 27

> 27 Contemptus repetit Phoebeia templa sacerdos

Turned away by the king, Chryses suffers outright rejection, marked with *Contemptus*. He goes back to where he belongs, a return told with *repetit*. The sanctuary he seeks belongs to Phoebus, named as the *Phoebeia templa*. As the commentary says, Phoebus is Apollo. The one who retreats to this sanctuary is the holy man himself, who closes the line as *sacerdos*.

#### Line 28

> 28 squalidaque infestis maerens secat unguibus ora

As he goes, his appearance becomes neglected, described with *squalidaque*, though what is unkempt is held back. His hands turn upon himself with cruel fury, declared *infestis*, but the means of it is not yet named. All this comes from his deep sorrow, pictured as *maerens*. With violent grief he tears at his flesh, an act told with *secat*. What was declared *infestis* turns out to be his own fingernails, which appear as *unguibus*. At the end of the line, what was called *squalidaque* is revealed as his face, designated as *ora*.

#### Line 29

> 29 dilaceratque comas annosaque tempora plangit.

His mourning drives him further, and he tears at himself, told with *dilaceratque*. What he pulls out is his hair, named *comas*. Next he beats his aged brow, described as *annosaque tempora*. In desperate sorrow he strikes them, closing the line with *plangit*.

#### Lines 30–31

> 30 Mox ubi depositi gemitus lacrimaeque quierunt,
>
> 31 Fatidici his sacras compellat uocibus aures:

After this violent outburst, a calmer moment arrives, marked by *Mox*. The shift to prayer comes when his anguish subsides, introduced with *ubi*. His groans are at last laid aside, described as *depositi gemitus*. His weeping also abates, joined to the pause as *lacrimaeque*. The cries and weeping at length fell silent, an ending told with *quierunt*.

Chryses turns to the god who reveals the future, designated as *Fatidici*, though what part of the god he addresses is held back. He prepares to speak words of prayer, pointing to them with *his*, though the words themselves wait. The ears he reaches for are holy, described as *sacras*, with their noun still waiting. Now the priest addresses the god, an invocation told with *compellat*. What was pointed to with *his* now arrives as spoken utterances, appearing as *uocibus*. Finally, what was designated as *Fatidici* and described as *sacras* is revealed as the god's listening ears, closing the section as *aures*.
