### 9–22 (*Iliad* 1.8–22)

#### Line 9

> 9 Quis deus hos ira tristi contendere iussit?

The poem turns from the quarrel of its opening to the power behind it, and asks: *Quis*. The one asked about is a god, named *deus*. Achilles and Atrides, the two of the quarrel, are pointed to as *hos*. The anger of the opening returns between them as *ira*. That anger is bitter, described as *tristi*. What the god made them do was clash, told with *contendere*. The god's bidding comes last, and it is *iussit*.

#### Lines 10–11

> 10 Latonae et magni proles Iouis. Ille Pelasgum
>
> 11 infestam regi pestem in praecordia misit

The answer names the god by his parents, beginning with his mother, *Latonae*. His father is joined to her by *et*. The father is spoken of as mighty, called *magni*, though who he is is not yet said. Between the two parents stands their child, who appears as *proles*. The mighty father is Jupiter, who completes the parentage as *Iouis*. As the commentary says, the son of Latona and Jupiter is Apollo. The god is then referred to again as *Ille*. Next come the Pelasgians, who close the line as *Pelasgum*, but what of theirs is meant is not yet said. As the commentary says, the Pelasgians are the Greeks.

Line 11 begins with something hostile that Apollo sends, described as *infestam*. It is aimed at a king, named *regi*. Only here does it turn out that the *Pelasgum* are his people. What was described as *infestam* is a ruinous passion, named *pestem*. Where it goes is the king's heart, told as *in praecordia*. The god sent it there, and that sending is *misit*.

#### Line 12

> 12 implicuitque graui Danaorum corpora morbo.

Apollo's work goes further, and he entangles the army too, told with *implicuitque*. What he entangles them in is heavy, described as *graui*, though what is heavy is not yet said. Those who bear it are called *Danaorum*. As the commentary says, the Danaans too are the Greeks. What is entangled is their bodies, spoken of as *corpora*. At the end of the line what was called *graui* turns out to be a sickness, appearing as *morbo*.

#### Lines 13–14

> 13 Nam quondam Chryses, sollemni tempora uitta
>
> 14 implicitus, raptae fleuit solacia natae

The reason for all this begins to unfold with *Nam*. The story reaches back to an earlier time, marked by *quondam*. The priest who suffered the wrong steps forward, named *Chryses*. He wears something of sacred rite, described as *sollemni*, though what it is is not yet told. It is worn about his temples, shown as *tempora*. What was called *sollemni* is a sacred band, which closes the line as *uitta*.

His temples are bound with this *uitta*, described as *implicitus*. Then he grieves for someone taken from him, set down as *raptae*, but who was taken is not yet named. Over this loss he wept, told with *fleuit*. What he lost was his comfort, remembered as *solacia*. At the end of the line the one who was *raptae* turns out to be his daughter, called *natae*.

#### Lines 15–16

> 15 inuisosque dies inuisaque tempora noctis
>
> 16 egit et assiduis impleuit questibus auras.

The father's sorrow drags on, and his days become hateful to him, described as *inuisosque dies*. The hours that follow them are hateful as well, spoken of as *inuisaque tempora*. These are the hours of the night, which closes the line as *noctis*.

Through these days and nights he dragged out his life, told with *egit*. Another thing he did is joined by *et*. Something of his went on without end, declared *assiduis*, though what it is is not yet said. With it he filled what lay around him, and this filling is *impleuit*. What was declared *assiduis* turns out to be his laments, which appear as *questibus*. What he filled with them was the air, closing the line as *auras*.

#### Lines 17–18

> 17 Postquam nulla dies animum maerore leuabat
>
> 18 nullaque lenibant patrios solacia fletus,

A turn comes after this long grief, marked by *Postquam*. Not one day brought him relief, and it is spoken of as *nulla dies*. What no day relieved was his heart, named *animum*. It was weighed down with sorrow, called *maerore*. That nothing lifted the sorrow is told with *leuabat*.

Nothing else helped either, and this second failure is joined on as *nullaque*; what it was that failed is not yet said. Nothing soothed him, told with *lenibant*. What was not soothed belonged to the father, described as *patrios*, but what of his it was is not yet said. What failed were comforts, which return as *solacia*. What was called *patrios* is the father's weeping, named at the end of the line as *fletus*.

#### Lines 19–21

> 19 castra petit Danaum genibusque affusus Atridae
>
> 20 per superos regnique decus miserabilis orat,
>
> 21 ut sibi causa suae reddatur nata salutis.

Finding no relief, Chryses goes to a camp, which comes first as *castra*. He makes for it, told with *petit*. The camp belongs to the Danaans, referred to again as *Danaum*. There he comes to someone's knees, spoken of as *genibusque*. He has thrown himself down at them, described as *affusus*. The man at whose knees he lies is the son of Atreus, named *Atridae*. As the commentary says, this son of Atreus is Agamemnon.

He makes his plea in the name of the gods above, told as *per superos*. He calls as well on the king's rule, which comes in as *regnique*. What he names of that rule is its honor, spoken of as *decus*. The father is a pitiful sight as he pleads, described as *miserabilis*. So he begs, and that begging is *orat*.

What he begs for follows, led in by *ut*. He asks that something be given back to him, pointing to himself as *sibi*. What he wants back is the very cause of something, declared *causa*. That something is his own, marked as *suae*, though what it is is not yet said. It is to be returned to him, told with *reddatur*. What is to be returned is his daughter, named *nata*. What was marked as *suae* is his own life, which closes the line as *salutis*.

#### Line 22

> 22 Dona simul praefert. Vincuntur fletibus eius

With his plea he brings gifts, named *Dona*. They come at the same time as his words, told with *simul*. He holds them out before the king, and this holding out is *praefert*. Then some are won over, told with *Vincuntur*, but who they are is said only in the next section. What wins them over is his weeping, named *fletibus*. That weeping belongs to the father, who is referred to at the end of the section as *eius*.
