### 9–22 (*Iliad* 1.8–22)

#### Line 9

> 9 Quis deus hos ira tristi contendere iussit?

The poem turns from the quarrel between Achilles and Atrides to ask what divine power brought it about. The question begins by asking which one, with *Quis*. The power asked about is a god, named *deus*. Achilles and Atrides are pointed to as *hos*. The anger that opened the poem returns here in the form of *ira*. That anger is bitter and grievous, described as *tristi*. What the god made them do was clash in strife, told with *contendere*. The question asks what god commanded this, ending with *iussit*.

#### Lines 10–11

> 10 Latonae et magni proles Iouis. Ille Pelasgum
>
> 11 infestam regi pestem in praecordia misit

The answer to the question names the god by his parents, beginning with his mother, *Latonae*. His father is joined to her by *et*. The father is set forth as mighty, called *magni*, though his name is held back. Between the two stands the offspring born of them, spoken of as *proles*. The mighty father who was held back is Jupiter, who completes the parentage as *Iouis*. As the commentary says, the son of Latona and Jupiter is Apollo. The god who takes action is referred to again with *Ille*. He directs his attention toward the Pelasgians, who close the line as *Pelasgum*. As the commentary says, the Pelasgians are the Greeks.

Line 11 begins with the harm Apollo sends; it is destructive, declared *infestam*, but what harm it is is not yet said. The one who receives it is the ruler of the *Pelasgum*, designated as *regi*. What was declared *infestam* is now revealed as a ruinous passion, named *pestem*. Apollo drove this *pestem* into the king's chest, directed inward with *in praecordia*. The god sent it straight into him, and that sending is *misit*.

#### Line 12

> 12 implicuitque graui Danaorum corpora morbo.

A further blow is linked to the first, as Apollo enmeshes his victims, told with *implicuitque*. The affliction he brings is heavy and grievous, described as *graui*, though the kind of trouble is not yet stated. Those who suffer it are the Greeks under another name, called *Danaorum*. As the commentary says, the Danaans are the Greeks. The blow falls upon their physical frames, spoken of as *corpora*. At the end of the line, what was called *graui* turns out to be a disease, appearing as *morbo*.

#### Lines 13–14

> 13 Nam quondam Chryses, sollemni tempora uitta
>
> 14 implicitus, raptae fleuit solacia natae

The reason for this visitation begins to unfold with *Nam*. The story reaches back to an earlier time, marked by *quondam*. The priest who suffered the loss steps forward, named *Chryses*. He wears an emblem of ritual worship, described as *sollemni*, though what it is has not yet been told. The place where he wears it is his temples, shown as *tempora*. The sacred emblem that was called *sollemni* is a headband, which closes the line as *uitta*.

His head had been bound with this *uitta*, expressed with *implicitus*. The priest grieves for someone torn from him, set down as *raptae*, but who was snatched away is not yet named. Over this loss he wept, told with *fleuit*. The one he lost had been his comfort, remembered as *solacia*. At the end of the line, the one who was *raptae* and his *solacia* is revealed as his daughter, called *natae*.

#### Lines 15–16

> 15 inuisosque dies inuisaque tempora noctis
>
> 16 egit et assiduis impleuit questibus auras.

The father's sorrow drags on, and daylight becomes hateful to him, described as *inuisosque*. The light he dreads comes across whole days, named *dies*. The dark brings no relief, joining the day as hateful too, with *inuisaque*. The night's hours are shown as *tempora*. These hateful hours belong to the dark, identified by *noctis*.

Through these days and nights he dragged out his life, told with *egit*. Another expression of his grief is joined by *et*. His mourning was unceasing, declared *assiduis*, though the form of it is not yet named. With this grief he filled all around him, rendered as *impleuit*. What was declared *assiduis* turns out to be his cries of grief, which appear as *questibus*. The expanse he filled with these cries was the open air, closing the line as *auras*.

#### Lines 17–18

> 17 Postquam nulla dies animum maerore leuabat
>
> 18 nullaque lenibant patrios solacia fletus,

The poem marks the turning point after this prolonged grief, beginning with *Postquam*. No single day brought any change, opening with *nulla*. That failure of relief spans each day, referred to again as *dies*. The sorrow weighed upon the priest's spirit, spoken of as *animum*. The burden pressing on his spirit was his deep grief, named *maerore*. Nothing lightened that grief, an easing expressed by *leuabat*.

No consolation arrived to aid him, joined to the previous line by *nullaque*, though what fails to come is held back. Nothing softened his pain, a soothing told with *lenibant*. His tears were those of a grieving father, described as *patrios*, with the tears themselves waiting. Now what was held back after *nullaque* appears as comforts, returning as *solacia*. The weeping that was described as *patrios* is named at the end of the line as *fletus*.

#### Lines 19–21

> 19 castra petit Danaum genibusque affusus Atridae
>
> 20 per superos regnique decus miserabilis orat,
>
> 21 ut sibi causa suae reddatur nata salutis.

Seeing no relief, Chryses makes his way to the Greek camp, setting out for the *castra*. He heads toward it, an approach told with *petit*. The camp belongs to the Greeks, referred to once more as *Danaum*. Reaching them, he bows down at someone's knees, joining his plea with *genibusque*. He casts himself down before the king, a humbling described as *affusus*. The man at whose knees he falls is the son of Atreus, designated as *Atridae*. As the commentary says, this son of Atreus is Agamemnon.

Chryses entreats him by what is holy, beginning his oath with *per*. The powers he invokes are the gods above, named *superos*. He adds the dignity of the king's own authority, joined by *regnique*. What he appeals to in that authority is its honor, spoken of as *decus*. In this plea the weeping father presents a pitiful sight, described as *miserabilis*. Thus he makes his supplication, an entreaty told with *orat*.

What he begs for is introduced with *ut*. He asks that she be given back to him, pointing to himself as *sibi*. His daughter was the whole cause of his well-being, declared *causa*. She is vital to his own life, signaled with *suae*, though that life is held back. He pleads that she be given back, an act of restoration told with *reddatur*. The person to be restored is his beloved daughter, named *nata*. Now what was linked to *suae* is revealed as his own survival, which closes the line as *salutis*.

#### Line 22

> 22 Dona simul praefert. Vincuntur fletibus eius

Along with his prayer the priest presents a ransom, bringing forward his *Dona*. These gifts accompany his words, offered together with *simul*. He holds them out before the king, an offering told with *praefert*. The listeners are moved and overcome by pity, told with *Vincuntur*, though who gives way to pity is kept for the next verse. What overcomes them is the old man's weeping, named *fletibus*. Those tears belong to the grieving father, who is referred to at the end of the section as *eius*.
