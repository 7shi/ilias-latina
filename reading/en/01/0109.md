### 109–110 (*Iliad* 1.601–604, 1.606–611)

#### Lines 109–110

> 109 et dapibus diui curant sua corpora largis;
>
> 110 inde petunt thalamos iucundaque dona quietis.

The evening brings also a feast, joined by *et* and appearing as *dapibus*, though what kind of meal it is is not yet said. Those who partake are the gods, spoken of as *diui*. They refresh themselves, told with *curant*. What they refresh is their own bodies, designated as *sua corpora*. What was called *dapibus* is revealed as lavish, closing the line as *largis*.

From the feast they turn next to sleep, marked by *inde*. They make their way there, given as *petunt*. Their destinations are their bedchambers, named *thalamos*. They seek also pleasant gifts, described as *iucundaque dona*. These gifts belong to peaceful rest, which stands as *quietis*. With this rest upon Olympus, the first book of the poem comes to an end.
