### 320–331 (*Iliad* 3.428–429, 3.432–433, 3.430–431, 3.434–436)

#### Lines 320–321

> 320 "Venisti mea flamma, Paris, superatus ab armis
>
> 321 coniugis antiqui? Vidi puduitque uidere,

Helen begins by questioning his return, asking: *Venisti*. She addresses him with affection, calling him *mea flamma*. She speaks directly to him, addressing him as *Paris*. She notes that he has been conquered, described as *superatus*. He was overcome by force of arms, met *ab armis*. Those weapons belonged to her husband, named *coniugis*. He is the spouse of earlier days, known as *antiqui*. She witnessed the humiliation herself, told with *Vidi*. She also felt deep shame at the spectacle, confessed as *puduitque*. She was grieved to look upon it, closing with *uidere*.

#### Lines 322–323

> 322 arreptum cum te traheret uiolentus Atrides
>
> 323 Iliacoque tuos foedaret puluere crines.

She recalls him seized by his enemy, described as *arreptum*. Her memory returns to that moment, introduced with *cum*. The one laid low was Paris, pointing to *te*. His captor pulled him along the field, told with *traheret*. That challenger was fierce, marked as *uiolentus*. The warrior who dragged him was the son of Atreus, appearing as *Atrides*.

Dust from Troy also rose around him, described as *Iliacoque*, though what dirt it is is not yet said. The humiliation touched what was his own, marked as *tuos*, though what part of him is not yet said. The victor defiled his appearance, told with *foedaret*. What was described as *Iliacoque* is revealed as the dust of the plain, met as *puluere*. What was marked as *tuos* is revealed as his locks of hair, named *crines*.

#### Lines 324–326

> 324 Nostraque - me miseram! - timui ne Doricus ensis
>
> 325 oscula discuteret; totus mihi, mente reuincta,
>
> 326 fugerat ore color sanguisque reliquerat artus.

She also feared for what belonged to both of them, described as *Nostraque*, though what shared intimacy it is is not yet said. An outburst of grief breaks in, crying: *me miseram*. She confesses her terror, told with *timui*. She was afraid lest destruction fall, introduced with *ne*. The enemy's blade is Doric, described as *Doricus*. As the commentary says, the Dorians are the Greeks. That Greek weapon is a sword, named *ensis*.

What was described as *Nostraque* is revealed as their shared kisses, named *oscula*. She feared the blade would sever them forever, shattered with *discuteret*. All of it was drained from her, described as *totus*, though what was lost is not yet said. The terror overcame Helen herself, pointing to *mihi*. Her mind was frozen with fear, met as *mente reuincta*.

The life within her had fled, departed with *fugerat*. It vanished from her face, met as *ore*. What was described as *totus* is revealed as her color, named *color*. Her blood also departed from her, described as *sanguisque*. It had completely forsaken her body, abandoned with *reliquerat*. The strength left her limbs, met as *artus*.

#### Line 327

> 327 Quis te cum saeuo contendere suasit Atrida?

Helen asks who urged this duel upon him, demanding: *Quis*. The one persuaded was Paris, pointing to *te*. He was urged to fight with a fierce opponent, met *cum saeuo*, though with whom is not yet said. He was urged to vie in single combat, striving with *contendere*. Someone prompted his reckless challenge, persuaded with *suasit*. What was met *cum saeuo* is revealed as the son of Atreus, named *Atrida*.

#### Lines 328–330

> 328 An nondum uaga fama tuas peruenit ad aures
>
> 329 de uirtute uiri? Moneo ne rursus inique
>
> 330 illius tua fata uelis committere dextrae."

She adds another question, opening with *An*. She asks whether it has not yet happened, marked by *nondum*. Rumor wanders abroad, described as *uaga*. That spreading report is rumor, named *fama*. The report reaches what belongs to him, addressed as *tuas*. It has arrived at his hearing, reaching him with *peruenit*. The tidings reach his ears, met *ad aures*.

The report tells of valor, met *de uirtute*. That courage belongs to the Greek warrior, named *uiri*. Helen gives Paris a solemn warning, declaring: *Moneo*. She cautions him against another duel, introduced with *ne*. He must not enter the fight again, marked by *rursus*. The contest would be unequal, described as *inique*.

Power rests with that other warrior, designated as *illius*. He risks what is his own, described as *tua*. What hangs in the balance is his fate, named *fata*. She warns him not to choose this path, inclined with *uelis*. He must not entrust his life to his rival, trusted with *committere*. He must not deliver himself to his enemy's right hand, closing her warning with *dextrae*.

#### Line 331

> 331 Dixit. Tum largis perfudit fletibus ora.

Helen brings her speech to an end, concluding with *Dixit*. Grief overtakes her immediately after, marked by *Tum*. Her weeping is copious, described as *largis*. She drenches her cheeks, told with *perfudit*. Her face is bathed in tears, met as *fletibus*. The weeping covers her face, closing the section with *ora*.
