### 256–266 (*Iliad* 3.36–40, 3.50, 3.46–49, 3.52–55)

#### Lines 256–257

> 256 ad socios amens. Quem postquam turpiter Hector
>
> 257 confusum terrore uidet: "O dedecus - inquit -

Paris retreats to his companions, seeking refuge *ad socios*. He is out of his mind with panic, described as *amens*. Paris comes back, as *Quem*. What happens after this retreat is marked by *postquam*. His flight is shameful, condemned as *turpiter*. The one who watches him is his brother, entering as *Hector*.

He sees Paris thrown into disorder, described as *confusum*. That confusion is born of fright, named *terrore*. Hector sees him in this condition, told with *uidet*. He cries out with *O*. He brands his brother a disgrace, calling him *dedecus*. His words are broken in upon with *inquit*.

#### Lines 258–262

> 258 "aeternum patriae generisque infamia nostri,
>
> 259 terga refers? At non dubitabas hospitis olim
>
> 260 expugnare toros, cuius nunc defugis arma
>
> 261 uimque times. Vbi sunt uires, ubi cognita nobis
>
> 262 ludorum quondam uaria in certamina uis est?

That shame will never end, described as *aeternum*. It falls upon their native land, named *patriae*. Their family also shares in this reproach, joined as *generisque*. Paris is a stain upon them all, called *infamia*. That family is Hector's own, marked as *nostri*.

What he turns to the enemy is his back, spoken of as *terga*. Hector asks if he retreats, told with *refers*. The speech turns to Paris's past deeds, marked by *At*. Hector declares that he was not always so fearful, indicated by *non*. He did not hesitate in former times, shown with *dubitabas*. His target was the man who had welcomed him, named *hospitis*. That betrayal happened long ago, recalled with *olim*. What Paris did not shrink from was storming, told with *expugnare*. The prize he violated was his host's marriage bed, appearing as *toros*.

That host comes back, as *cuius*. Hector returns to the present hour, marked by *nunc*. Now Paris flees before him, censured with *defugis*. What he avoids are his host's weapons, named *arma*. He shrinks also from his host's strength, joined as *uimque*. Paris is terrified of that power, told with *times*.

Hector demands where that former courage has gone, asking: *Vbi*. He asks where those qualities are, questioned with *sunt*. What he looks for is his brother's strength, named *uires*. He repeats his question with *ubi*. That strength was once widely known, described as *cognita*. It was familiar to his own comrades, expressed by *nobis*. It was displayed in public games, spoken of as *ludorum*. That renown belonged to former days, recalled with *quondam*. The competitions were diverse, characterized as *uaria*. Paris proved his skill in the matches, entering *in certamina*. That prowess was his strength, recognized as *uis*. Hector asks where it is now, concluding the question with *est*.

#### Lines 263–264

> 263 Hic animos ostende tuos: nihil adiuuat armis
>
> 264 nobilitas formae: duro Mars milite gaudet.

Hector points to this battlefield as the place to prove himself, declared with *Hic*. What Paris must show is courage, named *animos*. Hector commands him to display it, urging: *ostende*. That courage must be his own, closing the command as *tuos*.

Fine appearance offers no assistance, opening with *nihil*. It provides no help, told with *adiuuat*. In battle it is useless, spoken of as *armis*. What brings no aid is nobility, named *nobilitas*. It is the nobility of outward beauty, revealed as *formae*. In contrast, what is favored is hardy, described as *duro*, though who is hardy is not yet said. The god of war is named, entering as *Mars*. What was described as *duro* is revealed as a soldier, named *milite*. Mars takes delight in such men, closing the line as *gaudet*.

#### Lines 265–266

> 265 Dum iaceas in amore tuo, nos bella geremus
>
> 266 scilicet et nostrum fundemus in hoste cruorem.

Hector speaks of the time while Paris rests, opening with *Dum*. Paris lies idle, described as *iaceas*. He spends his time in romance, resting *in amore*. That love belongs to Paris alone, marked as *tuo*. The other Trojans refer to themselves as *nos*. What they must face is warfare, named *bella*. They will carry on the fight, told with *geremus*.

The concession is granted as of course, marked by *scilicet*. Another consequence is added by *et*. What will be shed belongs to Hector and his comrades, designated as *nostrum*, though what it is is not yet said. They will pour it out, told with *fundemus*. They will spill it against the enemy, named *in hoste*. What was designated as *nostrum* is revealed as their blood, closing the line as *cruorem*.
