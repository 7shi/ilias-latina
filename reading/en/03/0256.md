### 256–266 (*Iliad* 3.36–40, 3.50, 3.46–49, 3.52–55)

#### Lines 256–257

> 256 ad socios amens. Quem postquam turpiter Hector
>
> 257 confusum terrore uidet: "O dedecus - inquit -

He drew back to his companions, given as *ad socios*. He is out of his mind, described as *amens*. Then Paris comes back, as *Quem*. Something comes after, marked by *postquam*. It is shameful, marked by *turpiter*. Hector comes in, named *Hector*.

Paris is thrown into confusion, described as *confusum*. It is from fear, named *terrore*. Hector sees it, told with *uidet*. He cries out, beginning with *O*. Paris is a disgrace, called *dedecus*. Hector says so, told with *inquit*.

#### Lines 258–262

> 258 "aeternum patriae generisque infamia nostri,
>
> 259 terga refers? At non dubitabas hospitis olim
>
> 260 expugnare toros, cuius nunc defugis arma
>
> 261 uimque times. Vbi sunt uires, ubi cognita nobis
>
> 262 ludorum quondam uaria in certamina uis est?

The disgrace lasts forever, described as *aeternum*. It falls on their homeland, named *patriae*. Their family comes next, named *generisque*. Paris is its shame, called *infamia*. The family is Hector's own too, given as *nostri*.

Paris's back comes next, named *terga*. He turns it, told with *refers*. Hector sets something against this, marked by *At*. It begins with a denial, marked by *non*. Paris did not hesitate, told with *dubitabas*. A host comes in, named *hospitis*. It was once, marked by *olim*. What Paris did not hesitate to do was to storm, told with *expugnare*. What he stormed was the host's marriage bed, named *toros*.

The host comes back, as *cuius*. It is now, marked by *nunc*. Paris flees, told with *defugis*. What he flees is the host's arms, named *arma*. The host's strength comes next, named *uimque*. Paris fears it, told with *times*.

Hector asks where something is, given as *Vbi sunt*. It is strength, named *uires*. He asks where again, with *ubi*. Something was known, described as *cognita*, though what it is is not yet said. It was known to Hector and the others, spoken of as *nobis*. Games come in, named *ludorum*. It was once, marked by *quondam*. Something is varied, described as *uaria*. It is the contests, given as *in certamina*. What was described as *cognita* is prowess, which closes the question as *uis est*.

#### Lines 263–264

> 263 Hic animos ostende tuos: nihil adiuuat armis
>
> 264 nobilitas formae: duro Mars milite gaudet.

The place is here, marked by *Hic*. Courage comes next, named *animos*. Paris is to show it, told with *ostende*. The courage is his own, which closes the command as *tuos*. Nothing is any help, given as *nihil adiuuat*. It is in battle, named *armis*. What does not help is nobility, named *nobilitas*. It is nobility of looks, named *formae*. Something is hardy, described as *duro*, though what it is is not yet said. Mars comes in, named *Mars*. The hardy one is a soldier, named *milite*. Mars delights in him, which closes the line as *gaudet*.

#### Lines 265–266

> 265 Dum iaceas in amore tuo, nos bella geremus
>
> 266 scilicet et nostrum fundemus in hoste cruorem.

A time is set, marked by *Dum*. Paris may lie idle, told with *iaceas*. It is in love, given as *in amore*. The love is his own, given as *tuo*. Hector and the others come in, as *nos*. Wars come next, named *bella*. They will wage them, told with *geremus*.

It is so, of course, marked by *scilicet*. More follows, joined by *et*. Something is theirs, described as *nostrum*, though what it is is not yet said. They will shed it, told with *fundemus*. It is against the enemy, given as *in hoste*. What was described as *nostrum* is their blood, which closes the line as *cruorem*.
