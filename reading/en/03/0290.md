### 290–301 (*Iliad* 3.346–349, 3.355–359)

#### Line 290

> 290 hastam deinde iacit, quam deuitauit Atrides

What was whistling through the air is revealed as a spear, named *hastam*. The cast comes after his retreat, marked by *deinde*. Alexander hurls the weapon forward, told with *iacit*. The flying spear comes back, as *quam*. Menelaus swerves aside to dodge it, escaping with *deuitauit*. The warrior who avoids the blow is the son of Atreus, appearing as *Atrides*.

#### Lines 291–293

> 291 inque uicem misso fixisset corpora telo
>
> 292 praedonis Phrygii, ni uastum ferrea pectus
>
> 293 texisset lorica uiri septemplice tergo.

Menelaus prepares to strike in return, joined as *inque uicem*. What he casts flies from his hand, described as *misso*, though with what weapon is not yet said. His counter-thrust would have pierced his enemy, told with *fixisset*. The blow was aimed to run through his body, met as *corpora*. What was described as *misso* is revealed as his spear, striking as *telo*.

That body belongs to a robber, named *praedonis*. He comes from Phrygia, marked as *Phrygii*. As the commentary says, this Phrygian robber is Paris. The fatal stroke is averted by an exception, introduced with *ni*. What receives the blow is broad, pictured as *uastum*. The defense is forged of iron, described as *ferrea*, though what protection it is is not yet said. The broad target is the warrior's chest, met as *pectus*. Armor shielded his flesh from death, told with *texisset*. What was described as *ferrea* is revealed as his corslet, appearing as *lorica*. The corslet belongs to the Trojan warrior, referred to as *uiri*. His defense is sevenfold, praised as *septemplice*. That barrier is made of hide, closing the line as *tergo*.

#### Lines 294–296

> 294 Insequitur iuxta clamor; tum aduersus uterque
>
> 295 constitit et galeam galea terit et pede plantam
>
> 296 coniungit stridetque mucro mucrone corusco;

A great noise rises from the watching armies, told with *Insequitur*. It breaks out close by, marked by *iuxta*. What rises is the battle-cry, named *clamor*. The moment for close combat arrives, indicated with *tum*. Each turns directly toward his enemy, described as *aduersus*. Both champions advance, spoken of as *uterque*.

Each firmly takes his stand, planted with *constitit*. Action follows immediately in close combat, joined by *et*. One warrior pushes forward his helmet, named *galeam*. He presses it against his rival's helmet, confronting *galea*. He rubs one piece of armor against the other, worn with *terit*. Their steps also close in, tied with *et*. He locks foot to foot, met as *pede*. The sole of one fighter presses the other's sole, named *plantam*.

He brings them tightly together, bound with *coniungit*. Their clash also gives off a harsh ring, sounding as *stridetque*. A sword blade strikes forward, named *mucro*. It crashes against the rival blade, met as *mucrone*. That opposing steel glitters in the clash, described as *corusco*.

#### Line 297

> 297 corpus collectum tegitur fulgentibus armis.

Each warrior draws his own body tight, named *corpus*. His frame is gathered behind his guard, drawn together as *collectum*. He is sheltered from the attack, covered with *tegitur*. What shields him is gleaming in the sunlight, flashing as *fulgentibus*. That brilliant defense is his armor, met as *armis*.

#### Lines 298–299

> 298 Non aliter fortes nitida de coniuge tauri
>
> 299 bella gerunt uastisque replent mugitibus auras.

The combat is likened to another clash, opening with a denial, *Non*. The struggle unfolds in no other way, marked by *aliter*. The beasts that fight are mighty, described as *fortes*, though what creatures they are is not yet said. They battle over a sleek heifer, praised as *nitida*. The contest is waged over her as a mate, met *de coniuge*. What were described as *fortes* are revealed as bulls, named *tauri*.

They wage fierce warfare, named *bella*. The two rivals carry on the fight, told with *gerunt*. Their bellows are also huge, described as *uastisque*, though what fills the air is not yet said. They fill the whole plain around them, told with *replent*. What was described as *uastisque* is revealed as their lowing roars, echoing as *mugitibus*. Their bellowing reaches up into the sky, closing the simile as *auras*.

#### Lines 300–301

> 300 Atque diu rigido captabant corpora ferro,
>
> 301 cum memor Atrides raptae sibi coniugis instat

The narrative returns to the locked duel, continuing with *Atque*. The prolonged struggle goes on for a long time, marked by *diu*. They strike with unyielding metal, described as *rigido*, though what weapon it is is not yet said. Each hunts for an opening to wound the other, striving with *captabant*. They aim at each other's bodies, met as *corpora*. What was described as *rigido* is revealed as hard iron, struck with *ferro*.

A sudden turn arrives, introduced with *cum*. One warrior calls his grievance to mind, kept in thought as *memor*. The son of Atreus presses the fight, named *Atrides*. He recalls the woman seized from him, described as *raptae*, though who she is is not yet said. She was stolen from his own house, pointing to *sibi*. What was described as *raptae* is revealed as his wife, returning as *coniugis*. Urged on by that memory he presses his attack, closing the section with *instat*.
