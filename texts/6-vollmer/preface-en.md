All the manuscripts I have listed so far I have collated either anew or for the first time, and I have checked the collations against photographs.  Intending to publish their readings in full in the *Sitz.-Ber. Münch. Ak.* 1913, Abh. 3, I have excerpted in the edition those that are needed for establishing the words of the poet and for distinguishing the families of the manuscripts.  I know besides about 50 more recent ones, a list of which I have given in the place cited; I neither could nor would collate them all, yet I inspected not a few and collated certain passages.  The only one I have cited rather often is the Helmstadiensis (Wolfenbüttel, Helmstad. 384, formerly 349, formerly Misc. 32), of about the thirteenth century, in which vv. 1–266 have perished.  I name in this place also the Vienna manuscript lat. 3509, on paper, of the fifteenth century (cf. H. Schenkl, loc. cit., p. IV note 1), because in it the rubricator wrote the title *Bebii Italici poetae clarissimi epithome in quatuor viginti libros homeri iliados* ("The epitome of the most famous poet Bebius Italicus of the twenty-four books of Homer's *Iliad*").  Whatever other manuscripts I have used, one by one for single passages, I have named in full.

The verses from the Latin Homer taken up by the florilegia do not surpass the complete manuscripts in the quality of their readings: therefore I do not repeat here what I have reported elsewhere.

I note, however, that I have by no means taken up all their readings.

To judge the readings and authority of the manuscripts rightly, one must know that a single old exemplar, perhaps from Spain, came down to the Carolingians, and that this, being copied, produced that abundance of copies which the masters, as it seems, used diligently in the schools from about the ninth century onwards.  In nearly all these manuscripts both those who copied and those who read them acted with great licence, expelling ancient readings either by error or by design and substituting glosses or conjectures and interpolations; yet two families can still be distinguished today: to the one belong PW(A); from the other was derived first the stock MN, then ELR, and finally CFV.  It is to be noted, however, that some good readings from PW were transferred both into the exemplar of manuscript E and, in greater number, into that from which CFV are derived.  The more recent ones, such as B, G, the Helmstadiensis and the others, are all of a mixed kind.

So much for the manuscripts.  But as to the fact that in them the poem of about a thousand verses is read divided into 24 books, I am so far from believing that Italicus himself prescribed this, that I conclude from it that at the end of the Roman Empire, when Greek books were still read in Italy, some petty schoolmaster had already arranged the epitome for the use of schools.  Therefore I have not taken into the apparatus the notes of the manuscripts on the ends and beginnings of the books, much less the errors committed in this matter by individual ones. Instead of this useless division, which does not always agree with Homer either, I have preferred to set beside the single verses of the Latin poem the Greek ones that the epitomator reproduces; in this way it becomes clear at the same time in which parts he displayed his own art and invention.

What merits the philologists have earned since the first edition in explaining and emending the poem, it is not the place here to pursue at length.  Chance brought it about that the Dutch accomplished the best and the most: Petrus Bondam in his *Variae lectiones* and *Animadversiones*, Adrianus van Dorp and Joh. Schrader in their *Observationes*; of what E. W. Higt had brilliantly devised, later scholars suspected that Joannes van der Dussen (*Prodromus novae Pindari Thebani editionis*, Kampen 1769) had stealthily purloined a part; what could be restored to Higt, van Kooten restored from his copy; Antonius de Rooy corrected not a few things in his *Spicilegia critica*, 1771.  Jo. Christ. Wernsdorf gathered the studies of his predecessors and increased them with his own in volume IV of the *Poetae Latini Minores*, Helmstedt 1784 (repeated by Lemaire, Paris 1824, vol. III p. 455 ff.).  It was not granted to Theodor van Kooten to bring to a good end, down to 1785, what he had worked out at the instigation of Jo. Schrader; this was revised and edited by Henricus Weytingh, whose book appeared in 1809 at Leiden and Amsterdam.  After many years Lucian Mueller first edited the verses (*Über den Auszug a. d. Ilias, den sog. Pind. Theb.*, Berlin 1857), though without cutting to the quick; Emil Baehrens, as for other Latin poets, so for this one deserved excellently when he first drove out the vulgate reading and substituted the testimony of the manuscripts (*Poet. lat. min.* III, 1881), though he was not praised enough by Otto Rossbach (*Hermes* 17, 1882, pp. 515–521).  Frid. Plessis achieved almost nothing in his edition (Paris 1885): R. Ehwald judged the book rightly (*Philol. Anzeiger* 17, 1887, pp. 46–59).
