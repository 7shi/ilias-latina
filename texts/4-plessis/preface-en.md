## p. I

PDF page 15.

What I have aimed at in this little book I shall set out in a few words.

There survives an *Iliad* condensed into Latin hexameters, often given the false title *Homerus Latinus* or *Pindarus Thebanus*, which was read in the schools in the so-called Middle Age and is now also not seldom praised by scholars, whether for its metre or because, among so many Latin translations of Homer, this one alone has overcome the spite of time intact.

From the two acrostics (ἀκροστιχίοις) with which the *Epitome* begins and ends it is certainly established that the poet was named Italicus.  Nor is it less certain, from the metre and the grammar, and also from verses 899–902, 236 and 483, that the poem was written in the first century after the birth of Christ, before the death of Nero.  But since these points were put forward not long ago and have not yet been treated successfully in every respect, I thought it not out of place to inquire into them again, so that what seemed right might be shown by a right way as well and escape the hope of refutation, and so that the various fictions of opinion, and the reasons by which very learned men were once led into such great errors, might be at hand.

Our Italicus, who in my opinion was not the same man as Silius, the author of the *Punica*, but a grammarian not

otherwise known, was admittedly of little strength in the gifts of talent; yet I agree with most that he was not deprived of all elegance of judgment.  So that you may better understand what place he holds among the Romans who rendered the Homeric poems in Latin, I thought that in the first chapter of the second part I should briefly recall and judge what had been attempted in this field before him.  On the book of Italicus itself, however, it was fitting for us to dwell longer: on the limits of the poem, on the imitation of Virgil and Ovid, on its errors and faults, on the method that our poet followed in condensing, on the purpose that he set himself, you will find both what others have already established and what I myself have found through my familiarity with Italicus.  And since nearly everyone and everything admits that the Latin *Iliad* was composed for the use of schools, I for my part have tried to define rightly what ought to be expected of us who pass a fair judgment: namely, I deny that the work of Italicus, whatever it is, is to be utterly despised, since it shines with a constant purity of language and metre, and here and there with a certain grace and neatness, and these things are not to be held of small value in the schools.

For the rest, if you read through the poem, you, most learned reader, will see what is to be thought; this is a matter for your judgment rather than mine.  Therefore, after discussing in the third part the books, whether written by hand or printed, and the false title *Pindarus*, I have given you the text, emended as carefully as the matter allowed.  I have diligently recorded the complete variants of thirteen manuscripts and unpublished readings excerpted from three others, as I was not given the means of a fuller harvest.  In passages that are plainly corrupt I have ventured to write some things on my own account.  Finally I have added a full index, which gathers the whole matter of the poem, and by looking through it you can at once discover in what things Italicus departed from Homer.

## p. III

PDF page 17.

Before I begin, I cannot but give due thanks to the scholars who have given me various kinds of help and, as it were, the spurs of their kindness: to the most distinguished Professor E. Benoist, who was the originator and adviser of this little book and whose teaching I boast of having enjoyed; to Louis Havet, whose talent and diligence have helped me so greatly and so admirably, as is clear from many places both of the discussion and of the critical apparatus, that if anything new commends my small work, it is to be attributed above all to him; and to Paul Thomas of Ghent and Louis Duvau, who, though engaged in very great occupations, most kindly took upon themselves the task of providing me with a store of critical aids.  I therefore declare with a grateful and glad heart that they have all been of great service to me.
