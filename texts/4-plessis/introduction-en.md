PART ONE

CHAPTER I

BY WHAT NAME OUR POET WAS CALLED

Two parastichides (Παραστιχίδας), from which, with the reading of the manuscripts slightly altered, the name and the words *Italicus scripsit* ("Italicus wrote [this]") would shine out, one at the beginning of our poem (vv. 1–8), the other at the end (vv. 1063–1070), were first noticed by Franz Buecheler.  He was followed by Emil Baehrens, who, when he edited the poem in 1881, ventured to depart from the footsteps of the old and the more recent editors and did not hesitate to inscribe the name of Italicus on the little book.

On the name the scholar judged excellently; on the way in which both parastichides are produced, less rightly.  For I could by no means bring myself, with Baehrens as guide, to take *Ut primum* into v. 7 and to cast out of the text the words *Ex quo*, which, handed down by the manuscripts, we read at the beginning of the verse; verse 6 of the Homeric *Iliad* resists too strongly:

ἐξ οὗ δὴ τὰ πρῶτα διαστήτην ἐρίσαντε.
("from the time when first the two stood apart in strife")

But while I was struggling and was still in doubt (wrongly, as you will soon see) whether I should go on following that Italicus, perhaps nothing but a name and a shadow, or think of another way, the most acute mind of Havet came to my aid.  For since verse 7, as it is cited by Ermenricus in his letter to Grimold,

begins with the word *Protulerunt*, Louis Havet at once perceived that a gloss lay hidden here, and judged that the word should be divided into two, for *videlicet* and *tulerunt*: these, written in the margin of an old copy to explain some word that had the sound of *re-* but was unusual or less frequently used, or at least seemed so to a half-learned scribe, then burst into the text in place of the genuine reading.  And while Louis Havet was seeking what had vanished, he found at the same time another fault at the end of the verse, namely that *pugnas*, commonly handed down, is nothing but a fabrication, and that the true reading depends on the word *turmas* found in the same Ermenricus.  Thus the very learned man happily conjectured, or to speak more truly restored, *Volverunt — turbas*.  Whence comes ITALICVS.

Nor was Havet's skill of less use in restoring the other acrostic, with which the poem closes.  Verse 1065 caused difficulty: since in the manuscripts it begins with the letter Q, whereas R is required because of the parastichis, you will not unwisely hold it for certain that it suffers from a fault.  But beware of agreeing with Baehrens's remedy, the word *remis* being moved from the end to the beginning.  Here, relying on the reading *Vltima* of the Santen manuscript, while I was considering with myself the poet hastening his course, I had first conjectured *Raptim*; Louis Havet, fairly enough, thought it better to write *Raris*.  We now have the word SCRIPSIT.

Now that Louis Havet has emended verses 7 and 1065 so precisely, no one, I think, can deny that our poem was composed by a certain Italicus, unless he relies on unexpected and very secure aids.  Therefore we shall not comply even with Hertz, who keeps the words *Ex quo* at the beginning of the seventh verse and so deforms the ninth that, with the word *ira* placed before the words *Quis deus hos*, it begins with the letter I, whence the parastichis *Italice Sili* ("O Silius Italicus") is brought out, and you would have the work dedicated to Silius Italicus, not written by Italicus.  But to pass over other things, the later parastichis *scripsit* stands in the way, which requires the nominative case in the former, not the vocative.

CHAPTER II

AT WHAT TIME HE WROTE THE EPITOME

Now if we inquire at what time our poet composed his *Iliad*, first of all we must by no means approve what Henricus Weytingh¹ strives to draw out of some little verses written below the text in the Santen manuscript.  These verses are

Pindarus hunc secum trans pontum uexit Homerum;

Sed argumentum dedit esse poeta latinum.

("Pindar carried this Homer with him across the sea; but the poet made the argument to be Latin.")

When Weytingh had noted these lines, bad Latin and truly limping, and had attributed to them more authority and significance than was fair, the learned man devised, not by any divine inspiration, that our poet, by name Pindar, was « if not a contemporary, at least a fellow countryman » of Joseph of Exeter (*Iscanus*), an English monk who flourished at Exeter in the eleventh or twelfth century and had composed six books on the Trojan War.  But it is superfluous to rely on the name Pindar, on whose origin see below, part III, chap. 2, since we have placed beyond all hazard of doubt that our poet is to be called Italicus: let us be allowed to discuss briefly the age to which Weytingh assigned the poem.

CHAPTER III

WAS IT SILIUS ITALICUS?

Now, however, we must go to meet Buecheler¹ and Munk², who have claimed the poem for C. Silius Italicus, not without some appearance of truth.  For since the writer of the *Punica*, born in the twenty-fifth year after the birth of Christ, died, as is well known, in the hundred and first, it is sufficiently clear that the chronology does not conflict with what we have set out above, if the argument has been sound.  For Emil Baehrens has not altogether persuaded me that Silius Italicus by no means composed verses before the death of Nero.  This the learned man inferred from that well-known epigram of Martial (VII, 63):

Perpetui numquam moritura volumina Sili

Qui legis et latia carmina digna toga,

Pierios tantum vati placuisse recessus

Credis et Aoniae bacchica serta comae?

Sacra cothurnati non attigit ante Maronis

Implevit magni quam Ciceronis opus.

("You who read the never-dying volumes of the everlasting Silius, and poems worthy of the Latin toga, do you believe that only the Pierian retreats pleased the bard, and the Bacchic garlands of Aonian hair?  He did not touch the sacred things of the buskined Maro before he had fulfilled the work of great Cicero.")

ON THOSE AMONG THE ROMANS WHO BEFORE OUR POET

RENDERED THE POEMS OF HOMER IN LATIN

Now let us see what Homeric studies flourished among the Romans down to Italicus, so that we may estimate more rightly what the Epitome is worth.  But since two very learned men, Karl Philipp Euler¹ and Hermann Walther², in very useful little books full of learning, have already clearly discussed nearly everything that bears on the matter, we may discharge the same task more briefly and go forward only to sketch a very wide subject.  We shall neglect the poets who drew any part of their thoughts from the Homeric spring and here and there turned verses of Homer, even rendered with some subtlety, to their own purpose; since these are almost countless (nearly all, so to speak, except the comic poets), it is not our intention to traverse so immense an expanse of sea. You have seen well enough under what guides our poet conducted himself in verse and diction, and to whose words he swore allegiance; now it must be said in what manner he followed the argument of the *Iliad*, what things he set out, what he either barely touched or left out entirely. When I began to investigate for what reasons the Latin *Iliad* brings so much tedium to its readers, first of all it occurred to me that Italicus narrates mere wars, « battles and slaughters, attacks and flights »¹, nothing or almost nothing that touches the heart and the inmost feelings of the soul. We know that the Greeks, no less in the colonies than in the homeland itself, devoted active labour to the Homeric poems already from the earliest time. What he had set out to do, Italicus accomplished not so badly.  What more should we demand? Here, most learned reader, I too have ventured to submit to you an edition of the Latin *Iliad* furnished with a critical apparatus and adorned with a copious index of names and subjects.  You now know by what name the poet was called, at what time he lived, what place he held among the Romans who turned the Homeric poems into Latin, and in what manner and with what purpose he wrote his Epitome.  Our Italicus flourished in the first part of the first century after the birth of Christ; he seems to have been a grammarian who composed this Epitome for teaching boys, frigidly enough and with almost no talent, but with the purity of language and the elegance of metrical art scrupulously preserved.  And of so many Latin poets who did not fear to translate the verses of Homer into Latin ones, the work of Italicus alone survives complete; we have thought it not unlikely that Roman boys used it for a long time in the schools, so that that little book brings a great deal of light to the Homeric studies that flourished at Rome.
