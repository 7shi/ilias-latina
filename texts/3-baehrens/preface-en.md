XVIII.

THE LATIN ILIAD OF ITALICUS.

An epitome of the *Iliad* in the Latin language, chiefly after Virgil and Ovid, was composed by a certain Italicus, as the parastichis of verses 1–8 and 1063–70 attests.  Fr. Buecheler, who first detected that device [Rhein. Mus. XXXV p. 390 f.], persuaded himself that he was the well-known author of the *Punica*, less rightly, since it is established from Martial [VII 63] that Silius began to write poetry only after the death of Nero, whereas L. Mueller [Philologus XV p. 481], following Lachmann [Kleine philol. Schriften p. 161 f.], has proved that this poem came out, if not earlier, then certainly while Nero was still alive.  But although the person of Italicus is unknown and his name was soon forgotten, you will conclude not without reason from Lactantius, the commentator on Statius (cf. on v. 1048), that the little work itself passed already in antiquity into the schools (for whose use it had perhaps been composed); for whether M. Schmidt [preface to Hyginus p. LI] rightly detected a trace of it in the *Fabulae* of Hyginus is not so certain.  Corrupted in many ways, it reached the so-called Middle Age, in which from Charlemagne onwards it enjoyed no small honour; for between 850 and 855 Ermenricus, in his letter to Grimold [ed. Duemmler, Halle 1873, p. 10], cited v. 7 with the words *ut apud Homerum in Iliade* ("as in Homer in the *Iliad*"), and in the tenth century the poet who sang the deeds of the emperor Berengar took not a few small pieces of our

But when those good monks read that Homer had lived among the Greeks, the question soon arose who had rendered the poem in the Latin language; and with the ignorance that they had in these matters, confounding everything utterly, they assumed, by I know not what reasoning, that Pindar had performed that task.  Hence we read in Benzo, bishop of Alba, in a poem composed about 1087 [Monum. Germ. afterwards held its ground in the editions down to our century.  But since, as I have said, the epitome flourished in great favour through the later part of the Middle Age (of which there are testimonies and many imitations met with in Albert of Stade and other writers of that time, and a grammarian of the twelfth to thirteenth century published in A. Mai, *Auct. class.* vol. VII), and was diligently studied in the schools, as Eberhard of Béthune attests (*Labyrinthus* tract. III 45 f.), it came about both that the corruption of the text, which had already spread in antiquity, grew day by day, and that the old copies, worn out by much use, vanished, by a fate very like that which the *Metamorphoses* of Ovid underwent.  Hence today there is no manuscript, as far as we know, earlier than the eleventh century, while there are many recent and foully interpolated ones.  We have chosen eight books to use as guides in establishing the wording of the text.  They are these:

B, a manuscript of the British Museum in London, Addit. 15601, of the twelfth to thirteenth century, fol. 102–108, containing vv. 1–832.  I collated it myself.

E, Erfurt, Amplonianus no. 20, of the twelfth century, on which cf. Ritschl, *Opusc.* III p. 842 ff., with the title *Incipit liber Homeri ornatus*; it has undergone two correctors, of whom the first, if not the scribe himself, is certainly contemporary with him (m. 1) and corrected much modestly and prudently by comparison with some good exemplar, while the second, of about the fourteenth century, both added very many glosses and disfigured the text with interpolations (m. 2).  I myself examined it once, and Th. Krafft gave a collation of it in the Nuremberg school programme of 1874.

F, Florence, Laurentianus plut. 68, 24, of the eleventh century, which after Avianus gives the poem on fol. 55–74, provided with the same title as in E.  I collated it myself in 1872; cf. also C. Schenkl in the *Zeitschrift für die österreichischen Gymnasien* of 1875, p. 243 ff.

N, Munich 19462, of the eleventh century, fol. 1–18 (*de bello troyano homerus* written at the head by a later hand).  I collated both M and N myself in 1872; Wilhelm Meyer afterwards kindly instructed me when I was in doubt about some of their readings.

V, Venice, Bessarionis 497, of the beginning of the twelfth century, which among various small works of Latin grammarians gives our epitome on fol. 59–65, and besides it other minor poems, but such that nothing is gained from them.  I collated it myself.

These books are so related to one another that EL may have flowed from one exemplar, perhaps French, MN from a second, German, and FV from a third, Italian, while BG, somewhat more recent, their origin already obscured, have more often departed from them.  E and L stand out above the rest for the purity of their text; although the state of this little book is such that it forbids you to bind yourself to a single book or family of books, and bids you choose prudently from all whatever commends itself on its own merits.  But the immense multitude of variant readings found in the manuscripts listed above imposed on me this rule: leaving out nearly all orthographical ones, to note most of the readings from E and L alone, and from the rest the most remarkable ones.  You may be assured that I have discharged this task in such a way as to leave out nothing in any place of some weight.

Now the other manuscripts, written in the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries, of which we have inspected not a few, offer nothing good apart from new interpolations and faults*); nor does the Brussels manuscript 2718 of the fifteenth century (to testify to this in plain words, for Reiffenberg had raised great hopes of it in some) give anything other than the vulgate text.  Finally, the books used by Kooten besides L are also to be rejected.  I have added a few readings from them (ς).

Among the learned, the emendation of the text, which lay wretchedly neglected for a long time (only the editions of Fano of 1505 and 1515 had contributed a little), was advanced most by the Dutch, both Petrus Bondam [*Variae lectiones*, 1759] and above all Johannes Schrader.  Under his auspices and guidance several (I name Higt before the others) devoted their labour to this poet; their studies were collected and increased with his own discoveries by Theod. van Kooten, whose edition, tossed by various fortunes, was at last published by Weytingh [Amsterdam 1806], while in the meantime Wernsdorf had included the epitome in volume IV of the *Poetae Latini Minores*.  Finally L. Mueller issued its text furnished with a selected critical apparatus [Berlin 1857], and he also discussed many passages more fully in the Göttingen *Philologus*, loc. cit.; cf. also Rhein. Mus. XXII 457 ff.

\*) There should nevertheless be mentioned the Ambrosianus T 5 sup., which among other things has fragments of the epitome on palimpsest leaves of the thirteenth century; I owe the knowledge of it to Gustav Loewe, who made a specimen of the writing with the greatest labour.
