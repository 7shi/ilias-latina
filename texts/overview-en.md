## 1. The poem

There survives an *Iliad* condensed into Latin hexameters, of 1,070
verses (1,075 in Wernsdorf), "often given the false title *Homerus
Latinus* or *Pindarus Thebanus*"
([Plessis, preface, p. I](4-plessis/preface.md#p-i)).  On these points
all the editors agree: the author took from the *Iliad* little more
than the bare sequence of events, and either invented the rest himself
or adapted it from Virgil and Ovid
([Wernsdorf p. 456](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-456)); the poem was read
in the schools in the Middle Ages; and finally, as Plessis says, "of so
many Latin poets who did not fear to translate the verses of Homer into
Latin ones, the work of Italicus alone survives complete"
([p. LI](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-li)).  On nearly everything else
they disagree: the author's name, his date, his station, his purpose
and the worth of the work.

## 2. Title and author

### The titles in the manuscripts

In antiquity the poem seems to have been called "simply *Homerus*"
([Wernsdorf p. 457](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-457)).  Ermenricus of
Ellwangen, quoting verse 7 about the year 850, used only these words:
"as in Homer in the *Iliad*"; the catalogues of manuscripts compiled in
the 9th to 11th centuries also mention an *Omerus* fairly often
([Baehrens p. 4](3-baehrens/preface.md#p-4);
[Plessis p. XLIX](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xlix)).  The manuscripts
are headed *incipit liber homeri* (Erfurt), *incipit liber omeri*
(Leiden), *incipit Homerus* (Wolfenbüttel), *de bello troyano homerus*
(Munich 19462), *LIBER HOMERI POETAE* (Valenciennes)
([Plessis p. XLIX](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xlix);
[Vollmer p. V](6-vollmer/preface.md#p-v)).  The later manuscripts and
the editions add the titles *Homerus de bello Trojano*, *de
destructione Trojae*, *Homeri Iliadum opus*, *Epitome ac summa
universae Iliados Homeri*
([Wernsdorf p. 457](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-457)).

### The acrostic ITALICVS SCRIPSIT

Franz Bücheler (*Rhein. Mus.* 35, 1880) first noticed that the initial
letters of verses 1–8 and 1063–1070 almost spell a name and a verb.  In
the manuscripts, however, they read ITALICES and SCQIPSIT, so that two
verses must be emended.

- **Verse 7.**  To restore the acrostic, Baehrens took *Ut primum* to
  the beginning of the verse and removed the words *Ex quo* from the
  text.  Plessis objected that *Ex quo* renders the sixth verse of the
  *Iliad* (ἐξ οὗ δὴ τὰ πρῶτα) and cannot be removed.  Louis Havet
  found another way through Ermenricus, whose verse begins with
  *Protulerunt* and has *turmas* where the manuscripts have *pugnas*.
  He judged *Protulerunt* to be a gloss, *pro tulerunt*, written in the
  margin of an old copy to explain some word "the same in substance,
  but unusual", which then burst into the text in place of the genuine
  reading; *pugnas* is nothing but a fabrication, and *turmas* points
  to the true reading.  So he restored *Volverunt — turbas*: "whence
  comes ITALICVS" ([Plessis pp. V–VI](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-v)).
- **Verse 1065.**  In the manuscripts it begins with the letter Q,
  where R is required.  Baehrens moved *remis* from the end to the
  beginning; Plessis had first conjectured *Raptim*, and Havet thought
  it better to write *Raris*
  ([Plessis p. VI](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-vi)).
- **Hertz** kept *Ex quo* and altered verse 9 so as to bring out the
  acrostic *Italice Sili*, as if the work were dedicated to Silius
  Italicus.  Plessis answers that the later acrostic *scripsit*
  requires a nominative in the earlier one, not a vocative
  ([Plessis p. VI](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-vi)).

Baehrens was the first of the editors who did not hesitate to put the
name of Italicus on the little book
([Plessis p. V](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-v)); after Havet's
emendations Plessis considers the name placed beyond doubt.

### Baebius Italicus

In 1890 H. Schenkl published the title of the Vienna manuscript lat.
3509, a paper manuscript of the 15th century: *Bebii Italici poetae
clarissimi epithome in quatuor viginti libros homeri iliados*.  Neither
he nor anyone after him ventured to accept it, until Vollmer argued
that a scribe who could produce the correct cognomen ITALICVS without
having perceived the acrostic, if he also adds the family name BEBIVS,
"drew both from a good and pure source, namely from a lost manuscript
of the poem".  He therefore calls the poet Baebius Italicus
([Vollmer p. IV, note 1](6-vollmer/preface.md#p-iv);
[p. VI](6-vollmer/preface.md#p-vi)).

### Was it Silius Italicus?

Bücheler persuaded himself that the poet was the same as the author of
the *Punica*; Munk followed him, and Doering, in a dissertation
published at Strasbourg in 1884, defended the same view with much
learning, from similarities of metre and diction.

- **Baehrens** rejects this view: it is established from Martial (7.63)
  that Silius began to write poetry only after Nero's death, whereas
  this poem came out, "if not earlier, then certainly while Nero was
  still alive" ([Baehrens p. 3](3-baehrens/preface.md#p-3)).
- **Plessis** grants that the chronology does not conflict (Silius was
  born in 25 and died in 101), and does not think that Martial proves
  Silius wrote no verses even in his youth.  But he finds nothing in the
  poem or in external circumstances that adds any likelihood to the
  suspicion, and says it is remarkable that no ancient testimony of it
  exists.  Doering's similarities (*vulgus ruit undique* and *ruit
  undique vulgus*) are empty too, since Roman poets freely took the
  words of others.  He thinks the poet himself was a schoolmaster,
  perhaps humble and poor, and concludes in the words of Lucian
  Mueller: "There is also a certain virtue in not knowing"
  ([Plessis pp. XV–XVII](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xv)).

### Wernsdorf's Avienus

Wernsdorf, who did not yet know the acrostics, looked for the author as
if the poem were anonymous.  He first proposed Pentadius, then gave up
the view, because Pentadius entangled his poetry "with plays of wit and
difficult artifice" and cannot lie hidden under the name *Pindarus* in
the later manuscripts ([Wernsdorf p. 468](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-468)).
Finally he proposed Rufius Festus Avienus as the author, on these
grounds ([Wernsdorf pp. 457–468](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-457)):

- He infers that the author was a Roman from verse 902 [907], which
  reads *Non clarae gentis nobis mansisset origo*, and from his
  imitating Roman poets everywhere.
- That he was a Roman noble and a wealthy man, who composed the poem
  "for the sake of delighting his mind and his leisure in the
  retirement of his country estate", he conjectured from the epigram
  *De Navigatione* in the *Anthologia Latina*, in which the author,
  praising his seaside villa, says *hoc quondam rure solebam Iliadum
  armatas sollicitare manus* ("on this estate I was once wont to stir
  up the armed hands of the sons of Ilium") and at the end mentions
  Ulysses and Penelope.  He also took the images of seafaring with
  which the Epitome closes (the keel, the oars, the shores, vv. 1063
  ff.) to point to the same villa.  The epigram itself, with his notes
  and emendations, stands first among the
  [testimonia](2-lemaire/testimonia.md).
- A monument dedicated to the goddess Nortia attests that Avienus
  wrote many poems; he did not so much translate Aratus and Dionysius
  as interpolate them, in the same way as the Epitome treats Homer;
  Servius reports that he wrote the whole of Virgil and Livy in
  iambics, which Wernsdorf takes to mean condensed summaries of the
  works.
- The epilogues of the *Periegesis* and of the Epitome are alike,
  invoking Phoebus and the Muses, and both authors were pagans.

The Lemaire editor notes that Wernsdorf himself later withdrew the view
by which he had confused the poet Avienus with the proconsul Festus
([p. 465, note](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-465)).  Plessis refutes the
whole opinion from metre (see [Date](#3-date)), and rejects the picture
of a noble at leisure, which would fit Silius equally well: the poet
was a humble schoolmaster
([Plessis p. XVI](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xvi)).

### The false name Pindarus

From the Middle Ages to the 18th century the poem went under the name
of Pindarus Thebanus.  The testimonies collected by the editors are
these:

- Benzo, bishop of Alba, about 1087 names *Pindarus seu Homerus* among
  Roman writers; Hugo von Trimberg in the 13th century speaks of Homer,
  "whom the philosopher Pindar is said to have translated and turned
  into metre for the Latin teachers"
  ([Baehrens p. 4](3-baehrens/preface.md#p-4);
  [Plessis p. XLIX](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xlix)).
- Vatican Reginensis 1708, of the 13th–14th century, is headed
  *Incipit liber pindari translatoris homeri* and has the subscription
  *Pindarus hunc librum fecit sectatus homerum; / Grecus homerus erat,
  sed pindarus ipse latinus*.
- Vatican Palatinus 1611, of the end of the 14th century, has
  *Pandarus hunc secum trans pontum uexit homerum*; the Santenianus has
  *Pindarus* in the same verse, and Paris lat. 8413 (1403) several
  verses of the kind.
- Vossius mentions a Padua manuscript entitled *Pindarus Thebanus de
  destructione Trojae*, dated 1390 in the testimonia
  ([p. 514](2-lemaire/testimonia.md#p-514)) but 1300 in Wernsdorf's
  prooemium ([p. 469](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-469)).  The editions
  use the name from 1492 onwards.

The origin of the name is explained in various ways:

- **Wernsdorf** ([pp. 469–472](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-469)) thinks
  the name was invented only in the 14th century, because Eberhard of
  Béthune in the 13th names only Homer.  He offers two explanations:
  either the Alexandrian grammarian Ptolemaeus, surnamed Pindarion, who
  wrote on Homeric matters, was confused by a man of a later age with
  the Theban poet; or a title *Pandari de bello Trojano*, taken from
  the poem itself, was prefixed by some smatterer, after the manner of
  Dictys, Dares and Albert of Stade's *Troilus*, and then changed by
  scribes into the better-known name *Pindarus*.  He adds that in the
  second Wolfenbüttel manuscript *Pindari* is set beside the name
  *Pandari* as a variant in two places.
- **Baehrens** ([p. 4 and notes](3-baehrens/preface.md#p-4)) answers
  that *Pindarus* is confirmed by far older witnesses, while *Pandarus*
  arose by an easy error or a new fiction, and with Lucian Mueller
  refers the name "solely to the ignorance of the monks", who, reading
  that Homer had lived among the Greeks, asked who had put him into
  Latin.
- **Plessis** ([pp. XLVIII–L](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xlviii))
  follows Baehrens in restoring *Pindarus* for *Pandarus* from the
  Santenianus, and shows from Benzo's testimony that the name was
  already current in the 11th century.  Of Wernsdorf's two
  explanations he rejects Pandarus, who plays a very slight part in the
  poem, and grants that Pindarion is "something better"; for the rest,
  the name may be referred solely to the ignorance of a monk or scribe.
  The sea across which the subscriptions say Homer was carried is the
  sea that divides Greece from Italy, and *Thebanus* is a crude error
  on which all agree.
- **Weytingh** (1809), from the subscription of the Santenianus
  (*Pindarus hunc secum trans pontum uexit Homerum*), devised that the
  poet was, "if not a contemporary, at least a fellow countryman" of
  Joseph of Exeter, an English monk of the 11th or 12th century.
  Plessis, with Lucian Mueller, answers that those verses allude to the
  waves that separate Brundisium from Dyrrachium rather than to the sea
  between Dover and Calais, and that verse 7 is already quoted by
  Ermenricus about the year 850
  ([Plessis pp. VII–VIII](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-vii)).

## 3. Date

### Wernsdorf: the fourth century

Wernsdorf placed the Epitome in the age "from Constantine the Great
down to the Theodosian times"
([pp. 465–466](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-465)).  He recognized in its
language the marks of declining Latinity, and held that the very plan
of condensing the *Iliad* into a small poem suited the age of
breviaries and epitomes (Eutropius, Aurelius Victor, Vegetius).  He
also used verse 902 [907]: the author would not have recalled the
descent of the Caesars from Aeneas if he had written after the Empire
itself had ended.  Plessis describes this view as assigning the poem
"to the beginning of the fourth century after the birth of Christ or
the end of the third" ([p. VIII](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-viii)).

### Lachmann, Mueller, Baehrens, Plessis: before Nero's death

Lachmann first showed the way, and Lucian Mueller (1857) and Baehrens
([p. 3](3-baehrens/preface.md#p-3)) followed.  Plessis pursues the
matter more closely ([pp. VIII–XIV](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-viii)):

- **From metre.**  Italicus does not elide an iambic word even in a
  long syllable, nor a cretic; at the end of the hexameter he nowhere
  admits a non-enclitic monosyllable after a polysyllable, and a
  polysyllable only once, and that a Greek name (*Iphidamanta*, 750);
  he does not shorten final *o*; in Greek names no error of quantity
  offends.  Avienus, on the contrary, neglects all these rules in the
  *Periegesis* and the *Ora maritima*, as do Pentadius, Ausonius and
  Prudentius.  The metrical art is therefore that of the most elegant
  poets of the first century.
- **From language.**  The one suspicious word, *rimabant* (456), is an
  old active form used by Pacuvius and perhaps Phaedrus; Plessis
  suspects that Italicus took it from some old translator of the
  *Iliad*.
- **Terminus post quem.**  Since he borrows much from the *Aeneid* and
  the *Metamorphoses*, the poet could not have written before those
  works had long been spread among the public.
- **Terminus ante quem.**  In verses 899–902 Neptune is said to have
  saved Aeneas so that he might *Augustumque genus claris submitteret
  astris* ("send the Augustan race up to the bright stars"), and in
  verses 236 and 483 Aeneas is called the offspring of Venus.  These
  make sense while the Julian house held power; once it had died out
  with Nero, the poet would hardly have recalled a fable unwelcome to
  an emperor of another family.

Plessis therefore concludes that the Epitome was composed before the
year 68, perhaps, as Lachmann thinks, before the death of Tiberius,
certainly in the first part of the century, "and that Italicus lived
as a contemporary of Phaedrus rather than of Lucan"
([p. XIV](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xiv)).  Baehrens and Vollmer
accept a date before Nero's death.

## 4. The Latin translators of Homer

Both Wernsdorf ([pp. 474–498](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-474)) and
Plessis ([pp. XIX–XXIX](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xix)) review those
who put Homer into Latin before Italicus, so that the place of the
Epitome may be judged more rightly.  They agree on the names and
disagree on some of the attributions.

| Translator | Work | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Livius Andronicus | *Odyssey* in Saturnians | Long read to Roman boys, as Horace attests.  Plessis defends it against its critics, using fragments restored by Havet. |
| Naevius | *Cypria Ilias* | So Wernsdorf; Plessis, with Huschke, claims the fragments for Ninnius Crassus and calls it an error to give them to Naevius (Estienne, Heyne, Wernsdorf) or to Laevius (Scaliger). |
| Cn. Matius | *Iliad* in hexameters | Also the author of *Mimiambi*.  Both editors think him older than C. Matius, Caesar's friend.  Fragments in Varro, Gellius, Charisius, Diomedes and Priscian; Plessis judges the version free and not without brilliance. |
| Ninnius Crassus | *Iliad* in hexameters | Three verses survive.  Wernsdorf doubts whether the name is a corruption of Matius. |
| M. Tullius Cicero | Single passages | From the *Iliad* seven times and from the *Odyssey* once (the Sirens) in his own works, and two more verses in Augustine.  Plessis finds the Sirens' verses not unpleasing, though far from Homer. |
| Attius Labeo | *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, word for word | Mocked by Persius and his scholiast; one verse survives (*Crudum manduces Priamum Priamique pisinnos*).  Bergk's view that the Epitome is Labeo's work is rejected by Haupt and Plessis. |
| Poets in Ovid, *Pont.* 4.16 | Trojan subjects | Macer Iliacus (the events before and after Homer), Camerinus, Largus (Antenor), Lupus (the return of Menelaus), Tuticanus (a *Phaeacis*).  Plessis excludes them, because they did not translate but composed poems of their own. |

Wernsdorf goes further: Nero's *Troica* and *Capture of Troy*, Lucan's
*Iliacon*, Petronius's *Capture of Troy* (which he appends to the
Epitome), Statius's *Achilleid*, the Latin verses of Homer in
Chalcidius, the *Periochae* ascribed to Ausonius, and finally, in the
Middle Ages, Joseph of Exeter and Albert of Stade.  He also mentions
the Homeristae, who recited the poems of Homer, sometimes in Latin, at
the banquets of the rich (Petronius; Juvenal 11.177).

## 5. Sources and method

### Did the poet read Homer himself?

- **Wernsdorf** ([pp. 472–473](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-472)) suspects
  that the author had before his eyes "not the Greek work of Homer
  itself" but an older Latin translation of it, interpolated and
  adapted to Virgil's sense.  He draws his argument from the
  *Periochae* of the *Iliad* ascribed to Ausonius, which agree with the
  Epitome in some things foreign to Homer (Hector dragged three times
  round the walls; prayers to Achilles for the return of Hector's
  body), but have others that could not have been taken from the
  Epitome.  Both, therefore, flowed from a fuller *Iliad*, translated
  into Latin and conformed to Virgil.
- **Plessis** ([p. XXXI](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xxxi)) admits that
  the knot cannot be untied, but suspects that the poet, taken up with
  the duties of a schoolmaster, wrote the poem piece by piece in his
  spare time, helped above all by memory, and followed the usage of the
  Latins more than that of the Greeks.  He points to the recurrence of
  the same words at short intervals (*implicuit* 12, *implicitus* 14;
  *flevit* 14, *fletus* 18, *fletibus* 22).
- **Vollmer** set beside each verse, in the margin, the Greek verses
  that the epitomator reproduces, so that it becomes clear at the same
  time "in which parts he displayed his own art and invention"
  ([p. VIII](6-vollmer/preface.md#p-viii)).

### Where he departed from Homer

Plessis lists the chief places
([pp. XXXI–XXXII](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xxxi)):

| Verse | Epitome | *Iliad* |
|---|---|---|
| 78 | Minerva restrains Achilles after Briseis has been taken away | before she is taken (1.188 ff.) |
| 151 | The words are given to Nestor | spoken by Ulysses (2.300) |
| 372 | Democoon is killed by Agamemnon | by Ulysses (4.499) |
| 461 | Diomedes' stone was one twelve young men could not carry | two men (5.303) |
| 839 | The son of Nestor carries Patroclus's body to the camp | Antilochus only reports the death; Menelaus and Meriones carry the body (17.685, 717–722) |
| 855 | Achilles asks his mother for arms | Thetis promises them of her own accord (18.73) |
| 857 | Etna as Vulcan's workshop | nowhere in the *Iliad* |
| 1006 | Hector is dragged round the walls of Troy | round the tomb of Patroclus (24.16) |

It is easy to see that Etna and Hector dragged round the walls have
burst in from the *Aeneid*; from this Plessis concludes that Italicus
chose Virgil rather than Homer as his guide.  He also compiled his
index so that one might "at once discover in what things Italicus
departed from Homer" ([preface, p. II](4-plessis/preface.md#p-ii)).

### The imitation of Virgil and Ovid

On this all the editors agree.  Wernsdorf says the poet imitates the
older poets so zealously "that he often seems to desert Homer for the
sake of transferring there some famous passage of an old poet"
([pp. 456–457](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-456)), and in his commentary
set the parallel passages beside the text, as van Kooten had done
before him ([Plessis p. XLVI](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xlvi)).
Plessis gives a selection of examples
([pp. XXXIII–XXXIV](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xxxiii)):

| Verse | Epitome | Model |
|---|---|---|
| 58 | *exarsit subito violentia regis* | Virgil, *Aen.* 11.376 *exarsit dictis violentia Turni* |
| 96 | *Dixit; at illa leves caeli delapsa per oras* | repeated whole from *Aen.* 11.595 |
| 236 | *Veneris certissima proles* | *Aen.* 6.322 *deum certissima proles* |
| 311 | *Ultimus ille dies Paridis foret* | *Aen.* 9.758 *Ultimus ille dies bello gentique fuisset* |
| 655–657 | Jupiter holds the *aequato pondere lances* | *Aen.* 12.723 |
| 298 | *Non aliter fortes … tauri* | Ovid, *Met.* 9.46 |
| 821 | *Nam licet ipse suis Mavors te protegat armis* | *Met.* 8.394 |
| 111 | *Nox erat et toto fulgebant sidera mundo* | Horace, *Epod.* 15.1 |
| 857 | *Aetnaeos calidis fornacibus ignes* | Lucretius 6.681 |

Wernsdorf's [excursus](2-lemaire/excursus.md) show how much this
imitation matters for establishing the text.  He points out that the
description of the shield of Achilles (862–865 [867–870]) is modelled
on Ovid's palace of the Sun (*Met.* 2.5 ff.), and brackets the two
middle verses about the Nymphs and Nereids, which disturb Ovid's order
of heaven, earth and sea (Excursus II).  Mars and the Fates on the
shield (889–891 [894–896]) imitate Virgil's shield of Aeneas (*Aen.*
8.700 ff.), but join war with natural death, to signify the end of all
human life (Excursus III).  In the other two he proposes emendations
where the transmitted text does not fit the sense: *mox hoste
repulsi* for *repulso* at 688 [690], where the Greeks, beaten back,
send envoys to Achilles (Excursus I), and *Impetuosus* for
*Impediatur* at 915 [920], of the river Xanthus (Excursus IV).

Plessis concludes from this that the poet at first approached the work

only the main events; either his strength often failed him, or he did
not lay down firm enough rules of condensing for himself.

## 6. The book division

The manuscripts divide the poem into 24 books after the model of the
*Iliad*, but not always at the same places.  Wernsdorf himself marked
off the books of Homer in the Epitome and indicated them by numbers
placed at their beginnings, after comparison with Homer
([p. 498](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-498)).  Vollmer rejects the
division: he is so far from believing that Italicus himself prescribed
it that he concludes "that at the end of the Roman Empire, when Greek

## 7. Talent, purpose and worth

### Judgements

- **Wernsdorf** judges most kindly: "to those who read that poem
  attentively the author appears endowed with good talent and
  judgment, a stranger to the faults of the writers of the later age,
  such as bombast and the affectation of points", although he now and
  then uses common words ([pp. 456–457](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-456)).
  Yet he does not venture to call him an epic poet, because he is too
  narrow and thin in the sequence of events and untimely copious in the
  episodes ([p. 499](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-499)).
- **Earlier judgements** are collected in the testimonia: Lorenzo
  Valla, according to Pontano, said the poet was to be preferred to
  Virgil; Lindenbrog thought him no more inept than Accius Labeo; Barth
  called him "an ancient author and of quite good talent" and judged
  him far older than Joseph of Exeter; Bondam, with Barth, inferred from
  verse 902 [907] that he was a Roman
  ([testimonia pp. 511–514](2-lemaire/testimonia.md#p-511)).
- **Plessis** judges most harshly
  ([pp. XXXV–XXXVII](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xxxv)).  Italicus
  narrates "mere wars", "battles and slaughters, attacks and flights",
  nothing that touches the inmost feelings of the soul.  Achilles is a
  harsh soldier who never speaks of his own death; Phoenix is not even
  named; Nestor and Ulysses play small parts; Andromache is brought in
  only twice, Hecuba "in frigid verses"; Helen speaking with Priam is
  left out; the gods seldom intervene and never put on the faces of
  mortals.  Chryses' prayer, which Homer gave "in a few weighty words"
  (1.37 ff.), takes twelve verses in Italicus (32–43), "for commonplaces
  delight Italicus above all".
- **Vollmer** says nothing about poetic worth; his preface is wholly
  concerned with the manuscripts.

### Purpose

Baehrens concludes from Lactantius that the little work passed into
the schools already in antiquity, "for whose use it had perhaps been
composed" ([p. 3](3-baehrens/preface.md#p-3)).  On this Plessis builds
his defence ([pp. XXXVII–XXXIX](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xxxvii)).
Roman boys read Homer in Greek, and the master used to help with a
Latin translation; when the *Odyssey* of Livius had gone out of use a
little after Horace, some master could embrace the Homeric stories
briefly in a Latin book, so that the pupils might hold them more
firmly in memory.  Judged by that purpose, the Epitome is "a useful
work of a modest man, in which the purity both of language and of
metre is to be praised", and it held its place in the schools for a
long time, perhaps until the *Periochae* of Ausonius prevailed.  In
the Middle Ages, had people not received this small image of the
*Iliad*, they would have lived wholly ignorant of the Homeric poem.
Wernsdorf, on the contrary, thought of a noble's leisure (see above)
and of the men of his age, who "wished to devour even great Homer in an
epitome, as if in a nutshell"
([p. 499](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-499)).

## 8. The transmission of the poem

### Antiquity and the Middle Ages

- **Lactantius Placidus**, the commentator on Statius, quotes three
  verses on Hector's funeral (1048 ff.) as Homer's
  ([testimonia p. 511](2-lemaire/testimonia.md#p-511);
  [Baehrens p. 3](3-baehrens/preface.md#p-3)).  Wernsdorf thought he
  lived in the 6th century and was the first witness.  M. Schmidt's
  belief that he had found a trace of the poem in the *Fabulae* of
  Hyginus is, Baehrens says, not so certain.
- **Ermenricus of Ellwangen**, in his letter to Grimald (850–855),
  quotes verse 7, with readings that preserve traces of the true text
  ([Baehrens p. 3](3-baehrens/preface.md#p-3);
  [Plessis p. VI](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-vi)).
- **The *Gesta Berengarii*** (10th century) borrow not a few small
  pieces of the poem, the more important of which Baehrens copied into
  his notes; they already show nearly the same corruptions from which
  the surviving manuscripts suffer ([p. 4](3-baehrens/preface.md#p-4)).
- **Eberhard of Béthune**, *Labyrinthus* 3.45 f. (13th century), names
  Homer among the authors read in the schools
  ([testimonia p. 511](2-lemaire/testimonia.md#p-511)); Albert of Stade
  and a grammarian published by Mai used the poem
  ([Baehrens p. 5](3-baehrens/preface.md#p-5)).

From constant use in the schools it came about that the corruption of
the text grew day by day and the old copies, worn out, vanished, "by a
fate very like that which the *Metamorphoses* of Ovid underwent"
([Baehrens p. 5](3-baehrens/preface.md#p-5)).  Plessis blames the
scribes of the 11th to 13th centuries, whence "those monsters of Greek
names", transposed words and spurious verses; the Italians, who might
have applied healing hands, neglected the poem, at a time when it was
hardly studied in the German schools either
([p. XLV](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xlv)).

### The manuscripts

Each editor used more manuscripts than those before him and judged
them differently.

- **Wernsdorf** ([pp. 499–501](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-499)) used four
  late ones: two from Wolfenbüttel (one of the 13th century, lacking
  verses 180 to 628; the other of the late 15th century), one from
  Helmstedt (14th century, beginning at verse 266), and one from
  Annaberg, buried under scholia, whose title is *Homerus grecus per
  Pindarum insignem oratorem … traductus*.  He based his recension on
  the Basel edition of 1541.
- **Baehrens** ([pp. 5–7](3-baehrens/preface.md#p-5)) chose eight: B
  (London), E (Erfurt), F (Florence), G (Wolfenbüttel), L (Leiden), M
  and N (Munich) and V (Venice).  He thinks them so related that EL
  flowed from one exemplar, perhaps French, MN from a second, German,
  and FV from a third, Italian, while BG are mixed; E and L stand out
  above the rest for the purity of their text.  The later ones,
  including Brussels 2718, offer nothing good.
- **Plessis** ([pp. XLI–XLV](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xli))
  reproduced much of Baehrens's apparatus without having seen any
  manuscript himself.  He added the Burmannianus (R), which reaches
  only verse 644 and whose scribe rendered the letters of his exemplar
  most scrupulously, the Vossianus (A), the Santenianus (S), the
  Virgilianus (I) and the second Guelferbytanus (Y), and the readings
  of three more collated for him: Brussels 2718 (T), Paris lat. 8413
  (C, 1403) and Paris lat. 14909 (D).  His sixteen sigla partly clash
  with Vollmer's.
- **Vollmer** ([pp. IV–VII, X](6-vollmer/preface.md#p-iv)) collated
  fourteen himself or checked them against photographs, and says that
  Baehrens proceeded "more carelessly than usual", reporting much of M
  and N wrongly.  He added D (Prague), which Baehrens had overlooked,
  and five found since, AWPCR; of these A, W, P and R, of the 10th or
  10th–11th century, are the oldest witnesses of the poem.

On the transmission Vollmer teaches the following
([p. VII](6-vollmer/preface.md#p-vii)): a single old copy, perhaps from
Spain, reached the Carolingians, and when copied it produced the
abundance of copies that masters used in the schools from about the
9th century.  Two families can still be distinguished: to one belong
PW(A); from the other were derived first the stock MN, then ELR, and
finally CFV; but some good readings from PW passed into the exemplar
of E and, more of them, into the source of CFV.  The later ones, such
as B, G and the Helmstadiensis, are all of a mixed kind.  He knew about
fifty later manuscripts, of which he cites only the Helmstadiensis
often; he names the Vienna manuscript only for its title
([p. VI](6-vollmer/preface.md#p-vi)).  The dates also changed: Baehrens
assigns E and L to the 12th century, Vollmer to the 11th.

### The editions

Which was the first edition was already unclear to Wernsdorf and
Plessis.  Wernsdorf and his Lemaire editor
([pp. 502–505](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-502)) list, among others: a
Florentine edition without date, entitled *Homerus de bello Trojano*;
the Parma edition of Ugoletus (1492), whose title says that Pindar made
the epitome for the instruction of his son; the Leipzig editions of
Martinus Herbipolensis (about 1494–1497), with epitaphs of Hector and
Achilles; the Fano edition of Abstemius (1505, repeated by Polyardus in
1515); the Vienna edition (1513), divided into eight books; the
Hagenau edition of Secerius (1531); the Basel edition of Torinus
(1541), with Joseph of Exeter under the name of Dares; Spondanus's
Homer (Basel, 1583 and 1606), which reproduced Torinus's copy; and the
Pesaro collection (1766).  Vollmer collated five: α (1477, without
place, "the first edition, as it seems"), β (Ugoletus, 1492), δ
(Denidel, 1498), φ (Fano, 1505) and λ (Leipzig, 1509)
([pp. VI–VII](6-vollmer/preface.md#p-vi)).  Of the early editions
Baehrens, Mueller and Plessis value only the Fano edition.

## 9. The history of scholarship

From the editors' accounts the following sequence emerges, with the
judgements each passed on the others:

- **The Renaissance.**  Valla's immoderate praise; Barth's
  emendations and praise in the *Adversaria* and in his commentary on
  Statius (testimonia; [Wernsdorf p. 501](2-lemaire/prooemium.md#p-501)).
- **The Dutch of the 18th century.**  Bondam (*Variae lectiones*, 1759,
  especially in restoring proper names), van Dorp and Schrader
  (*Observationes*), Higt, van der Dussen (*Prodromus*, 1769, suspected
  of stealthily taking part of Higt's discoveries), de Rooy
  (*Spicilegia critica*, 1771).  Vollmer: "chance brought it about that
  the Dutch accomplished the best and the most"
  ([p. VIII](6-vollmer/preface.md#p-viii)).
- **Wernsdorf** (1784) gathered the studies of his predecessors and
  added his own.  Plessis: though he used poor manuscripts and did some
  things with a hasty hand, he "deserved far better of our poet than
  those who preceded him" ([p. XLVI](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xlvi)).
- **Van Kooten and Weytingh** (Leiden and Amsterdam, 1809; Baehrens
  says Amsterdam, 1806).  Van Kooten read the Leiden manuscript,
  healed several passages by praiseworthy conjecture and noted the
  passages of Virgil, Ovid and Homer; Weytingh added little
  ([Plessis p. XLVI](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xlvi)).
- **L. Mueller** (Berlin, 1857) joined the Erfurt manuscript to the
  Leiden one and "established the true critical foundation"; Plessis,
  however, reproves him lightly for excerpting superfluous variants
  from bad manuscripts ([pp. XLVI–XLVII](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xlvi)).
  Vollmer says he edited the verses "without cutting to the quick".
- **Baehrens** (1881) was the first to drive out the vulgate reading
  and substitute the testimony of the manuscripts (Vollmer); Plessis
  judges that he resorted too much to conjecture, Vollmer that he was
  careless in collating.
- **Plessis** (1885), with Havet's help.  Vollmer: he "achieved almost
  nothing", and R. Ehwald judged the book rightly
  ([p. VIII](6-vollmer/preface.md#p-viii)).
- **Vollmer** (1913), who set out the readings of the manuscripts more
  fully in *Sitz.-Ber. d. Münch. Akad.* 1913, no. 3, and H. C. Remme,
  who at his suggestion wrote on the fortunes of the manuscripts
  (1906).

## 10. The indexes

Two of the editions end with indexes of different kinds:

- **Plessis's index of names and subjects**
  ([index.md](4-plessis/index.md)) arranges each name by its cases and
  briefly tells the story at each verse, for Achilles, for example,
  from his anger at Agamemnon (8) to Priam's prayers (1043), with
  separate headwords *Aeacides*, *Pelides*, *Nereius*, *Thetideius
  heros*.  He printed the so-called ornamental epithets in heavier
  type, to show how much more the poet followed the usage of the Latins
  than that of the Greeks
  ([p. XXXI, note 2](4-plessis/introduction.md#p-xxxi)).
- **Vollmer's index of names** ([index.md](6-vollmer/index.md)) is
  shorter and more philological: it quotes the words of the poem in
  Latin, identifies the persons (adding patronymics and Homeric forms),
  marks emended passages with an asterisk and adds the transmitted
  reading (*trad.*).  Many of these names are restored from Homer:
  *Laertiades* for *atrides* (372), *Nireus* for *teucer* (195),
  *Pylaeus* for *ephialtes* (240), *Poeante* for *phetonte* (217),
  *Latiis* for *laetis* (900).
