ON

THE EPITOME OF HOMER'S ILIAD AND ITS AUTHOR

TESTIMONIES AND JUDGMENTS OF AUTHORS.

I. The author of an old epigram, which in Pithou's *Epigrammata vetera* book I, p. 29, and in Burmann's *Anthologia Latina* book III, ep. 62, is entitled *De Navigatione* ("On Seafaring") (*), an author whom I suspect to be Rufius Avienus:

O shore, sweeter to me than life!  O sea!  Happy he
Who may go from time to time to my lands.
O lovely day!  On this estate I was once wont
To stir up the armed hands of the sons of Ilium.

### Notes

- * This title is not very apt for the epigram, and Burmann attests that it is not found in the Vossian manuscript or in Scaliger's papers.  The unknown author does nothing but praise his villa, situated on the seashore, from which he could look out on the ships going on the sea and now and then putting in to the shore.  Hence the thought occurs to him of comparing the discomforts and dangers of seafaring with the security of his seat on land.  Compare Martial, epigr. 51 of book X.
- 1. *Felix, Cui licet*, that is, he is to be held happy to whom it falls from time to time to withdraw to this villa of mine and to enjoy this pleasantness of the land.  Similar is that of Horace, Epod. 2: « Happy he who, far from business, etc. », and Claudian, epigr. 2: « Happy he who has spent his life in his ancestral fields ».
- 3. *O formosa dies*.  Heinsius emended *o formosa quies* ("O lovely rest") in his copy, and on Ovid, Fast. VI, 594.  And indeed this suits better the following words, in which he declares that his leisure is occupied with letters, as Pliny, Epist. I, 9: « O sweet and honourable leisure! … O sea, O shore! how much you find, how much you dictate! »  But *dies* is not unfitting either, since he looked to the words of Martial, loc. cit.: « O suns!  O rest in a tunic! »  Pithou printed with an asterisk *hoc quondam * rure solebam Iliadas armatas*, and in the margin *Iliadum*.  Sca-

### Notes

- (cont.) liger had noted in the margin *rure solebat Ilias*.  To Lindenbrog it seemed that one could read *Naidas armata sollicitare manu*.  Christ, in his Excursus on the *Villatica*, p. 270, tries *hac quondam, rure, solebam Iliade*.  Most ingeniously Heinsius emended in his copy *hoc quondam rure solebam Phyllidos hamatas sollicitare manus* ("to provoke the hooked hands of Phyllis"), though in his notes on Ovid, Fast., loc. cit., he kept *armatas*.  He thought, then, that what is described here is what Horace, Carm. I, 6, speaks of: « the battles of maidens, fierce against young men with pared nails ».  But there is no need of these subtleties.  I hold to *Iliadum*, which I printed with Pithou's assent, and I interpret it thus: the author once described on his estate the Trojan War from Homer's *Iliad*, and now, with regard to Homer's *Odyssey*, he compares the dangerous voyages of Ulysses with the life that Penelope leads on land; see the last verse.  Poets say that they themselves do what they describe as done by others, as is clear from countless passages of theirs.  Hence *Iliadum armatas manus sollicitare* is the same as to describe the battles of the Trojans: just as Propertius, II, 1, 17, says « to lead the hands of heroes to arms »; and in the *Aetna* of Lucilius, v. 45, the impious fable of the poets is said « to stir up the fires of Etna's summit with the camp of Phlegra », that is, to describe the fires of Etna as if they were arms moved against the gods.  *Iliadum* is from *Iliades*, as the Trojans are called, just as the brothers Romulus and Remus are called *Iliades* in Ovid, Fast. III, 62: and it is noteworthy that in the old editions the Epitome of the *Iliad* which we are editing is entitled *Iliadum opus*.  If *Iliadum* should please less, you could also read *Iliade*. — This reading is maintained in volume II of this work of ours, p. 314.  ED.
- 6. The vulgate editions read *victa Cupidinibus*.  But all scholars reading this poem call these words into question and try to emend them, doubtless because a "roadstead of the Cupids" seemed to them very obscure.  Pithou wants *fida Cupidinibus*; Barth, Advers. XXIII, 12, « *Hic statio est tacitis vita Cupidinibus* »; Tollius tried *placitis laeta* or *lecta Cupidinibus*.  At last the emendation of Heinsius put forward on Ovid, Fast., loc. cit., most commended itself to Burmann: « *Hic statio est tacitis dicta Cupidinibus* ».  But these men did not notice that *Cupidinibus* itself is out of place.  I boldly read: « *tacitis juncta crepidinibus* » ("joined to calm quays"). — *Statio* here is not the seat or place of the Loves and Cupids, but the roadstead of ships.  This is suggested by the preceding *sinus*, with which *statio* is also joined in the *Ciris*, v. 472: « Here a bay, here a roadstead lies open opposite Hermione ».  To the roadstead of ships belongs the *crepido*, the fortified and raised ground of the shore, to which ships are joined or bound.  Hence Virgil, Aen. X, 653: « By chance a ship stood joined to the quay of a high rock ».  — *Tacitae crepidines* are those that are free from winds and storms, as Horace, Carm. III, 29, 24, says of a bank: « and the silent bank lacks the wandering winds ».  Lest you be offended by the plural *crepidinibus*, see Manilius, IV, v. 48: « And from the quays he took the city of Carthage ».  You could also read *tuta* or *fida* for *victa*, as happens in that line of Virgil, Aeneid II, 23: « Now

### Notes

- (cont.) only a bay and a roadstead unsafe for keels ».  For the rest, at the beginning of v. 5 I read with Heinsius *Hic fons, hic lacus est*.  The poet means, namely, that on one side of his villa there is a spring and a lake, on the other side is seen partly a bay of the sea surging with waves, partly a roadstead safe for ships.
- 7. *Pervixi*, that is, I have lived my life well up to this time.  A twin passage to these verses is that of Horace, Carm. III, 29, 41: « He will live master of himself and glad, who can say each day, I have lived: yet he will not make void whatever lies behind, etc. »
- 11. *Illic divisas*, i.e. broken or scattered.  Heinsius wanted *dispersas*, or *depressas*, or *elisas*.
- 13. *Mors obsita*.  So Scaliger had emended in the margin of his papers, and printed it in his edition.  But in the Vossian manuscript and in Scaliger's papers *oblita*.  Heinsius and Opitz had conjectured *obvia*, which also pleases Burmann more.  Oudendorp, however, wanted *Illic immani mors horrida saevit hiatu* ("There dreadful death rages with monstrous jaws").  I would rather make *abdita* ("hidden") out of *oblita*, that is, death which emerges from the abyss of the sea through the gaping of the waves, like a sea monster.
- 16. In the vulgate editions it is *Hic dat perjuro*, which has greatly troubled the interpreters: for it does not appear to what *dat* is to be referred, and if a girl, who is not expressed, is to be understood, that is surely a harsh ellipsis.  In the Vossian manuscript was read *da Piuro bassa*.  Tollius wanted *nulla*, i.e. a girl gives kisses not to a forsworn man but to a faithful one.  Oudendorp conjectured *Hic dat Penelope basia multa viro*.  Burmann: *Hic dat securo basia amica viro*.  I prefer to read from the writing of the Vossian manuscript: *Hic data perjuro basia multa viro*, so that *urunt* ("burn") is supplied from the preceding verse.  As thirst burns the seafarers amid the waters, so on land the frequent kisses of girls, given so liberally to a man, though forsworn and deceitful, burn and inflame him: perhaps it would also be better to read *percaro* ("very dear") for *perjuro*.  So Horace, Carm. I, 13: « I burn, whether your white shoulders have been disfigured, etc. »
- 18. *Candida Penelope*.  He seems to have taken the epithet *candida* from Ovid, Amor. II, 18, 29: « Fair Penelope recognized the sign of Ulysses ».  And the author of the Epitome frequently uses Ovidian words and phrases.

II. LUTATIUS, or LACTANTIUS PLACIDUS, THE SCHOLIAST OF STATIUS, ON THEB. VI, 20.

Religious custom ordains that the trumpet be sounded for the elder dead, the pipe for the younger.  Persius: *hinc tuba, candelae*.  Homer says at the funeral of Hector:

Then a pyre is built, on which twice six bodies of Greeks,
And four-footed horses are added, and chariots, and trumpets,
And shields, and hollow helmets, and Argive weapons.

III. EBERHARD of Béthune, a writer of the thirteenth century, in the *Labyrinthus*, tract III, *On Versification*, a poem that Pol. Leyser in his *Historia poetarum medii aevi*, p. 825, and Fabricius, *Bibl. Lat. med. et inf. aet.* vol. II, p. 220, have published, among the Latin poets whom he reviews there and indicates were read in the schools in his age, names Homer as well after Dares (that is, Joseph of Exeter), and by it means, like Lutatius, the Latin Epitome of the *Iliad*, v. 45:

Homer marshals the Greeks against Troy, and reveals
What waves carry the ships, and the Argive trick.

IV. JO. JOVIANUS PONTANUS, BOOK I *DE SERMONE*, CHAP. 18, *DE CONTENTIOSIS*, P. 359, VOL. II OF THE WORKS, BASEL EDITION 1538.

Lorenzo Valla, in his grammatical, rhetorical and dialectical works, both wrote and was wont to dispute in such a way that he seemed not at all to wish to teach, nor does it appear that he wished so much to contend for truth and propriety, or to instruct, as to abuse, reproach and contradict the ancient writers.  So he plucked at Cicero, carped at Aristotle, and sneered at Virgil.  Indeed he openly dared to assert, concerning the author, whether called by his own name some Pindar or by another (for this is in doubt), who turned into Latin all the Homeric books of the Ili-

V. FRID. LINDENBROG IN HIS OBSERVATIONS ON STATIUS, P. 507, ON THE PASSAGE OF THE SCHOLIAST OF STATIUS CITED ABOVE.

Thus I have partly restored these verses from a manuscript which I used in Germany, in which this author was called Pindarus.  Whoever he may be after all, I do not think him so recent: indeed perhaps not more inept than Accius Labeo, who, as the scholiast of Persius says on the verse *Non est hic Ilias Acci* ("This is not the *Iliad* of Accius"), composed Homer's *Iliad* in the foulest verses.

Barth, on Stat. Theb. VI, 120, takes the words of Lindenbrog as if he were speaking here of a manuscript copy of Lutatius, in which he had found the author of the little *Iliad* called Pindar.  In this he is plainly wrong.  Lindenbrog is speaking of a manuscript of the poem itself, which bore the name of Pindar of Thebes.

VI. CASPAR BARTH, *ADVERSARIA* BOOK XX, CHAP. 12.

Pindar on the Trojan War, an ancient author and of quite good talent.

VII. THE SAME, *ADVERSARIA* BOOK LVIII, CHAP. 14.

Not the least among the authors who are not altogether ancient, but whom you could nevertheless in some way assign to their number, is he who wrote in verse a sort of compendium of the Trojan War under the name of Pindar of Thebes; I for my part think that, after the manner of the ages that departed more widely from the ancient learning, he used the title of a very ancient poet above all to win authority for his book;

untroubled meanwhile about what suited that poet, whom he knew to be known to very few of his contemporaries even by name alone.  In the same way the name of Dares was adopted by Joseph of Exeter, of Devon, whom I might perhaps consider the writer of this compendium as well, except that I see this fictitious Pindar to be by a long way more correct and of better judgment than he, avoiding the faults that the other affects, namely points depending on the sounds of words, and plays between like words and letters of like sound, which ruined the poetry even of later times, though it rested on good talent and was furnished with good learning, etc.

VIII. THE SAME ON STATIUS, THEB. VI, 120, AFTER QUOTING THE SCHOLION OF LUTATIUS.

Let him (Pindar on the Trojan War), then, both by his own worth and by the testimony of this most learned grammarian, henceforth not be despised among the writers of the later barbarism, but rather esteemed.  Nor would he be unfit to be placed last among the old little poems, if any should comb them out again after Pithou.  After other things: We do not doubt that the name of this author too will one day be brought back to light, provided he himself inscribed his own on the work.  But he is altogether older than the Walters, the Iscanii (if that one is rightly so called) and others of that stamp, as is shown, besides this testimony of Lutatius, by his style, which is not at all swollen, not at all affecting absurdities and taking them for learning, although we acknowledge that it is now interpolated.

IX. THE SAME, IN THE *ANIMADVERSIONES* ON PAULINUS OF PÉRIGUEUX, P. 40.

The author of the little *Iliad*, not to be despised among the ancient bards.  ED.

XI. PETRUS BONDAM, *VARIAE LECTIONES* BOOK II, CHAP. 4, P. 139 FF.

Who this Pseudo-Pindar was, and when he lived, I could not easily say.  That he was a Roman one might, with Barth, Advers. book LIX, ch. 15, p. 2809, not ineptly conjecture from verse 907, where he says, *nisi Aeneam servasset magnarum rector aquarum, Non clarae gentis nobis mansisset origo* ("had not the ruler of the great waters saved Aeneas, the origin of our famous race would not have remained to us").  Lindenbrog, in his Observ. on Statius, p. 507, A, rightly judges him a poet not so recent.  For Lutatius, or whoever the author of the scholia on Statius may be, already read his Epitome in his own time, as is clear from what he noted on Theb. VI, 120. …  And hence Barth, Advers. book XX, ch. 12, p. 984, rightly calls him *an ancient author and of quite good talent*, and the same, in his notes on Avianus, Fab. XI, 5, even *ingenious and learned enough*.  Other testimonies of the same on our poet will be given by the great Fabricius, *Bibl. Lat.* vol. I, p. 72. …  However this may be, our poet sometimes proposed to himself, quite happily indeed, the old poets for imitation, Ovid above all, from whose poems he often took whole half-lines, etc.
