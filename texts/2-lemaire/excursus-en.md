EXCURSUS I

ON THE EPITOME OF HOMER'S ILIAD, v. 690 ff.

… soon, the enemy repulsed (*mox hoste repulso*),

They send envoys, and urge the right hand of Achilles

To bring help to the wretched.

In these words, which are read complete and without variation in all the books, the most judicious Bondam, Var. Lect. p. 165, found great difficulties, and I think there will be no other reader who does not suspect in them either a notable error of the scribe or great drowsiness on the part of the author himself.  The words *mox hoste repulso* ("soon, the enemy repulsed") do not cohere with the rest of the narrative.  For the Danaans, who were said just before to have been put to flight by the Trojans into their camp, shut in by the rampart and hard pressed by the watches, and who, stunned by so great a danger, dared neither to refresh their spirits with food, nor to care for their bodies, nor to do anything else than wretchedly bewail their fate — whence do they so suddenly take such courage that they soon dare to repulse the enemy?  And if they succeeded in repulsing the enemy, how is it that they, as if still in the utmost danger, find it necessary to implore the right hand and help of Achilles through envoys, and still call themselves wretched?  The author must have said something quite different in this part of the verse, if indeed we believe that he did not say incongruous and contradictory things; and the distinguished Bondam, having reckoned well and compared Homer's narrative, conjectures that some indication of time lies hidden there.  For with the coming on of night Hector was prevented from pursuing the fleeing Greeks further.  Homer states this clearly, Iliad VIII, 487.  Hector meanwhile besieges the Achaeans shut within the rampart, and orders fires to be lit through the whole night and watches

to be kept most diligently, for the reason that he himself discloses to the Trojans, Iliad VIII, 510:

Lest even through the night the long-haired Achaeans
Make haste to flee over the broad back of the sea.

Thus reduced to straits and fearful for their fortunes, the leaders of the Greeks consult on their safety that same night, and at last, on Nestor's advice, decide on an embassy to Achilles, Hom. Il. IX, 165.  The envoys, coming to Achilles' tent, set forth their instructions and urge him with many words to let himself at last be won over, v. 226 ff.  He persists, resisting and inexorable, and at last forces the envoys to depart with nothing accomplished, keeping back however Phoenix, with whom he threatens to return to Greece with his ships the next day, v. 613.  So the envoys return, and when Achilles' answer has been reported Diomedes advises that they leave him aside and look to themselves, meanwhile go to bed, and, refreshed with food and sleep, be at the ships with their forces as soon as day has dawned, v. 703.  That all this was done in one night, then, is evident from Homer's narrative.  Therefore Bondam bids us read *mox nocte repulsi* for *hoste repulso*, so that the sense is that the Greeks, who had been repulsed by Hector and put to flight to their camp, sent envoys to Achilles in that night in which they were besieged by the Trojans.  This correction, probable in itself, I would readily have taken into the text, did I not believe that the place can be healed much more easily and with a lighter change.  For I think a mention of night is by no means necessary here, so that *hoste* must on that account be changed to *nocte*, since the author himself after his narrative has added a sufficiently definite description of the time in verse 698.  If night was to be mentioned in this place, it would surely not have been touched so briefly, as if in passing and with one word, as the writer would have done according to the opinion of the distinguished Bondam.  But what almost persuades me not to think that night is named here is the little word *mox* ("soon") placed before, which by itself indicates a following time, and, if *nocte* is added, seems to mean that what is narrated by the author before happened not in the earlier part of the night but in the daytime, whereas the contrary is plain from Homer's narrative and of itself.  Therefore I think a lighter cor-

rection is needed, so that we write *mox hoste repulsi legatos mittunt* ("soon, repulsed by the enemy, they send envoys"); the author perhaps had in view verse 13 of Aeneid II:

broken by war and repulsed by the fates,
The leaders of the Danaans.

That is, those Greeks who, thus repulsed by the enemy and driven within their rampart, were bewailing their fate, are soon so moved by their fear and anxiety that they send envoys to Achilles to ask for help.  That all this was done at nightfall the author soon indicates distinctly and expressly in verse 698.  Moreover the thought occurred to me that perhaps the words *hoste repulso* are sound, and that the author, who does not follow the footsteps of Homer everywhere but now and then those of others as well, perhaps had in view another hostile attack made that night and recorded by another writer; and, to say briefly what I think, that under *hoste repulso* Achilles himself is indicated, whom the Greeks soon thought they must reconcile to themselves and invite to join his forces with theirs.  Dictys of Crete, namely, records that Achilles once, when he desired to avenge his wrongs, attempted to attack the camp of the Greeks secretly, as he says, but, as the rest of the sequence of events indicates, by night.  For he so arranges that narrative as to record before it an unsuccessful battle of the Greeks with the Trojans and their return to the ships, and after it to place the fate of Dolon, sent by the Trojans to spy out the affairs of the Greeks and intercepted by Ulysses; and so he narrates the whole affair, though not in accordance with Homer's account, yet in the same connexion as Homer.  Now I shall set down the full words of Dictys of Crete from book II, chap. 37: «And now the Greeks, returned to the ships, had begun to lay down their arms and each in his usual place to refresh his body with food, when Achilles, desiring to avenge his wrongs, attempted to attack our men secretly, as they were unaware of his plan and therefore at their ease.  But when Ulysses learned the matter from the guards, who had perceived his sally in advance, running hastily round the leaders he warned and urged them with a loud voice to seize their arms and defend themselves; then he disclosed the plan and undertaking of Achilles to each: when this was known, a great shout arose,

Thus Achilles, the news of him having gone before, when all were in arms and his attempts could not proceed, returned to his tent with the business untried».  This passage from Dictys of Crete I have added as a kind of surplus, in case the vulgate reading of our Homerist could be supported and explained in this way.  Yet I do not maintain that the conjecture I draw from that passage is such that I think the reading which I gave above as emended should on that account be abandoned.

EXCURSUS II

ON THE EPITOME OF HOMER'S ILIAD, vv. 867–870.

There the Fire-Lord had engraved the axis of the world,
And the stars, and [the flowing Nymphs garlanded on every side.
He had made, too, with wondrous art the flowing Nereids,
The Ocean,] the lands, and Nereus going round them.

THE DESCRIPTION of Achilles' shield, which the author of the Latin *Iliad* begins with these verses, is, as the most judicious Bondam, Var. Lect. II, 4, p. 145, excellently observes, modelled on what Ovid said of the palace of the Sun at the beginning of book II of the *Metamorphoses*.  And I believe that not only this passage but also others of Ovid in which Achilles' shield is mentioned, as Metam. XIII, 110 and 191 ff., were present to the mind of our author.  This observation will help us not a little in examining more closely the four verses set out, which Barth, Advers. LIX, 15, p. 2808 at the end, recognizes as very faulty, and in them everything tautological (ταυτόλογα) and silly; and hence he wants to remove either the two middle ones, *Sideraque* ….. and *Fecerat*, or at least one of them, and to read thus:

And the stars, and with wondrous art the flowing Nereids.

## p. 615

PDF page 625.

And indeed Barth judges fairly here.  For first, how inept and silly is the second verse, *Sideraque et liquidas redimitas undique Nymphas*!  And although the distinguished Anton. de Rooy, Spicil. Crit. p. 99, wants to come to the rescue with an emendation and write *Nymphis redimitas* ("garlanded with Nymphs"), this phrase too is insipid and would not easily be approved by any poet.  In the third verse, *Fecerat et mira liquidas Nereidas arte*, the Nereids are also called in superfluously, the Nymphs having already been named before, and they are called *liquidas* ("flowing") with the word repeated, like the Nymphs, which easily betrays a glossator of poor talent.  Next, the same verse *Fecerat et*, etc., in the manuscripts Guelf. 1 and 2, whose authority is not slight with me, is repeated below after v. 879, where it plainly appears set in a much more fitting place, and the Nereids are named after the other sea gods.  On the other hand it is absent in that place in the Helmst. manuscript and in the editions of Torinus and Spondanus.  From this we easily gather that that verse, if indeed it is itself genuine, was written in the best and oldest manuscripts after 879, where the place is most suitable for it, but that an inept schoolman came along who rashly drew it back to adorn and illustrate the description of the world contained in verses 867 ff.  In that place it was then left by most copyists, who neither understood the verses to be suspect nor dared rashly to change or omit anything, yet in such a way that others, who found it set in its own place after v. 879 in other manuscripts, repeated it there again; among these are Guelf. 1 and 2.  That it is read in the same place where a plainly silly and wretchedly composed verse, namely 868, precedes it, is a manifest sign of interpolation.  Finally this too is to be considered, that the author at the beginning of the description of Achilles' shield wishes to name the principal parts of the world figured on it, heaven, lands, sea: and then to proceed to what is in each part of the universe, to the stars of heaven, to the gods of the sea, to the cities and inhabitants of the earth.  We see that he did this entirely after the model of Ovid, who in the description of the palace of the Sun, Metam. II, 5 ff., after naming sea, earth and heaven, then reviews one by one, in the same order, their gods and inhabitants.  Our poet names heaven in verse 867, the lands

## p. 616

PDF page 626.

and seas in v. 870.  When, therefore, the deities of the waves, the Nymphs and Nereids of the sea, are inserted between these, and that before Ocean and Nereus, who are held to be the fathers and guardians of the Nymphs, it is abundantly clear that this is done against the order and in a place not their own.  If our poet wished to name the Nymphs and Nereids, he certainly ought to have done so in the place where he recorded the gods of the sea, as we have noted is done in some manuscripts.  In these, however, from verse 877 Nereus and Ocean return with the Nymphs, and so it remains that we recognize those two verses, in which the Nymphs and Nereids are brought in, as superfluous, and cast them out as spurious, and write thus:

There the Fire-Lord had engraved the axis of the world,
And the stars, and the lands, and Nereus going round them.

That these words, which I have left after casting out the spurious ones, are the genuine words of the author may easily be shown by Ovid's verses, Metam. XIII, 291, dealing with Achilles' shield itself, which he no doubt had before his eyes as was his custom:

for he would not know the engravings of the shield,
The Ocean and the lands, and the stars with the high heaven.

And the same, Metam. II, 5, on the doors of the palace of the Sun:

… Mulciber there
Had engraved the seas encircling the midmost lands,
And the orb of the lands, and the heaven that hangs over it.

For the rest, so as not to disturb too much, by casting out those spurious verses, the number and order of the verses that stand in the vulgate and printed copies of our writer, it has sufficed me, in writing out the text itself, to separate the words that I would have deleted from the other verses by enclosing them in brackets, and thus to indicate the genuine sequence of the verses and the true reading.

EXCURSUS III

ON THE EPITOME OF HOMER'S ILIAD, vv. 894–896.

Here in the midst stood Mars, golden in his arms,
The mighty goddess Atropos around, and the others sat,
Sad Clotho and Lachesis, with blood-red hair.

The image or engraving added here, of Mars standing in the midst and the Fates sitting around Mars, seems not very ingenious nor apt enough; nor is it easy to see what reason there is for bringing it in here.  I recognize, indeed, an imitation of Virgil, a part of whose shield of Aeneas, Aen. VIII, 700 ff., the writer wished to reproduce here:

Mavors rages in the midst of the fight,
Engraved in iron, and the grim Dirae from the sky;
And Discord goes rejoicing in her rent mantle,
Whom Bellona follows with her bloody scourge.

But Virgil is speaking before of the battle of Actium, where it was fitting to set Mars appearing with Discord and the Dirae in the midst of the fight.  Nor otherwise Homer, whom Virgil himself had in view, who in Iliad XVIII, 535, says that Strife, Tumult and Fate move among men, where he has narrated the siege of a city and a battle joined with an enemy suddenly attacking:

And among them Strife and Tumult mingled, and deadly Fate.

Hesiod has similar things in the *Shield of Heracles*, v. 154 ff.  Our poet brings in Mavors in the midst and the Fates sitting around, after he had narrated the labours of farmers, reapers, vine-dressers and shepherds, among whom there is hardly a place for Mars with his companions.  But nevertheless the author, lest we leave him quite undefended

## p. 618

PDF page 628.

or insufficiently excused, seems in devising this image to have followed another reason than Homer and Maro, and deliberately to have joined Mars with the Fates, not, as they did, with Discord, Fate and the Dirae.  These deities are above all companions and guardians of wars, and are named by Homer and Virgil because they wish to describe only the calamity of wars.  Κήρ, used so often by Homer, where he speaks of battles, primarily denotes the fate of men slain in war; and Virgil, where he calls up the grim Dirae from the sky in the passage cited, rendered by that name, in the judgment of the illustrious Heyne on Virgil, loc. cit., the Κῆρες of Hesiod.  The Parcae, whom our Homerist wished to bring in here, are said to have in their power the natural death of men and every fate.  These he joins to Mars to signify the twofold kind of death by which men are wont to perish, the one violent and premature in war, the other natural, which befalls each by his own fate: as Ovid joined them, Trist. book I, 2, 53:

It is something, falling by one's own fate or by the sword,
To lay one's body, dying, on solid ground.

He did not wish, then, to add the Dirae and Discord and Fate as companions to Mars, because he did not wish to describe the particular dangers and fates of war, but to sketch the end that every life of men and the whole race would have.  After he had enumerated, then, the various kinds of living, as of the citizens living in cities, men and women, v. 882 ff., of those tilling the fields, reapers, vine-dressers, shepherds, v. 890 ff., he adds finally that Mars stands in the midst of them with the Fates, that is, that death, to be met either by war or by some other fate, at last hangs over all these; and he seems even to have had before his eyes the verses of Virgil in which he embraces the fates of human life, Georg. book III, 66:

Every best day of life for wretched mortals
Is the first to flee: diseases come on, and gloomy old age,
And toil, and the harshness of cruel death snatches them away.

EXCURSUS IV

ON THE EPITOME OF HOMER'S ILIAD, vv. 919–921.

which straightway on every side with all his waters
Is to be hindered (*Impediatur*); but headlong in a vast whirlpool
He rolls, and hinders the hero with his torrent waves.

THESE words, which we have restored from the vulgate editions, *qui protinus undique totis Impediatur aquis*, seem to be badly affected and to cohere badly with those next to them: *sed vasto gurgite praeceps volvitur*, etc.  For the relative *qui protinus*, by the sequence of the sentence, refers to the river Xanthus, which was compelled to rise and straightway rises; but *Impediatur*, which follows, can be understood only of Achilles, who is to be hindered by the waters; and again the following words *sed praeceps volvitur* deal with Xanthus.  So the word *Impediatur*, which tears the sentence apart, seems to need changing, especially since *impedit* recurs soon after in the following verse.  And the writer of the Helmst. manuscript seems already to have felt the fault in it, who changed that word to *Expediatur* and wrote *Expediatur aquis et vasto*, etc.  For this can be understood of the river, in the sense that it unfolds its waters.  But since there follow not verbs in the subjunctive mood, such as the copula *et* after *expediatur* demanded, but *volvitur* and *impedit*, it is evident from this that that *Expediatur* is a correction attempted by the copyist that is by no means sufficient, and that in place of that word something else is required which fully restores the sense and the connexion of the sentence.  In short, I think it should certainly be written thus:

which (Xanthus) straightway on every side, with all his waters
Impetuous (*Impetuosus*), and headlong in a vast whirlpool
Rolls, and hinders the hero with his torrent waves.

PDF page 630.

The word *impetuosus* is not unworthy of the age of our writer, and, if it lacks the usage of better writers, it can certainly stand on the authority of this author of ours.  And an *impetus* is fittingly and expressively attributed to a swelling river, as in this of Ovid, Met. III, 79:

Now with a vast onrush (*impete*), like a river roused by rains,
He is borne along.

And in book XI, 530: « Rising more vastly, the onrush of the tenth wave rushes on ».  If you spurn the word *impetuosus* as one to be rejected and little approved, put in its place *imperiosus* ("imperious"), as Horace said *imperiosum aequor* ("the imperious sea"), Carm. I, 14, 8.  I myself, however, prefer the former.
