XIV.

PROOEMIUM

ON

THE LATIN EPITOME OF THE ILIAD

AND ITS AUTHOR.

WHERE AT THE SAME TIME

THE OLDER

LATIN HOMERISTS

WITH THEIR FRAGMENTS

ARE REVIEWED.

THE poem which we now publish, embracing the sum of Homer's *Iliad*, has experienced a varied fortune at different times, and fates so unkind, that we may affirm that no poem published in our collection needed an edition as much as this one.  Not unknown to the ancients nor despised, it was held in especial honour in later centuries and in the age which we call the Middle, which loved meagre compendia of every art and books condensed into shorter form rather than the full and copious writers, and it was read and explained in the public schools, as is proved both by some testimonies of writers and by the frequent manuscripts still found in libraries.  But from this use in the schools it contracted a great stain and much squalor, being wretchedly disfigured by the errors and interpolations of foolish schoolmasters;

PDF page 466.

and after better literature and other excellent writers had begun to be widely published by means of printing, this work, on the contrary, covered with filth, lost its value and lay utterly neglected: indeed in the century next after the revival of letters we know that it was printed hardly three or four times, nor treated so kindly by any editor as to be freed from the immense mass of faults.  After Jo. Spondanus, who had this Epitome, together with Dares the Phrygian, published among the works of Homer at Basel in 1583 and 1606, in a repeated but, as far as I know, not different edition, no one was found in so long an interval down to our own times who wished, I will not say to emend and polish it, but to render it from the old copies and bring it more into use among the learned.  Yet this writer did not deserve so unkind a fortune, and some of the learned, recognizing his talent and elegance, praised him in no ordinary measure; among whom indeed Lorenzo Valla ineptly preferred him to Virgil himself: but above all Caspar Barth, in his *Adversaria* and his commentaries on Statius, emended him here and there and adorned him with choice praises.  After him very few were found who touched him.  At last in our age the study of the Dutch scholars has turned to this poet, who, in the books of critical observations they published, have striven to correct many of his passages and restore them to their brilliance; of their work we shall speak more exactly below.  Indeed to those who read that poem attentively the author appears endowed with good talent and judgment, a stranger to the faults of the writers of the later age, such as bombast and the affectation of points, and praiseworthy for the purity and elegance of his Latin and for his quite happy imitation of the older poets.  He so translates and condenses Homer as to leave himself much freedom, and he takes from the *Iliad* hardly anything but the bare sequence of events, while the rest, which pertains to the description and embellishment of things, he either invents by his own talent or adapts from the thoughts and phrases of the chief Roman poets, Virgil and Ovid; which

indeed he does so zealously and diligently that he often seems to desert Homer for the sake of transferring there some famous passage of an old poet.  His language does bear marks of declining Latinity and of the later age in which he wrote: he uses now and then common and less choice words, from which the nobler poets are wont to abstain; but he also has some rather remarkable little gems, and graces of the choicest Latinity, which easily reveal an elegant talent practised in reading the most excellent writers.  These I have taken care to show in my notes all the more diligently, the more I wished to set out the gifts of that poet and to vindicate him from the contempt of very many, under which he has seemed to labour until now.

The work itself is variously entitled in printed and written books.  Anciently it seems to have been called simply *Homerus*.  For so Lutatius, the scholiast of Statius, and some writers of the Middle Age cite it, and so it is entitled in the older manuscripts.  Other manuscripts, however, entitle it *Homerus de bello Trojano* ("Homer on the Trojan War"), or *de destructione Trojae* ("on the destruction of Troy"), with little aptness or wisdom.  Some printed editions call it *Homeri Iliadum opus* (1), or the *Epitome and sum of the whole Iliad of Homer*, and add the inept name of Pindar of Thebes as the author, which some written manuscripts also keep.  But the more absurd and intolerable that name is, so that it seems it should be hissed off with every care, the more we seem to be admonished to strive to search out the true age of the poem we are publishing and, if possible, the name of its author.  If we succeed in showing this with probable reasoning, then it will also be helpful to conjecture whence the name of Pindar as the author came.

But now halt your step, and set an end to the labour,

Indeed I think it did not happen by chance that the author of the Epitome used so many images drawn from seafaring and named the keel, the oars, the shores, the harbour, the ropes, and Homer himself, whom he had traversed, as an open sea; unless from the place and time and other circumstances in which the author was then engaged when he was writing, he had occasion to think much about seafaring.  And this very thing is sufficiently shown by the epigram cited.  His villa, the author says, lying very close to the sea, had on one side all the delights of the country, on the other a harbour and roadstead for ships, from which he was wont constantly to look out on ships, some entering the harbour, others tossed on the high sea.  And amid these views, he says too, he once wrote an *Iliad*.  And so we seem safely to conclude from this that the little *Iliad* which we have, and which the author compares in many words to a voyage, is the very one which is said there to have been written out in the seaside villa, and therefore that the epigram *On Seafaring* and the Homeric Epitome have the same author.  What has been said so far we shall prove further in our notes on the epigram itself, which we shall give in full among the testimonies of the ancients on the Epitome of Homer. But let no one wonder that such a comparison among these three poems can be made only with the greatest difficulty, indeed can hardly have a place.  In the *Aratea*, as in the *Description of the World*, a singular subject and a peculiar kind of narration prevail, common to no other poet. In the *Iliad*, on the contrary, where the history of a war was to be described, the subject is a more common one among poets, and the discourse is continuous and unbroken, wonderfully different from that astronomical and geographical poem, which consist of a review of single parts.  In those, a modest poem had to be translated and expanded; in the *Iliad* a vast work had to be condensed into shorter form.  In writing those, imitation of hardly any poet had a place; in this, he could set before himself several to imitate, because of the common subject. So far, indeed, by the reasons adduced, I have thought that the Homeric poem should be claimed for Festus Avienus, not because I thought him the sole true and certain author, but because I thought no one else among those known more certain. X. There remains, among the contemporaries of Ovid, the poet who translated Homer's *Odyssey*.  For this he means, loc. cit., verse 27:

And he who turned the Maeonian *Phaeacis*, and you too,

Rufus, the one player on Pindar's lyre.

It may seem doubtful whether by *Phaeacis* the whole *Odyssey* of Homer is designated, or a part of it, namely the arrival of Ulysses among the Phaeacians and at the palace of Alcinous, and the other things done and narrated there.  For Aelian, *Var. hist.* XIII, 14, records that Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey* were once divided into various parts, and each marked with its own names and titles.  Hence that part could be called by a particular name *Phaeacis* or *Phaeacia*, just as Aelian reports it was called Ἀλκινόου ἀπόλογοι ("the tales told to Alcinous").  Yet I would rather understand the name used by the poet, and derived from the chief part of the *Odyssey*, of the whole, especially since it is said to have been translated by the poet from Homer, a labour which can hardly seem to have been undertaken on only some part of the work.  The name of the poet who translated the *Odyssey* Naso declares in another place, the 12th elegy of the fourth book *ex Ponto*, v. 27, writing to Tuticanus:

When your Pierides had thoroughly taught you

To compose a *Phaeacis* worthy of Maeonian pages.

In which place, since he says that the *Phaeacis* was composed by Tuticanus, we understand that the version of the *Odyssey* was not made word for word, but varied by the poet's talent, and drawn from the spring of Homer as if it were his own poem.

XVII. Among the works of Ausonius survive *Periochae* of the individual books of Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, from which it is not difficult to gather that Ausonius himself had earlier turned the books of Homer into Latin verse, or had in his hands the books translated by someone else, from which he made these *Periochae*.  For we have asserted above, moved by clear indications, that they seem by no means to have been condensed into a summary from the Greek of Homer, but from a Latin and interpolated Homer; and the author himself prefixes to each *Periocha* Latin verses indicating the beginning of the book, which attest a translation of Homer made earlier.  Whether this is to be attributed to Ausonius himself or to another, I for my part do not dare to define.  That these *Periochae* agree with the Latin Epitome of the *Iliad* which we are publishing we have said elsewhere, and so we judge, not rashly, that both flowed from one source.
