Page references ("p. XIII") are to the pages of the edition, which are
the `## p. N` headings of the files.  Verse numbers are those of The
Latin Library ([ilias.txt](ilias.txt)), which Baehrens, Plessis and
Vollmer share for the passages cited here; where Wernsdorf's numbering
differs it is added in brackets (see [concordance.md](concordance.md)).
The four editors span 130 years and often disagree; their views are
reported as theirs, not as settled fact.

## 1. The poem

The poem is a Latin hexameter epitome of Homer's *Iliad* in 1,070
verses (Wernsdorf counts 1,075).  All four editors agree on its main
features: it takes from Homer little more than the sequence of events;
its diction and much of its phrasing come from Virgil and Ovid; it was
read in schools through the Middle Ages; and it is the only Latin
version of Homer from antiquity to survive complete (Plessis, pp. I,
LI).  They disagree about nearly everything else: the author's name,
his date, his social position, his purpose and the worth of his work.

## 2. Title and author

### Titles in the manuscripts

The oldest witnesses call the poem simply *Homerus*.  Ermenricus of
Ellwangen, quoting verse 7 about 850, says only "as in Homer in the
*Iliad*"; the library catalogues of the 9th to 11th centuries list an
*Omerus* (Baehrens, p. 4; Plessis, p. XLIX).  The manuscripts have
*incipit liber homeri* (Erfurt), *incipit liber omeri* (Leiden),
*incipit Homerus* (Wolfenbüttel), *de bello troyano homerus* (Munich
19462), *LIBER HOMERI POETAE* (Valenciennes) (Plessis, p. XLIX;
Vollmer, p. V).  Later manuscripts and the printed editions add titles
such as *Homerus de bello Trojano*, *de destructione Trojae*, *Homeri
Iliadum opus* and *Epitome ac summa universae Iliados Homeri*
(Wernsdorf, p. 457).

### The acrostics: ITALICUS SCRIPSIT

F. Bücheler (*Rhein. Mus.* 35, 1880) noticed that the initial letters
of verses 1–8 and 1063–1070 almost spell a name and a verb.  In the
manuscripts they read ITALICES and SCQIPSIT; two verses have to be
emended.

- **Verse 7.**  Baehrens restored the acrostic by moving *Ut primum*
  to the head of the verse and removing the transmitted *Ex quo*.
  Plessis objected that *Ex quo* renders *Iliad* 1.6 (ἐξ οὗ δὴ τὰ
  πρῶτα) and must stay.  Louis Havet found another way through
  Ermenricus, whose quotation begins *Protulerunt* and has *turmas*
  where the manuscripts have *pugnas*.  *Protulerunt* is a marginal
  gloss, *pro tulerunt* ("for *tulerunt*"), written in an old copy to
  explain a rarer word of the same meaning, which it then displaced; *pugnas*
  is a fabrication, and *turmas* points to the true reading.  The
  verse is to be read *Volverunt … turbas*, giving the V of ITALICVS
  (Plessis, pp. V–VI).
- **Verse 1065.**  The manuscripts begin with Q where R is needed.
  Baehrens moved *remis* from the end to the beginning; Plessis first
  conjectured *Raptim*, and Havet proposed *Raris* (Plessis, p. VI).
- **Hertz** kept *Ex quo* and rearranged verse 9 to read ITALICE SILI,
  making the poem a dedication to Silius Italicus.  Plessis answers
  that the closing SCRIPSIT requires a nominative in the opening
  acrostic, not a vocative (p. VI).

Baehrens was the first editor to put the name Italicus on the poem
(Plessis, p. V).  After Havet's emendations Plessis considers the name
beyond doubt.

### Baebius Italicus

In 1890 H. Schenkl published the title of a 15th-century paper
manuscript in Vienna (lat. 3509): *Bebii Italici poetae clarissimi
epithome in quatuor viginti libros homeri iliados*.  Nobody accepted
it until Vollmer, who argued that a scribe who could give the correct
cognomen without having noticed the acrostic must have drawn both
names from a good source, a lost manuscript of the poem.  He therefore
calls the poet Baebius Italicus (p. IV, note 1; p. VI).

### Was it Silius Italicus?

Bücheler identified the poet with Silius Italicus, author of the
*Punica*, and Munk followed him; Doering's dissertation (Strasbourg,
1884) argued the case at length from similarities of metre and
phrasing.

- **Baehrens** rejects the identification: Martial (7.63) shows that
  Silius began to write poetry only after Nero's death, while the poem
  was written before it (p. 3).
- **Plessis** thinks the chronology possible (Silius lived c. 25–101)
  and does not accept that Martial excludes youthful verse; but he
  finds no positive evidence, notes that no ancient source ascribes
  such a work to Silius, and dismisses Doering's parallels (*vulgus
  ruit undique* against *ruit undique vulgus*) as the common stock of
  Roman poets.  He takes the poet for an otherwise unknown schoolmaster
  and quotes L. Mueller: "there is also a certain virtue in not
  knowing" (pp. XV–XVII).

### Wernsdorf's Avienus

Wernsdorf, writing before the acrostics were known, assumed an
anonymous poem and looked for an author.  He first proposed Pentadius,
then withdrew it, because Pentadius's artificial verse does not suit
so plain a poem and his name cannot lie under the late title
*Pindarus* (p. 468).  His final candidate was Rufius Festus Avienus,
on these grounds (pp. 457–468):

- The poet was a Roman: verse 902 [907] speaks of "the origin of our
  race" (*nobis*), and he imitates Roman poets everywhere.
- He was a wealthy noble writing at leisure in a seaside villa.  An
  epigram *De navigatione* in the *Anthologia Latina*, which Wernsdorf
  ascribes to Avienus, recalls how "on this estate I was once wont to
  stir up the armed hands of the sons of Ilium" (reading *Iliadum*),
  and ends with Ulysses and Penelope.  The seafaring images of the
  poem's close (the keel, the oars, the shore, verses 1063 ff.) seemed
  to him to fit such a villa.  The epigram, with his notes and
  emendations, heads the [testimonia](2-lemaire/testimonia-en.md).
- Avienus wrote many poems, as his inscription to the goddess Nortia
  says, and his versions of Aratus and Dionysius treat their Greek
  models with the same freedom as the epitome treats Homer.  Servius
  reports that he turned Virgil and Livy into iambics, which Wernsdorf
  takes to mean condensed versions.
- The epilogues of the *Periegesis* and of the epitome both invoke
  Phoebus and the Muses, and the poet was a pagan, as Avienus was.

The Lemaire editor adds that Wernsdorf later withdrew the
identification of the poet Avienus with the proconsul Festus (p. 465,
note).  Plessis refutes the whole hypothesis from metre (see
[Date](#3-date)), and rejects the picture of a wealthy amateur, which
would suit Silius as well: for him the poet was a humble schoolmaster
(p. XVI).

### The false name Pindarus

From the Middle Ages to the 18th century the poem went under the name
of Pindarus Thebanus.  The evidence gathered by the editors:

- Benzo of Alba, about 1087, lists *Pindarus seu Homerus* among Roman
  writers; Hugo von Trimberg (13th century) says that "the philosopher
  Pindar is said to have translated" Homer for Latin teachers
  (Baehrens, p. 4; Plessis, p. XLIX).
- Vatican Reginensis 1708 (13th–14th century) begins *Incipit liber
  pindari translatoris homeri* and ends with the couplet *Pindarus hunc
  librum fecit sectatus homerum; / Grecus homerus erat, sed pindarus
  ipse latinus*.
- Vatican Palatinus 1611 (late 14th century) has *Pandarus hunc secum
  trans pontum vexit homerum*; the Santenianus has *Pindarus* in the
  same line, and Paris lat. 8413 (1403) a longer set of such verses.
- Vossius reports a Padua manuscript entitled *Pindarus Thebanus de
  destructione Trojae*, dated 1390 in the passage of Vossius printed in
  the testimonia (p. 514) but 1300 in Wernsdorf's prooemium (p. 469).
  The printed editions from 1492 onwards use the name.

The explanations offered:

- **Wernsdorf** (pp. 469–472) dates the name to the 14th century,
  since Eberhard of Béthune in the 13th knows only Homer.  He suggests
  either an Alexandrian grammarian Ptolemaeus Pindarion, who wrote on
  Homer, confused by a later reader with the Theban poet; or a title
  *Pandarus*, after the Trojan archer, prefixed by a "smatterer" in the
  manner of Dictys, Dares and Albert of Stade's *Troilus*, and later
  changed by scribes into the better-known *Pindarus*.  He notes that
  the second Wolfenbüttel manuscript gives *Pindarus* as a variant of
  *Pandarus* at two places in the poem.
- **Baehrens** (p. 4 and notes) answers that *Pindarus* is attested
  far earlier than *Pandarus*, which is a scribe's error or invention,
  and, with L. Mueller, puts the name down solely to the ignorance of
  monks who, knowing that Homer was Greek, asked who had put him into
  Latin.
- **Plessis** (pp. XLVIII–L) follows Baehrens in restoring *Pindarus*
  for *Pandarus*, and notes that Benzo shows the name current in the
  11th century, earlier than Wernsdorf's date.  Of Wernsdorf's two
  explanations he rejects Pandarus, who plays too small a part in the
  poem to give it its title, and finds Pindarion "something better";
  but the ignorance of a monk or scribe would be explanation enough.
  The "sea" of the subscriptions is simply the sea between Greece and
  Italy, and *Thebanus* is a crude error on which all scholars agree.
- **Weytingh** (1809), from the subscription of the Santenianus
  (*Pindarus hunc secum trans pontum vexit Homerum*), inferred that
  the poet was "if not a contemporary, at least a fellow countryman" of
  Joseph of Exeter, an English monk of the 11th or 12th century.
  Plessis, with Mueller, answers that the sea meant is the one between
  Brundisium and Dyrrachium, not between Dover and Calais, and that the
  poem is quoted by Ermenricus about 850 (pp. VII–VIII).

## 3. Date

### Wernsdorf: the fourth century

Wernsdorf placed the poem between Constantine and the Theodosian age
(pp. 465–466).  He saw in its language "marks of declining Latinity",
and in its very form the spirit of an age of breviaries and epitomes
(Eutropius, Aurelius Victor, Vegetius).  He also used verse 902 [907]:
the poet would not have praised the descent of the Caesars from Aeneas
after the Empire had fallen, so the poem cannot be later than the
Theodosian age.  Plessis summarizes the view as "the beginning of the fourth
century or the end of the third" (p. VIII).

### Lachmann, Mueller, Baehrens and Plessis: before Nero's death

Lachmann first placed the poem in the early Empire; L. Mueller (1857)
and Baehrens (p. 3) followed.  Plessis develops the case in detail
(pp. VIII–XIV):

- **Metre.**  The poet never elides an iambic word or a cretic, never
  ends a verse with a non-enclitic monosyllable after a polysyllable,
  and uses a polysyllabic ending only once, for a Greek name
  (*Iphidamanta*, 750).  He does not shorten final *-o* and makes no
  false quantities in Greek names.  Avienus breaks every one of these
  rules (Plessis lists examples from the *Periegesis* and *Ora
  maritima*), as do Pentadius, Ausonius and Prudentius.  The metre is
  that of the best poets of the first century.
- **Language.**  The only suspicious word, *rimabant* (456), is an
  old active form found in Pacuvius and perhaps Phaedrus; Plessis
  suspects the poet took it from an earlier Latin translation.
- **Terminus post quem.**  The poet borrows heavily from the *Aeneid*
  and the *Metamorphoses*, so he wrote after they had long been in
  circulation.
- **Terminus ante quem.**  Verses 899–902 say that Neptune saved Aeneas
  so that he might "send the Augustan race up to the bright stars",
  and verses 236 and 483 call Aeneas the offspring of Venus.  Such
  praise of the Julian line makes sense only while it ruled, and would
  have been unwelcome to an emperor of another house after Nero.

Plessis concludes that the poem was written before AD 68, probably in
the first half of the century and perhaps, with Lachmann, before the
death of Tiberius: the poet was "a contemporary of Phaedrus rather
than of Lucan" (p. XIV).  Baehrens and Vollmer accept a date before
Nero's death.

## 4. Latin Homer before and after the poem

Both Wernsdorf (pp. 474–498) and Plessis (pp. XIX–XXIX) review the
earlier Latin versions of Homer, to set the epitome in context.  They
agree on the names but differ on several attributions.

| Translator | Work | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Livius Andronicus | *Odyssey* in Saturnians | A school book in Horace's day.  Plessis defends it against its critics, quoting fragments restored by Havet. |
| Naevius | *Cypria Ilias* | So Wernsdorf; Plessis, after Huschke, gives the fragments to Ninnius Crassus and calls the Naevian *Cypria* a fiction of Estienne, Scaliger and Heyne. |
| Cn. Matius | *Iliad* in hexameters | Author also of *Mimiambi*.  Both editors hold him older than Caesar's friend C. Matius.  Fragments in Varro, Gellius, Charisius, Diomedes and Priscian; Plessis judges the version free and not without brilliance. |
| Ninnius Crassus | *Iliad* in hexameters | Three fragments.  Wernsdorf doubts whether the name is a corruption of Matius. |
| Cicero | Single passages | Seven from the *Iliad* and one from the *Odyssey* (the Sirens) in his works, two more in Augustine.  Plessis finds the Sirens passage pleasing, though far from Homer. |
| Attius Labeo | Literal *Iliad* and *Odyssey* | Mocked by Persius and his scholiast; one verse survives (*Crudum manduces Priamum Priamique pisinnos*).  Bergk's view that the epitome is Labeo's work is rejected by Haupt and Plessis. |
| Poets in Ovid, *Pont.* 4.16 | Trojan subjects | Macer *Iliacus* (the events before and after the *Iliad*), Camerinus, Largus (Antenor), Lupus (the return of Menelaus), Tuticanus (a *Phaeacis*).  Plessis excludes them, since they wrote original poems, not translations. |

Wernsdorf goes further: Nero's *Troica* and *Capture of Troy*, Lucan's
*Iliacon*, Petronius's *Troiae Halosis* (which he prints as an
appendix to the epitome), Statius's *Achilleid*, the Homeric verses in
Latin quoted by Chalcidius, the *Periochae* ascribed to Ausonius, and,
in the Middle Ages, Joseph of Exeter and Albert of Stade.  He also
notes the Homeristae who recited Homer, sometimes in Latin, at Roman
banquets (Petronius; Juvenal 11.177).

## 5. Sources and method

### Did the poet read Homer?

- **Wernsdorf** (pp. 472–473) suspected that the poet worked not from
  the Greek but from an older, interpolated Latin translation already
  adapted to Virgil.  His evidence is the *Periochae* of the *Iliad*
  ascribed to Ausonius, which share non-Homeric details with the
  epitome (Hector dragged three times round the walls; prayers to
  Achilles for the return of Hector's body) and have others that the
  epitome cannot have supplied.  Both, he concludes, go back to a
  fuller Latin *Iliad*.
- **Plessis** (p. XXXI) finds the question undecidable, but suspects
  that the poet, busy with teaching, wrote piece by piece and largely
  from memory, following the Latin poets more than the Greek.  He
  points to words repeated at short intervals (*implicuit* 12,
  *implicitus* 14; *flevit* 14, *fletus* 18, *fletibus* 22).
- **Vollmer** printed in his margin the lines of the *Iliad* that each
  verse follows, "so that it becomes clear in which parts he displayed
  his own art and invention" (p. VIII).

### Departures from Homer

Plessis lists the main ones (pp. XXXI–XXXII):

| Verse | Epitome | *Iliad* |
|---|---|---|
| 78 | Athena restrains Achilles after Briseis is taken | before (1.188 ff.) |
| 151 | The speech is Nestor's | Odysseus's (2.300) |
| 372 | Agamemnon kills Democoon | Odysseus (4.499) |
| 461 | Diomedes' stone is too heavy for twelve young men | for two (5.303) |
| 839 | Nestor's son carries Patroclus's body to the camp | Antilochus only reports the death; Menelaus and Meriones carry the body (17.685, 717–722) |
| 855 | Achilles asks Thetis for arms | She offers them unasked (18.73) |
| 857 | Vulcan's forge is in Etna | Not in Homer |
| 1006 | Hector is dragged round the walls of Troy | round Patroclus's tomb (24.16) |

Etna and the dragging round the walls come from the *Aeneid*, which
shows, for Plessis, that Virgil rather than Homer was the poet's guide.
His index of names and subjects is designed to show "at once in what
things Italicus departed from Homer" (preface, p. II).

### Imitation of Virgil and Ovid

All four editors stress this.  Wernsdorf says the poet "often seems to
desert Homer for the sake of transferring there some famous passage
of an old poet" (pp. 456–457), and his commentary sets the parallels
beside the text; van Kooten did the same (Plessis, p. XLVI).  Plessis
gives a selection (pp. XXXIII–XXXIV):

| Verse | Epitome | Model |
|---|---|---|
| 58 | *exarsit subito violentia regis* | Virgil, *Aen.* 11.376 *exarsit dictis violentia Turni* |
| 96 | *Dixit; at illa leves caeli delapsa per oras* | Repeated whole from *Aen.* 11.595 |
| 236 | *Veneris certissima proles* | *Aen.* 6.322 *deum certissima proles* |
| 311 | *Ultimus ille dies Paridis foret* | *Aen.* 9.758 *Ultimus ille dies bello gentique fuisset* |
| 655–657 | Jupiter weighs the fates in *aequato pondere lances* | *Aen.* 12.723 |
| 298 | *Non aliter fortes … tauri* | Ovid, *Met.* 9.46 |
| 821 | *Nam licet ipse suis Mavors te protegat armis* | *Met.* 8.394 |
| 111 | *Nox erat et toto fulgebant sidera mundo* | Horace, *Epod.* 15.1 |
| 857 | *Aetnaeos calidis fornacibus ignes* | Lucretius 6.681 |

Wernsdorf's [excursus](2-lemaire/excursus-en.md) show how the models
bear on the text.  The opening of the shield of Achilles (862–865
[867–870]) follows Ovid's palace of the Sun (*Met.* 2.5 ff.), and he
brackets the two middle verses about Nymphs and Nereids as an
interpolation that breaks Ovid's order of heaven, earth and sea
(Excursus II).  Mars and the Fates on the shield (889–891 [894–896])
recall Virgil's shield of Aeneas (*Aen.* 8.700 ff.), but join war with
natural death to show the end of every human life (Excursus III).
The other two excursus propose emendations where the transmitted text
does not fit the sense: *mox hoste repulsi* for *repulso* at 688 [690], where the
Greeks, beaten back, send envoys to Achilles (Excursus I), and
*Impetuosus* for *Impediatur* at 915 [920], of the river Xanthus
(Excursus IV).

## 6. The book division

The manuscripts divide the poem into 24 books after the *Iliad*, but
not always at the same places.  Wernsdorf numbered the books himself by
comparison with Homer (p. 498).  Vollmer rejects the division: it
"does not always agree with Homer", and a poem of about a thousand
verses was not divided by its author, but by "some petty schoolmaster"
at the end of the Empire, when Greek was still read in Italy.  He
leaves the book notes of the manuscripts out of his apparatus, gives a
list of the books in a footnote, and puts the *Iliad* line numbers in
the margin instead (pp. VII–VIII).  His list, with the probable
misprint at books 16–18 discussed in [README.md](README.md#book-divisions):

## 7. Character, purpose and worth

### Judgements

- **Wernsdorf** is the most favourable.  He finds "good talent and
  judgment", purity and elegance of Latin, freedom from bombast and
  point-making, and "happy imitation" of the older poets, though with
  some common words of a later age (pp. 456–457).  Yet he would not
  call the poet epic: too thin in the main narrative and too copious
  in accessory episodes (p. 499).
- **Earlier opinion**, collected in the testimonia: Lorenzo Valla
  preferred the poem to Virgil (reported by Pontano); Lindenbrog
  thought it no worse than Labeo's; Barth called the author "ancient
  and of quite good talent" and older than the medieval poets;
  Bondam, following Barth, inferred from verse 902 [907] that he was a
  Roman.
- **Plessis** is the most critical (pp. XXXV–XXXVII).  The poem
  narrates "battles and slaughters, attacks and flights" and nothing
  that touches the heart.  Achilles is a harsh soldier who never
  speaks of his own death; Phoenix is not named; Nestor and Odysseus
  have little part; Andromache appears twice, Hecuba in "frigid
  verses"; Helen on the wall is left out; the gods seldom intervene and
  never take human shape.  Chryses' prayer, a few weighty words in Homer
  (1.37 ff.), takes twelve verses (32–43), "for commonplaces delight Italicus above all".
- **Vollmer** says nothing about literary merit; his preface is
  concerned with the manuscripts.

### Purpose

Baehrens (p. 3) suggests the poem may have been written for schools,
since Lactantius Placidus already knew it there.  Plessis builds his
defence on this (pp. XXXVII–XXXIX).  Roman boys read Homer in Greek
with the help of a Latin version; when Livius Andronicus's *Odyssey*
fell out of use after Horace, a master could write a short Latin
summary to fix the story in pupils' memories.  Judged as that, the
poem is "a useful work of a modest man", with pure language and
metre, and it held its place in schools until, perhaps, Ausonius's
*Periochae* replaced it.  In the Middle Ages it was all that most
readers knew of the *Iliad* (p. XXXIX).  Wernsdorf, by contrast,
thought of a noble's leisure (see [Author](#wernsdorfs-avienus)) and
of an age that "wished to devour even great Homer in an epitome, as if
in a nutshell" (p. 499).

## 8. Transmission

### Antiquity and the Middle Ages

- **Lactantius Placidus**, the commentator on Statius, quotes three
  verses on Hector's funeral (1048 ff.) as Homer's (testimonia, p.
  511; Baehrens, p. 3).  Wernsdorf dated him to the 6th century and
  took him for the first witness.  M. Schmidt's claim to find traces
  in Hyginus is doubted by Baehrens.
- **Ermenricus of Ellwangen** quotes verse 7 in his letter to Grimald
  (850–855), with readings that preserve traces of the true text
  (Baehrens, p. 3; Plessis, p. VI).
- **The *Gesta Berengarii*** (10th century) borrow many verses; Baehrens
  prints the important ones in his apparatus, and notes that they
  already show the corruptions of the extant manuscripts (p. 4).
- **Eberhard of Béthune**, *Labyrinthus* 3.45 f. (13th century), lists
  "Homer" among school authors (testimonia, p. 511).  Albert of Stade
  and a grammarian published by Mai also used the poem (Baehrens,
  p. 5).

Heavy use in schools corrupted the text and wore out the old copies,
"by a fate very like that of Ovid's *Metamorphoses*" (Baehrens, p. 5).
Plessis blames the scribes of the 11th to 13th centuries for the
"monsters of Greek names", transpositions and spurious verses.  The
Italian humanists, who might have healed them, neglected the poem,
and it was then little studied in the German schools either (p. XLV).

### The manuscripts

Each editor used more manuscripts than the last and judged them
differently.

- **Wernsdorf** (pp. 499–500) had four late books: two from
  Wolfenbüttel (one of the 13th century, lacking 180–628; one of the
  late 15th), one from Helmstedt (14th century, from verse 266) and
  one from Annaberg, heavily glossed, with the title *Homerus grecus
  per Pindarum insignem oratorem … traductus*.  He based his text on
  the Basel edition of 1541.
- **Baehrens** (pp. 5–7) chose eight: B (London), E (Erfurt), F
  (Florence), G (Wolfenbüttel), L (Leiden), M and N (Munich) and V
  (Venice).  He grouped them in three families, EL perhaps French, MN
  German and FV Italian, with BG mixed, and ranked E and L above the
  rest.  He dismissed the later manuscripts, including Brussels 2718,
  as interpolated.
- **Plessis** (pp. XLI–XLV) reproduced much of Baehrens's apparatus
  without seeing any manuscript himself.  He added the Burmannianus
  (his R), which ends at verse 644 and whose scribe copied his exemplar
  letter by letter, the Vossianus (his A), the Santenianus (S), the
  Virgilianus (I) and a second Guelferbytanus (Y), and readings of
  three more collated for him: Brussels 2718 (T), Paris lat. 8413 (C,
  dated 1403) and Paris lat. 14909 (D).  His sixteen sigla partly
  clash with Vollmer's.
- **Vollmer** (pp. IV–VII, X) collated fourteen from photographs or at
  first hand, and says that Baehrens "proceeded more carelessly than
  usual", reporting much of M and N wrongly.  He added D (Prague),
  which Baehrens had overlooked, and five found since: A, W, P, C and
  R.  A, W, P and R, of the 10th and 10th–11th centuries, are the
  oldest witnesses of the poem.

Vollmer's account of the tradition: a single old copy, perhaps from
Spain, reached the Carolingian age and was copied for schools from
about the 9th century.  Two families can still be told apart: PW(A),
and a second from which descend first MN, then ELR, then CFV, with
some good readings of PW passing into E and, more of them, into the
source of CFV.  B, G, the Helmstadiensis and the other late
manuscripts are mixed.  He knew about 50 more recent manuscripts and
cited only the Helmstadiensis often; the Vienna manuscript matters only
for its title (pp. VI–VII).  The dates also changed: Baehrens put E and
L in the 12th century, Vollmer in the 11th.

### The printed editions

Which edition came first was already unclear to Wernsdorf and Plessis.
Wernsdorf and his Lemaire editor (pp. 502–505) list, among others: an
undated Florentine edition entitled *Homerus de bello Trojano*; Parma
(Ugoletus, 1492), which says that Pindar made the epitome to teach his
son; Leipzig (Martinus Herbipolensis, c. 1494–1497), with epitaphs of
Hector and Achilles appended; Fano (Abstemius, 1505, reprinted 1515);
Vienna (1513) in eight books; Hagenau (Secerius, 1531); Basel (Torinus,
1541) with Joseph of Exeter under the name of Dares; Spondanus's Homer
(Basel, 1583 and 1606), which copied Torinus; and the Pesaro corpus
(1766).  Vollmer collated five: α (1477, no place, "the first, as it
seems"), β (Ugoletus, 1492), δ (Denidel, 1498), φ (Fano, 1505) and λ
(Leipzig, 1509) (p. VII).  Of the early editions only Fano's is valued
by Baehrens, Mueller and Plessis.

## 9. History of scholarship

The editors' accounts, taken together, give this sequence, with their
verdicts on one another:

- **Renaissance.**  Valla's extravagant praise; Barth's emendations
  and praise in his *Adversaria* and his Statius (testimonia; Wernsdorf,
  p. 501).
- **The Dutch, 18th century.**  Bondam (*Variae lectiones*, 1759,
  especially the proper names), van Dorp and Schrader (*Observationes*),
  Higt, van der Dussen (*Prodromus*, 1769; suspected of taking some of
  Higt's work), de Rooy (*Spicilegia critica*, 1771).  Vollmer: "the
  Dutch accomplished the best and the most" (p. VIII).
- **Wernsdorf** (1784) gathered their work and added his own
  commentary.  Plessis: he used poor manuscripts and worked hastily,
  but "deserved far better of our poet … than those who preceded him"
  (p. XLVI).
- **Van Kooten and Weytingh** (Leiden and Amsterdam, 1809; Baehrens
  gives Amsterdam, 1806).  Van Kooten used the Leiden manuscript,
  healed several passages and noted the parallels in Virgil, Ovid and
  Homer; Weytingh added little (Plessis, p. XLVI).
- **L. Mueller** (Berlin, 1857) joined E to L and laid the critical
  foundation; Plessis praises him highly but faults his apparatus for
  variants from bad manuscripts (pp. XLVI–XLVII).  Vollmer thought he
  did not "cut to the quick" (p. VIII).
- **Baehrens** (1881) was the first to replace the vulgate with the
  manuscripts' testimony (Vollmer), though Plessis found him too fond of
  conjecture and Vollmer his collations careless.
- **Plessis** (1885), with Havet's help.  Vollmer: he "achieved almost
  nothing", as R. Ehwald's review had rightly judged (p. VIII).
- **Vollmer** (1913), with the fuller report of the manuscripts in
  *Sitzungsberichte der Münchner Akademie* 1913, no. 3, and H. C.
  Remme's dissertation on their history (1906).

## 10. The indexes

Two of the editions end with indexes that serve different purposes:

- **Plessis's index of names and subjects**
  ([index-en.md](4-plessis/index-en.md)) lists for each name every
  verse where it occurs, sorted by grammatical case, with a short
  account of the scene: for Achilles, for example, from his anger at
  Agamemnon (8) to his yielding to Priam (1043), with separate entries
  for Aeacides, Pelides, Nereius and Thetideius heros.  Ornamental
  epithets were printed in heavier type, to show how far the poet
  follows Latin rather than Greek usage (introduction, p. XXXI, note 2).
- **Vollmer's index of names** ([index-en.md](6-vollmer/index-en.md))
  is shorter and philological.  It quotes the phrases in Latin,
  identifies the persons (with patronymics and Homeric forms), marks
  emended passages with an asterisk and gives the transmitted reading
  (*trad.*).  Many of these are names restored from Homer:
  *Laertiades* for *atrides* (372), *Nireus* for *teucer* (195),
  *Pylaeus* for *ephialtes* (240), *Poeante* for *phetonte* (217),
  *Latiis* for *laetis* (900).
