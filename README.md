# ilias-latina

Texts and tools for writing a commentary on the Latin text of the
*Ilias Latina* (Baebius Italicus, 1st century AD), a 1,070-line Latin
summary of Homer's Iliad.

## About the poem

The *Ilias Latina* retells Homer's *Iliad* in Latin hexameters, reducing
the 15,693 lines of the original to 1,070.  It is attributed to Publius
Baebius Italicus, reportedly a Roman senator, and dated to about AD 60–70.
The poem was popular in antiquity and remained a standard school text
throughout the Middle Ages; Ernst Robert Curtius nevertheless called it a
"crude condensation".  Its opening lines, once emended, form the acrostic
ITALICUS and its closing lines SCRIPSIT, together "Italicus wrote (it)".

## Background

Numbers in brackets refer to the [Internet Archive](#internet-archive)
list under References.

### Latin Homer before the *Ilias Latina*

Livius Andronicus' Saturnian *Odyssey* was a school book, but the *Iliad*
had no lasting Latin version.  Cn. Matius and Ninnius Crassus translated
it under the Republic, and in the early Empire Attius Labeo made a
literal hexameter version mocked by Persius, while Polybius, the freedman
of Claudius, produced a work praised by Seneca, possibly in prose.  Of
all these only the *Ilias Latina* survives [5].

### Date

Verses 899–902, which praise the line of Aeneas, point to the
Julio-Claudian dynasty, and the poem is clearly later than Ovid.  It is
generally placed before the death of Nero [3][4][5].

### Author

- The initial letters of verses 1–8 and 1063–1070 read ITALICES and
  SCQIPSIT in the manuscripts; after emendation they give ITALICUS
  SCRIPSIT.  The acrostics were first noticed by F. Bücheler (1880) [3][5].
- Bücheler took the author to be Silius Italicus, author of the *Punica*.
  This is rejected because Martial implies that Silius began writing
  poetry only after retiring from public life, after Nero, and because
  the style differs from the *Punica* [3][4][5].
- The full name comes from the title in a 15th-century Vienna manuscript
  (lat. 3509): *Bebii Italici poetae clarissimi epithome in quatuor
  viginti libros Homeri Iliados*.  Vollmer accepts it as Baebius
  Italicus [6].
- From the Middle Ages to the 18th century the poem went under other
  names: *Homerus*, *Homerus Latinus*, and from the 11th century
  *Pindarus* or *Pindarus Thebanus* (Benzo of Alba, c. 1087; Hugo von
  Trimberg, 13th century; also *Pandarus* in one manuscript) [3][4][5].
  The printed editions kept the name *Pindarus Thebanus* [1].  Wernsdorf
  proposed Avienus, having earlier suggested Pentadius [2].

### Structure

The manuscripts divide the poem into 24 books after the *Iliad*, though
the division does not always agree with Homer.  Vollmer thinks it was
added by a late-antique schoolmaster, not by the poet [6].

The divisions used here are based on the Portuguese translation listed
under [References](#references), which follows Scaffai's edition.  The
first verse of each book was checked against the Latin text of The Latin
Library, and the verse numbers agree with it.  Each book is defined by
its first verse and runs up to the verse before the next book.  The first
verses and a summary of each book are listed in
[texts/README.md](texts/README.md).

This repository follows the text and verse order of The Latin Library;
the translation was consulted for the book divisions and is used only as
an aid for checking the content.  Scaffai's edition, as printed in the
translation, differs in three places:

- Verse 791, a gap in The Latin Library text (`<>`), is absent: the
  numbering goes from 790 to 792, and book 15 begins at 792.  As the
  books are defined by their first verses, the gap falls in book 14.
  According to Vollmer's apparatus, the verse is *Instaurantque manus,
  cedit Pelopeia uirtus*; it is absent from most manuscripts and stands
  in the text of Munich lat. 5594 and of the edition Vollmer calls X,
  and in the margin of others [6].  Baehrens prints it in square
  brackets [3], Plessis below the text [4].
- Book 16 has a verse 827a that The Latin Library lacks.  Plessis prints
  it in square brackets as 827 bis (*Obicit et saxum magno cum robore
  missum*) [4].
- Verse 597 (*alterni librant gladios et uulnera miscent*) is moved after
  601.  In the manuscript order it interrupts the simile of the fighting
  boars (595–600); after 601 it completes the sentence *Tales Priamides
  ardorque Aiacis in armis …* that applies the simile to Hector and Ajax.
  Vollmer also prints the verse after 601 [6]; Plessis prints it below
  the text as a gloss ("nihil hoc nisi glossema est") [4].

The numbering of Vollmer, Baehrens, Plessis and Wernsdorf (Lemaire), as
far as it differs from The Latin Library, is described with their texts
([texts/6-vollmer/](texts/6-vollmer/README.md),
[texts/3-baehrens/](texts/3-baehrens/README.md),
[texts/4-plessis/](texts/4-plessis/README.md),
[texts/2-lemaire/](texts/2-lemaire/README.md)); for example, Baehrens
omits verse 69 and counts a lacuna as 80, Plessis keeps the same
numbers and prints the verses he rejects, such as 69, below the text,
and Wernsdorf counts 1075 verses, with seven that The Latin Library
does not have.
The editions have not yet been compared as a whole, in their numbering
or in their book divisions.

The proportions are very uneven: books 1–5 take 537 verses, just over
half, while book 13 has seven verses and book 17 three [5].

The introduction to the Portuguese translation adds the following:

- The book divisions are not always clear and sometimes fall within a
  verse; the poet also changes the order of some events, especially
  towards the end.
- The poem is a selective rather than a regular summary.  Some episodes,
  such as Chryses' prayer or the duel of Menelaus and Paris, keep nearly
  their Homeric length, while others are left out.  Several episodes are
  merged, so that one character does what several do in Homer.
- Hence the poem was probably not written for schools, although it was
  later used there.

### Style

The poet writes clear, fluent hexameters modelled above all on Vergil and
Ovid [3][4][5].  Butler judges that he knew "his Vergil well and his Ovid
better" [5].

### Transmission and reception

- The poem seems to have been used in schools already in antiquity; a
  trace is found in Lactantius Placidus, the commentator on Statius [3].
- According to Vollmer, a single old copy, perhaps from Spain, reached
  the Carolingian age and was copied for schools from the 9th century on.
  The manuscripts fall into two families.  The oldest witnesses are
  10th–11th-century fragments and manuscripts (Brussels, Valenciennes,
  Antwerp) [6].
- Medieval use: Ermenricus quotes verse 7 in a letter to Grimald
  (850–855); the *Gesta Berengarii* (10th century) borrows many verses;
  Eberhard of Béthune's *Labyrinthus* lists it among school authors [3][5].
- The first printed edition appeared about 1477 [6].  Spondanus printed
  the poem after his Homer (1606) as an *Epitome* by Pindarus Thebanus
  [1].

### History of scholarship

Dutch scholars of the 18th century did much of the early work (P. Bondam,
A. van Dorp, J. Schrader, E. W. Higt, J. van der Dussen 1769, A. de Booy
1771).  Wernsdorf collected it in *Poetae Latini Minores* IV (1784),
reprinted by Lemaire [2]; the edition of Th. van Kooten was completed by
H. Weytingh (Leiden and Amsterdam, 1809, according to Vollmer [6];
Baehrens gives Amsterdam, 1806 [3]).  Critical editions followed from
L. Müller, Baehrens [3], Plessis [4] and Vollmer [6].  Modern editions and
commentaries include Scaffai (1982; 2nd ed. 1997), Kennedy (1998),
Perkins (2006), Falcone & Schubert (2022) and Green (2025).

## Text

[texts/ilias.txt](texts/ilias.txt) is the Latin text of The Latin Library
with a Markdown heading for each of the 24 books (`## 1` to `## 24`) at
the divisions under [Structure](#structure).  The verses keep their
numbers (`N TEXT`) and the editorial signs of the page (`<que>`, `<>`,
`*`).  The books are listed with summaries in
[texts/README.md](texts/README.md).

## Tools

This repository provides helper tools for preparing the commentary:

- Number the verses of the Latin text of The Latin Library.
- Add the headings of the 24 books to it and write
  [texts/ilias.txt](texts/ilias.txt).
- Organize the public-domain editions for reference by page and verse
  (Lemaire [2]: [texts/2-lemaire/](texts/2-lemaire/README.md);
  Baehrens [3]: [texts/3-baehrens/](texts/3-baehrens/README.md);
  Plessis [4]: [texts/4-plessis/](texts/4-plessis/README.md);
  Vollmer [6]: [texts/6-vollmer/](texts/6-vollmer/README.md)).
- Optionally, align the Portuguese translation with it verse by verse
  into a parallel text, as an aid for checking the content.

Both texts are listed under [References](#references).

See [src/README.md](src/README.md) for requirements and usage.

### Internet Archive

Public-domain books on archive.org.

1. Jean de Sponde (Spondanus) (ed.), [*Homeri quae extant omnia*](https://archive.org/details/homeriquextant00home).
   Basel: Henricpetri, 1606.  Homer with Latin translation and
   commentary.
   - The poem is printed after the notes on *Iliad* 24 as *Epitome ac
     summa universae Iliados Homeri, Pindaro Thebano auctore*.
   - Text only, without notes.  Of interest as evidence of the
     Renaissance attribution to Pindarus Thebanus.
   - The OCR is poor: two columns are mixed and the long s is misread.

2. N. E. Lemaire (ed.), [*Poetae Latini Minores*, vol. 3](https://archive.org/details/poetaelatinimin00unkngoog).
   Paris: Lemaire, 1824.  Reprints Wernsdorf's edition (1784) of the poem
   as *Incerti auctoris (vulgo Pindari Thebani) Epitome Iliados Homeri*.
   - The only running commentary among these books: Latin notes on each
     passage with parallels from Vergil, Ovid, Statius, Valerius Flaccus
     and others, and discussion of readings, keyed to Wernsdorf's own
     numbering (1075 verses).  Four excursus discuss difficult passages.
   - A long prooemium (pp. 455–507): Wernsdorf's argument for Avienus
     as the author (giving up his earlier Pentadius, p. 468) and against
     the name Pindarus Thebanus, outdated but useful for the history of
     the question; the earlier Latin translators of Homer with their
     fragments; the manuscripts and editions of the poem.
   - *De Epitome Iliados Homeri ejusque auctore testimonia auctorum ac
     judicia*: ancient and later testimonia and judgements on the poem.

3. Emil Baehrens (ed.), [*Poetae Latini Minores*, vol. 3–4](https://archive.org/details/poetaelatinimino34baeh).
   Leipzig: Teubner, 1881–1882.  Vol. 3 contains the *Ilias Latina*.
   - A short Latin preface on the author, the date and medieval
     reception (Ermenricus, *Gesta Berengarii*, Benzo, Hugo von
     Trimberg, Eberhard of Béthune), on the name Pindarus, on the eight
     manuscripts used (B E F G L M N V) and on the earlier editions.
   - The apparatus is mostly manuscript readings, but notes the verses
     borrowed by the *Gesta Berengarii* and the conjectures of earlier
     editors (Higtius, Schraderus, Kootenus, L. Muellerus and others).
   - Verses held to be interpolated are printed in square brackets.
   - Largely superseded by Vollmer [6].

4. Frédéric Plessis (ed.), [*Italici Ilias Latina*](https://archive.org/details/italiciiliaslati00plesuoft).
   Paris: Hachette, 1885.  Edition of the poem.
   - A Latin thesis with a full introduction (pp. V–LI): the name of the
     author, the date, the Silius Italicus question; Latin translations
     of Homer at Rome; the method, talent and purpose of the poem
     (imitation of Vergil and Ovid, errors, how it abridges); manuscripts
     and editions; the false name Pindarus.
   - The apparatus gives the readings of 13 manuscripts throughout and
     of three more (T C D) in part (p. XLIII); separate notes give the
     conjectures of earlier editors and some parallels in the *Iliad*.
   - Verses held to be spurious are printed below the text, with a
     short remark.
   - An index of names and subjects lists, for each name, the verses
     where it occurs with a short account of each scene.

5. H. E. Butler, [*Post-Augustan Poetry from Seneca to Juvenal*](https://archive.org/details/postaugustanpoet00butluoft).
   Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1909.  Includes a discussion of the
   *Ilias Latina*.
   - A short English account (pp. 160–163): earlier Latin translations
     of Homer, the uneven proportions of the books, the date, the
     acrostics, the name Pindarus and the attribution to Silius
     Italicus.

6. Friedrich Vollmer (ed.), [*Poetae Latini Minores*, vol. 2, fasc. 1–3](https://archive.org/details/p1poetaelatinimi02baeh).
   Leipzig: Teubner, 1911–1923.  Fasc. 3, *Homerus Latinus* (1913),
   revises Baehrens's edition of the *Ilias Latina*.
   - The margin of the text gives the line numbers of the *Iliad* that
     the poet follows, which also shows where he departs from Homer.
   - The preface gives the 24-book division, the evidence for the name
     Baebius, the manuscript families, the early printed editions and
     the history of scholarship.
   - The apparatus includes medieval quotations (e.g. Ermenricus) and
     short explanatory glosses.
