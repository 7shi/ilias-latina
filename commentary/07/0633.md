### 633–635 (*Iliad* 7.313–320, 7.482)

> 633 Implentur dapibus largis Bacchique liquore
> (They take their fill of plentiful food and Bacchus’s wine,)
>
> 634 atque auidi placido tradunt sua corpora somno.
> (and eagerly surrender their bodies to peaceful sleep.)
>
> 635 Postera cum primum stellas Aurora fugarat,
> (As soon as Aurora had driven away the stars the next day,)

After the champions’ exchange of gifts and the armies’ departure, eating and sleeping complete the movement from combat to rest. Bacchus is the god of wine, whose name here stands for the drink itself. The men eagerly yield to sleep: for the moment, the demands of the body replace those of battle.

The Latin greatly compresses Homer’s account. In the *Iliad*, the feast takes place in the quarters of Agamemnon, the Greek commander. He sacrifices a five-year-old bull to Zeus, and the poet describes its preparation before the men enjoy their meal (*Iliad* 7.313–320). Here the sacrifice and cooking disappear, leaving only abundance and satisfaction. The diners are not explicitly identified; coming after the departure of both armies, the brief description gives a more general impression of rest after fighting. It also passes over the councils, burial of the dead, and Greek defensive preparations that separate this feast from the later night’s sleep in Homer.

Aurora is the goddess of dawn. Her driving the stars from the sky answers the preceding image of black night covering the heavens. The final line begins a sentence whose action is still to come: the night’s peaceful interval ends, and a new day opens the next stage of the story.
