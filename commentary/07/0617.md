### 617 (*Iliad* 7.282, 7.293)

> 617 coeperat igniferos Titan immergere currus
> (Titan had begun to plunge his fire-bearing chariot)

The sentence begun in the preceding passage continues: just as Hector and Ajax prepare to renew their duel, the weary sun begins to sink into the waves. Titan here means the sun god, imagined driving a fiery chariot across the sky and descending into the sea at sunset. The weariness belongs to him, not to either fighter; his day’s journey is ending as their combat approaches its limit.

In Homer, nightfall becomes an explicit reason to stop the duel. The herald Idaeus declares, “Night is already here; it is good to heed the night” (*Iliad* 7.282), and Hector later repeats those words (*Iliad* 7.293). The Latin instead marks the approaching darkness through the sun god’s descent. Against the fighters’ renewed readiness to attack, it sets the close of day: their encounter is to end not with a decisive victory, but because the time for fighting has run out.
