### 586 (*Iliad* 9.186–189)

> 586 et cithara dulci durum lenibat amorem.
> (and with his sweet lyre was soothing his harsh love.)

While the other champions prepare to face Hector, Achilles remains apart, finding comfort in music. His “harsh love” suggests longing for Briseis, the captive woman Agamemnon has taken from him. The verse thus gives his withdrawal a more intimate aspect: alongside wounded honour and anger lies the distress of separation.

The Latin brings forward a scene from a later part of Homer’s story. In the *Iliad*, envoys sent to persuade Achilles to return to battle find him delighting in an ornate lyre with a silver crossbar. He is “singing the glorious deeds of men” (*Iliad* 9.189). The Latin keeps the instrument and its comforting power, but leaves out its decoration and the subject of his song. Instead, it explicitly makes love the pain his music relieves. Homer’s Achilles remains absorbed in the heroic world through song even while refusing to fight; here, the emphasis falls on the private suffering that separates him from his comrades.
