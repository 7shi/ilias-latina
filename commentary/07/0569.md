### 569–571 (*Iliad* 6.469–470, 6.472–474)

> 569 terribilemque fugit galeam cristamque comantem.
> (and recoils from the terrifying helmet and its flowing crest.)
>
> 570 Vtque caput iuuenis posito detexerat aere,
> (And when the young warrior, the bronze laid aside, had uncovered his head,)
>
> 571 protinus infantem geminis amplectitur ulnis
> (he at once embraces the infant in both arms,)

The cause of the child’s fear now becomes clear: Astyanax recoils not from his father himself, but from his helmet and its threatening crest of horsehair. Hector, the warrior who protects Troy, must put aside this part of his armour before he can hold his son. His response is immediate and gentle. The equipment that makes him formidable in battle has become an obstacle to affection.

The Latin closely follows the movement of Homer’s scene, while simplifying its details. In the *Iliad*, the child sees the horsehair crest nodding fearfully from the helmet’s top; Hector removes the helmet and sets it, still gleaming, on the ground. Here those movements become a swift uncovering of his head, followed at once by the embrace. Homer describes Hector kissing his son and rocking him in his hands (*Iliad* 6.474). The Latin has already shown him trying to kiss the frightened baby; now the child is finally gathered into his arms. The brief interruption of fear gives way to the closeness Hector had sought.
