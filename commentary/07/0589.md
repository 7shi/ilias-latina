### 589–594 (*Iliad* 7.244–254)

> 589 principio iactis committunt proelia telis:
> (First they begin the battle by hurling spears:)
>
> 590 mox rigidos stringunt enses et fortibus armis
> (soon they draw their stout swords and with strong weapons)
>
> 591 decernunt partesque oculis rimantur apertas
> (fight it out, their eyes searching for unprotected places,)
>
> 592 et modo terga petunt, duros modo fortibus ictus
> (now aiming at each other’s backs, now beating back hard blows)
>
> 593 depellunt clipeis; ingens ad sidera clamor
> (with sturdy shields; a mighty shout rises to the stars,)
>
> 594 tollitur et uastis impletur uocibus aer.
> (and the air is filled with thunderous voices.)

Aiax, the chosen Greek champion, now faces Hector in single combat. The opening exchange of spears quickly becomes a close struggle with swords and shields. Both fighters are watchful as well as powerful: each searches for a place where the other’s protection leaves him vulnerable, trying to reach his back while guarding against incoming blows. The exposed neck at which a sword is aimed later in the duel makes this search for an opening concrete. Around the combatants, the watching armies fill the air with their shouting.

Homer describes the spear throws much more precisely. Hector’s spear pierces the bronze facing and six layers of hide in Aiax’s shield, but the seventh layer stops it. Aiax’s answering spear passes through Hector’s shield and breastplate and cuts his tunic beside the flank; Hector escapes death by swerving aside (*Iliad* 7.244–254). The Latin reduces this carefully distinguished exchange to a single opening action, then moves straight to sword fighting. Instead of following each weapon through successive layers of protection, it presents a rapid alternation of attack and defence. The balanced movements keep both champions before us, while the rising clamour makes their duel the centre of the whole battlefield’s attention.
