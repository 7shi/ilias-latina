Andromache, Hector’s most faithful wife, seeks to speak with him, holding their infant son, Astyanax. The child recoils from his father’s helmet and flowing crest, so Hector lays aside the bronze, embraces him, and prays to the “best of fathers” that his son may imitate his father’s virtues from his earliest years. Hector then returns through Troy’s gates to the battle, accompanied by Paris, and challenges the Greek leaders.

The Greeks who come forward are Ulysses, “contriver of deceit”; fierce Idomeneus; Meriones, renowned for his father’s lineage; Atrides, the Greeks’ valiant leader; the two Ajaxes; Eurypylus, splendid in shining arms; Thoas, son of great Andraemon; and the warrior who wounded Venus’ hand, unnamed here. Achilles, “terror of the Trojans,” is absent, soothing his harsh love with his sweet lyre. Lots are cast in King Atrides’ gilded helmet, and mighty Ajax comes forth to fight Hector. This Ajax is also called Telamonian Ajax, Telamon’s son, and Aeacides; Hector is called Priamides and repeatedly described as mighty, famed in war, or greatest in war.

Hector and Ajax exchange spear casts, then fight with swords and shields amid tremendous shouting. Their ferocity is compared to that of enraged boars. Ajax aims at Hector’s bare neck; Hector ducks and drives off the blade with his shield, but the sword grazes his neck. Hector then hurls a great stone, which Ajax withstands with his sevenfold shield before striking Hector down with the same stone. Apollo, hostile to the Greeks, catches Hector, raises him, and restores his courage. They are drawing their swords again when the weary Titan begins to plunge his fiery chariot into the waves and night climbs the sky, and men are sent to part the combatants.

Hector asks Ajax about his homeland and parents, praising the nobility evident in his strength. Ajax names Hesione as his mother and Telamon as his father. Recalling Hesione’s name and misfortunes, Hector declares their common blood and proposes that they desist. He gives Ajax a gold-adorned sword and receives an engraved belt in return. Both armies withdraw, eat, drink Bacchus’s wine, and sleep.

When Aurora disperses the stars the next morning, the Phrygians, that is the Trojans, assemble. Remembering the previous day’s losses, Hector urges returning Helen to the unconquered Achaeans, together with plunder to soothe the harsh fires of Menelaus, and all approve. Idaeus carries the proposal to fierce Atrides, who refuses to listen or accept the plunder and orders him out of the camp. Idaeus returns to the camp of Troy, scorned. The Greeks are also called Achaeans and Danaans. The book ends with the grieving Danaans gathering and burning their dead on great pyres, then restoring their trenches and surrounding their rampart with an oak palisade.
