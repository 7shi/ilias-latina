Andromache, Hector’s devoted wife, approaches him carrying their infant son, Astyanacta. The child recoils from his father’s bronze helmet and flowing crest, so Hector removes the helmet, embraces him, and prays to the “best of fathers” that his son may imitate his virtues from his earliest years. Hector then returns through Troy’s gates to the battle, accompanied by Paris, and challenges the Greek leaders.

The Greeks who come forward are Vlixes, “contriver of deceit”; fierce Idomeneus; Meriones, renowned for his paternal lineage; Atrides, the Greeks’ valiant leader; the two Aiaces; Eurypylus, splendid in shining armour; Thoas, son of great Andraemon; and the warrior who wounded Venus’ hand, unnamed here. Achilles, “terror of the Trojans,” is absent, soothing his painful love with his sweet-sounding lyre. Lots are cast in King Atrides’ gilded helmet, and mighty Aiax is selected to fight Hector. This Aiax is also called Telamonian Aiax, Telamon’s son, and Aeacides; Hector is called Priamides and repeatedly described as mighty, famed in war, or greatest in war.

Hector and Aiax exchange spear casts, then fight with swords and shields amid tremendous shouting. Their ferocity is compared to that of enraged boars. Aiax aims at Hector’s exposed neck; Hector ducks and deflects the blow with his shield, but the sword grazes his neck. Hector then hurls a great stone, which Aiax withstands with his sevenfold shield before striking Hector down with the same stone. Apollo, called the enemy of the Grai, catches Hector, raises him, and restores his courage. They are about to resume sword fighting when sunset and approaching night bring intervention: Titan sinks his fiery chariot into the waves, and messengers separate the combatants.

Hector asks Aiax about his homeland and parents, praising the nobility evident in his strength. Aiax names Hesiona as his mother and Telamon as his father. Recalling Hesiona and her misfortunes, Hector recognizes their shared blood and proposes that they cease fighting. He gives Aiax a gold-adorned sword and receives an engraved belt in return. Both armies withdraw, eat, drink Bacchus’s wine, and sleep.

When Aurora disperses the stars the next morning, the Trojans assemble. Remembering the previous day’s losses, Hector urges returning Helene to the unconquered Achiui, together with plunder to appease Menelaus, and all approve. Idaeus carries the proposal to fierce Atrides, who refuses to listen or accept the plunder and orders him out of the camp. Idaeus returns to Troy unsuccessful. The Trojans are also called Troes and Phryges; the Greeks are called Graians, Grai, Achiui, and Danai. The book ends with the grieving Danai gathering and burning their dead on great pyres, then restoring their trenches and surrounding their rampart with an oak palisade.
