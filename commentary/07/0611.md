### 611–613 (*Iliad* 7.264–272)

> 611 sed magno saxi iactu petit. At ferus Aiax
> (but with a mighty throw of a stone he attacks. But fierce Ajax)
>
> 612 ingentem clipeo septemplice reppulit ictum
> (beat back the massive blow with his sevenfold shield)
>
> 613 et iuuenem saxo percussum sternit eodem.
> (and strikes the young man with the same stone, laying him low.)

Hector, continuing his attack, replaces his sword with a stone. Ajax withstands the blow and turns the weapon back against him: the same stone that Hector throws now knocks Hector himself to the ground. The “young man” is Hector, not a new opponent, and “laying him low” describes his fall rather than his death. Ajax’s sevenfold shield has seven layers of oxhide beneath its bronze covering, a formidable defense against the heavy missile.

The Latin compresses a much fuller exchange in Homer. Hector there picks up a large, black, jagged stone and strikes the central boss of Ajax’s shield, making the bronze ring (*Iliad* 7.264–267). Ajax then throws a different, much larger stone, driving Hector’s shield inward and buckling his knees; Hector falls backwards with his shield pressed against him (*Iliad* 7.268–272). By making Ajax use the same stone, the Latin turns this contest of increasingly heavy missiles into a swift reversal of Hector’s attack. This passage ends with Hector down; in the next verse, as in Homer, the god Apollo raises him up (*Iliad* 7.272).
