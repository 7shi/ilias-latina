### 614–616 (*Iliad* 7.272–273)

> 614 Quem leuat exceptum Grais inimicus Apollo
> (Him Apollo, hostile to the Greeks, catches and lifts up)
>
> 615 integratque animum; iam rursus ad arma coibant
> (and restores his courage; already they were closing again to fight)
>
> 616 stringebantque iterum gladios, cum fessus in undas
> (and were drawing their swords again, when, weary, into the waves)

Hector’s fall is not the end of the duel. Apollo supports him and raises him, undoing the immediate effect of Ajax’s successful throw. The god is described as hostile to the Greeks, whose champion Ajax is, and his intervention here favours their Trojan opponent. He restores not only Hector’s footing but also his courage: Hector is made ready to face Ajax again.

Homer says simply that Apollo “quickly raised him up” (*Iliad* 7.272); the Latin adds the restoration of Hector’s spirit and makes the god’s allegiance explicit. It also presents the renewed combat more directly. Where Homer says that the two warriors “would now have struck each other at close quarters with their swords” (*Iliad* 7.273), the Latin shows them already approaching and drawing their blades. After the exchange of stones, they are returning to sword-fighting, but the word “when” introduces an interruption. The final phrase remains unfinished here, carrying the narrative into the next passage.
