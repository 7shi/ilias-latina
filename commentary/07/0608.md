### 608–610 (*Iliad* 7.262–264)

> 608 ensis et exiguo ceruicem uulnere libat.
> (and the sword grazes his neck, leaving a slight wound.)
>
> 609 Acrius impugnans rursus consurgit in hostem
> (Attacking more fiercely, he springs at his foe once more—)
>
> 610 Priamides nec iam ferro Telamone creatum,
> (Priamides—and now no longer with his sword against Telamon’s son...)

The sword that slipped along Hector’s shield now reaches his neck, but only grazes it. Rather than checking him, the wound prompts a fiercer attack. Priamides means “son of Priam,” the king of Troy, and here names Hector; Telamon’s son is his opponent, Aiax. The passage ends mid-sentence, as Hector abandons the sword for another means of fighting.

In Homer, it is Aiax’s spear that cuts Hector’s neck, and “dark blood welled up” (*Iliad* 7.262). The Latin continues its reshaping of that spear-thrust into a sword-stroke, explicitly making the wound slight and omitting the blood. Both poems stress Hector’s refusal to yield, but the Latin makes his renewed aggression more emphatic: he attacks more fiercely than before. Homer then has Hector draw back and take up a stone in his powerful hand (*Iliad* 7.263–264). Here the unfinished sentence prepares that change of weapon without yet revealing what will replace the sword.
