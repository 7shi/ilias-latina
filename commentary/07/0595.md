### 595–604 (*Iliad* 7.256–257, 7.260–262)

> 595 Non sic saetigeri exacuunt feruoribus iras
> (Not so fiercely do bristling boars stoke their rage in their frenzy)
>
> 596 pectoribusque petunt uastis, modo dentibus uncis
> (and charge with their massive chests; now with hooked tusks)
>
> 597 alterni librant gladios et uulnera miscent.
> (they take turns swinging swords and exchanging wounds.)
>
> 598 fortia terga premunt spumantque per ora uicissim;
> (They press hard on each other’s sturdy backs and foam in turn at the mouth;)
>
> 599 fumiferae nubes concretaque fulgura et ignes
> (smoky clouds, massed lightning bolts and flames)
>
> 600 iactantur magnoque implentur murmure siluae.
> (are hurled forth, and the woods are filled with a mighty rumbling.)
>
> 601 Tales Priamides ardorque Aiacis in armis
> (Such are Priamides and Aiax in his armed fury.)
>
> 602 Tandem animis teloque furens Telamonius Aiax
> (At last Telamonian Aiax, furious at heart and fierce with his weapon,)
>
> 603 insignem bello petit Hectora, quaque patebat
> (attacks Hector, famed in war, and where)
>
> 604 nuda uiri ceruix, fulgentem derigit ensem.
> (the warrior’s neck lay bare, aims his gleaming sword.)

The duel’s alternating attacks and defences now become a struggle between raging boars. Their massive bodies, curved tusks and foaming mouths give animal force to the champions’ violence. Even within the comparison, the language of human combat persists: the tusks are wielded like swords, and the animals exchange wounds. Their pressure on each other’s backs recalls the fighters’ search for an opening in the preceding passage. Yet the opening claim makes the champions fiercer still: not even boars fight with quite such rage.

Homer briefly compares the two warriors to flesh-eating lions or wild boars of unflagging strength (*Iliad* 7.256–257). The Latin selects the boars and expands the comparison into a fantastic spectacle. Smoke, lightning and fire turn animal fury into something like a storm sweeping through the woods. The forest’s rumbling also answers the clamour of the watching armies, carrying the noise of the duel into the imagined scene.

Priamides is Hector, son of Priam, the king of Troy; “Telamonian” identifies Aiax as the son of Telamon. As the comparison ends, Aiax takes the initiative and aims at Hector’s exposed neck—the kind of vulnerable place both men have been seeking. In Homer, Aiax leaps forward and thrusts a spear through Hector’s shield; it grazes his neck and draws blood (*Iliad* 7.260–262). Here the weapon is a sword, continuing the close combat already introduced by the Latin. The passage ends with that gleaming blade directed at the opening, before revealing the blow’s result.
