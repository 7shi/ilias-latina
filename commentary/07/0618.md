### 618–619 (*Iliad* 7.274–279)

> 618 noxque subire polum: iuxta mittuntur, utrosque
> (and night to climb the sky: close by are sent, both)
>
> 619 qui dirimant a caede uiros, nec segnius illi
> (to part the men from slaughter, and no less promptly they)

As the sun sinks, night begins to fill the sky, completing the scene begun in the preceding verse. The duel between Hector, the Trojan champion, and Ajax, his Greek opponent, is now interrupted by men sent to separate them. Neither fighter has defeated the other: an intervention from outside brings their encounter towards its close.

Homer gives this intervention more ceremony and a clearer sense of shared authority. Two heralds approach, Talthybius from the Greek army and Idaeus from the Trojans. Described as messengers of Zeus and of men, they hold their staffs between the combatants. Idaeus then appeals to both: “My dear sons, fight and battle no longer” (*Iliad* 7.279). Their role allows them to stand between enemies and speak to both sides.

The Latin compresses their arrival and intervention into the simple statement that men are sent to stop the bloodshed. It omits their names, their staffs, and their special standing as heralds, keeping attention on the urgent practical task of separating the warriors. The unfinished final phrase carries the action into the next passage.
