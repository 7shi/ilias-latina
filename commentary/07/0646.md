### 646–648 (*Iliad* 7.430–431, 7.434, 7.432)

> 646 Interea Danai confusi caede suorum
> (Meanwhile the Danaans, grieving over the slaughter of their own,)
>
> 647 ingentes struxere pyras collectaque passim
> (built great pyres and gathered from all around)
>
> 648 fortia tradiderunt sociorum corpora flammis.
> (the brave bodies of their comrades they consigned to the flames.)

While the herald returns to Troy, the story turns to the Danaans, as the Greeks are called here, as they bury their dead. Their distress is sorrow at the loss of their companions, not confusion about what to do. Gathering the bodies and building the funeral pyres gives that grief a practical expression. Calling the fallen “brave” honours them even as their bodies are surrendered to the fire.

Homer describes the Greeks heaping their dead upon a pyre, “grieving at heart,” then burning them and returning to their ships (*Iliad* 7.430–432). The Latin retains the sorrow and cremation but makes the scene more expansive through its great pyres and bodies collected from across the battlefield. It leaves out the return to the ships, ending instead with the flames. In Homer, both armies recover and burn their dead under an agreed pause in the fighting. Here, after the embassy has ended on a note of rejection, the Greek funeral proceeds without that agreement being recounted: the emphasis falls on the shared loss within the army rather than cooperation between enemies.
