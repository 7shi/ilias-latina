### 587–588 (*Iliad* 7.176, 7.181–183)

> 587 Ergo ubi deiectis auratam regis Atridae
> (So when, after lots had been cast into King Atrides’ gilded)
>
> 588 sortibus in galeam magnus processerat Aiax,
> (helmet, mighty Aiax had stepped forward,)

The story returns from Achilles’ solitary music to the choice of a champion to face Hector, the foremost Trojan warrior. The Greek volunteers submit themselves to a drawing of lots, using their commander’s helmet as the container. King Atrides is Agamemnon: “Atrides” means “son of Atreus.” The chosen fighter, Aiax, is the son of Telamon, a warrior renowned for his size, strength, and defensive prowess. With Achilles still refusing to fight, he offers the Greeks a formidable champion.

The Latin compresses the drawing into a swift transition from the casting of lots to Aiax’s emergence. In Homer, Nestor, the elderly Greek leader, shakes Agamemnon’s helmet, and the lot of Aiax leaps out—the very lot the Greeks had hoped would win (*Iliad* 7.181–183). A herald then carries it through the gathering for identification. Here those intermediate actions disappear, bringing the chosen warrior directly before us. The gilding of Agamemnon’s helmet, a detail absent from the corresponding Homeric passage, adds a touch of royal splendour to the selection.
