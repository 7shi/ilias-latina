### 564–568 (*Iliad* 6.394–395, 6.399–403, 6.466–468)

> 564 Colloquium petit interea fidissima coniunx
> (Meanwhile the most faithful wife seeks converse,)
>
> 565 Hectoris Andromache paruumque ad pectora natum
> (Hector’s Andromache, and her little son to her breast)
>
> 566 Astyanacta tenet, cuius dum maximus heros
> (she holds, Astyanax; and while the mighty hero)
>
> 567 oscula parua petit, subito perterritus infans
> (seeks his little kisses, suddenly terrified, the infant)
>
> 568 conuertit timidos materna ad pectora uultus
> (turns his frightened face toward his mother’s breast,)

Hector’s return from the fighting brings him into contact not only with those responsible for Troy’s public worship, but also with his own family. Andromache is his wife, and Astyanax their infant son. After the violence of the battlefield and the urgent appeal for divine help, this brief encounter shows the Trojan defender as a husband and father. His greatness offers no advantage here: the mighty warrior approaches affectionately, but his frightened child turns away.

The Latin brings together moments separated by a substantial conversation in Homer. In the *Iliad*, Andromache appeals to Hector not to leave her a widow and their child an orphan; Hector answers with his sense of duty and his fears for her future. Here her desire to speak is retained, but the passage moves directly to his attempt to kiss their son. Calling her “most faithful” also places her attachment to Hector at the forefront, where Homer initially introduces her through her marriage gifts and her father’s identity.

One change makes the scene especially intimate: Andromache herself holds the baby. In Homer, a nurse carries him, and it is against the nurse that he recoils. Homer also explains that the child is frightened by his father’s bronze armour and nodding helmet crest; the Latin gives the cause of his terror in the next verse. The name Astyanax means “lord of the city.” Homer explains that others call the child this because Hector alone protects Troy, while Hector himself calls him Scamandrius (*Iliad* 6.402–403). The Latin keeps only the public name, but sets its grandeur beside the helplessness of a baby seeking safety against his mother.
