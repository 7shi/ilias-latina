### 931–932 (*Iliad* 22.35–36)

> 931 Vnus tota salus in quo Troiana manebat
> (The one man in whom all Troy’s hope of safety still lay,)
>
> 932 Hector adest, quem non durae timor undique mortis,
> (Hector is there, whom fear of cruel death on every side does not …)

After the Trojan army’s retreat behind the city walls, Hector remains outside to face Achilles. Hector is the son of Priam, Troy’s king, and the city’s foremost defender. Against the mass flight at the end of the preceding book, his solitary presence marks the last resistance to Achilles’ advance. Calling him the one man on whom Troy’s safety depends makes the coming encounter more than a contest between two warriors: the city’s hopes rest with its champion.

In Homer, Priam is pleading with his son, while Hector stands before the gates, fiercely determined to fight Achilles (*Iliad* 22.35–36). The Latin compresses that scene, leaving the father’s appeal out of these verses and bringing Hector directly into view. It also adds the emphatic claim that all Trojan safety rests in him. Where Homer stresses Hector’s eagerness to fight, the Latin begins to describe his resistance to fear. The sentence continues beyond this excerpt, but already sets his resolve against the prospect of a cruel death.
