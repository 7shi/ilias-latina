### 931–932 (*Iliad* 22.35–36)

> 931 Vnus tota salus in quo Troiana manebat
> (The one man in whom all Trojan safety remained,)
>
> 932 Hector adest, quem non durae timor undique mortis,
> (Hector is at hand, whom not the fear of harsh death on every side,)

After the Trojan army’s retreat behind the city walls, Hector remains outside to face Achilles. Hector is the son of Priam, Troy’s king, and the city’s foremost defender. Against the mass flight at the end of the preceding book, his solitary presence marks the last resistance to Achilles’ advance. Calling him the one man on whom Troy’s safety depends makes the coming encounter more than a contest between two warriors: the city’s hopes rest with its champion.

In Homer, Priam is pleading with his son, while Hector stands before the gates, fiercely determined to fight Achilles (*Iliad* 22.35–36). The Latin brings Hector directly into view and adds the emphatic claim that all Trojan safety rests in him; the father’s prayers follow in the next verse. Where Homer stresses Hector’s eagerness to fight, the Latin begins to describe his resistance to fear. The sentence continues into the next verses, but already sets his resolve against the prospect of a harsh death.
