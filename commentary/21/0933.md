### 933–936 (*Iliad* 22.37–38, 22.131–135, 22.226–231)

> 933 non patriae tenuere preces, quin obuius iret
> (nor did his country’s pleas hold him back from going out to meet him)
>
> 934 et contra magnum contendere uellet Achillem.
> (and seeking to fight mighty Achilles.)
>
> 935 Quem procul ut uidit tectum caelestibus armis,
> (When he saw Achilles from afar, clad in heavenly armor,)
>
> 936 ante oculos subito uisa est Tritonia Pallas
> (Tritonia Pallas suddenly appeared before his eyes.)

The opening completes the preceding description of Hector’s resolve: neither fear of death nor his country’s appeals can keep him from confronting Achilles. In Homer, those appeals take the immediate, personal form of Priam, Hector’s father and Troy’s king, stretching out his hands and begging his son not to face this opponent. The Latin condenses that scene into a brief reference to his country’s pleas. Hector’s determination thus carries a painful contradiction: the man on whom Troy’s safety depends refuses the appeals that might preserve his life.

Achilles now comes into view wearing armor forged by Vulcan, the divine smith. This is what makes it “heavenly”: it is equipment made by a god, not merely splendid human craftsmanship. Homer describes its bronze flashing like blazing fire or the rising sun as Achilles approaches; the sight then breaks Hector’s resolve and sends him fleeing.

Tritonia Pallas is the goddess also known as Minerva or Athena, who supports Achilles. Her sudden appearance here brings divine intervention directly alongside the sight of his formidable armor. In Homer, her encounter with Hector comes later, after Achilles has pursued him around Troy. She takes the shape and voice of Hector’s brother Deiphobus and offers to stand beside him against his pursuer. That apparent promise of support is a trap. The Latin draws these widely separated moments together, moving rapidly from Hector’s defiance toward the divine deception that will help bring about his defeat.
