### 81–82 (*Iliad* 1.348–357)

> 81 inuocat aequoreae Pelides numina matris
> (Pelides invokes the divine power of his sea-dwelling mother,)
>
> 82 ne se Plistheniden contra patiatur inultum.
> (asking her not to leave him unavenged against Plisthenides.)

Prevented from attacking Agamemnon, Achilles now seeks help from his mother, the sea-goddess Thetis. “Pelides” means “son of Peleus,” Achilles’ mortal father. “Plisthenides” names Agamemnon through another figure in his ancestry, Plisthenes, rather than through Atreus, whom Homer names as his father. The appeal therefore continues the same quarrel: Achilles has been restrained, but his grievance remains, and he now seeks divine redress for the insult he cannot avenge by force.

The Latin reduces a scene of isolation and grief to a single prayer. In Homer, Briseis leaves unwillingly with Agamemnon’s messengers, while Achilles withdraws from his companions and sits weeping beside the sea. Looking across the water, he stretches out his hands to his mother. The Latin retains the appeal to Thetis and her connection with the sea, but omits the setting, the gesture and the tears.

Homer also gives Achilles’ complaint a reason beyond the immediate loss of Briseis. Knowing that his life will be short, he argues that Zeus should at least have granted him honour; instead, Agamemnon has dishonoured him by taking his prize (*Iliad* 1.352–356). Briseis represents the public recognition his fighting has earned. The Latin leaves this argument unstated and concentrates on the desired outcome: Achilles must not remain unavenged. His anger has not subsided with Athena’s intervention; it has found a new means of acting.
