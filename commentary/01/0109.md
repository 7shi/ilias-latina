### 109–110 (*Iliad* 1.601–604, 1.606–611)

> 109 et dapibus diui curant sua corpora largis;
> (and the gods refresh their bodies with a lavish feast;)
>
> 110 inde petunt thalamos iucundaque dona quietis.
> (then they seek their chambers and the pleasant gifts of rest.)

The gods’ quarrel gives way to the familiar comforts of food and sleep. Their abundant meal and peaceful retirement complete the picture of harmony restored on Olympus. Rest is something welcome and generous, a “gift” enjoyed by beings who, despite their immortality, share the bodily pleasures of human life.

The Latin compresses Homer’s richly furnished evening into two simple actions: feasting and going to bed. In the *Iliad*, the feast lasts all day until sunset, accompanied by Apollo’s lyre and the alternating songs of the Muses, the goddesses of song (*Iliad* 1.601–604). Here, with sunset already mentioned, the meal becomes part of the brief evening conclusion. Homer then sends each god to a separate home built by Hephaestus, the divine craftsman, before following Jupiter to bed with his wife Juno beside him (*Iliad* 1.606–611). The Latin leaves out both the individual homes and this final glimpse of the reconciled couple, bringing all the gods together under the same quiet ending.
