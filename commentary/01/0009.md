### 9–22 (*Iliad* 1.8–22)

> 9 Quis deus hos ira tristi contendere iussit?
> (What god made these men clash in bitter anger?)
>
> 10 Latonae et magni proles Iouis. Ille Pelasgum
> (The son of Latona and mighty Jupiter. He, of the Pelasgians,)
>
> 11 infestam regi pestem in praecordia misit
> (sent a destructive passion into the king’s heart)
>
> 12 implicuitque graui Danaorum corpora morbo.
> (and entangled the bodies of the Danaans in grievous sickness.)
>
> 13 Nam quondam Chryses, sollemni tempora uitta
> (For once Chryses, his temples with a sacred fillet)
>
> 14 implicitus, raptae fleuit solacia natae
> (bound, wept for the comfort his stolen daughter had given him,)
>
> 15 inuisosque dies inuisaque tempora noctis
> (and hateful days and hateful hours of night)
>
> 16 egit et assiduis impleuit questibus auras.
> (he passed, and filled the air with ceaseless lamentation.)
>
> 17 Postquam nulla dies animum maerore leuabat
> (When no day relieved his heart of sorrow,)
>
> 18 nullaque lenibant patrios solacia fletus,
> (and no comfort soothed his fatherly tears,)
>
> 19 castra petit Danaum genibusque affusus Atridae
> (he sought the camp of the Danaans and, falling at Atrides’ knees,)
>
> 20 per superos regnique decus miserabilis orat,
> (piteously begged him, by the gods above and the honor of his kingship,)
>
> 21 ut sibi causa suae reddatur nata salutis.
> (to restore his daughter to him, on whom his own life depended.)
>
> 22 Dona simul praefert. Vincuntur fletibus eius
> (At the same time he offers gifts. Won over by his tears,)

The question takes up the opening quarrel between Achilles and Agamemnon, but its answer leads back to an earlier wrong. The unnamed son of Latona and Jupiter is Apollo, the god whose priest, Chryses, has lost his daughter to the Greek captors. Pelasgians and Danaans are alternative names for the Greeks; their king, Atrides, is Agamemnon. Chryses’ daughter, usually called Chryseis, has been taken in a raid and allotted to Agamemnon as a captive prize. Her father now comes to recover her.

Apollo’s intervention links the king’s private desire with the suffering of his followers. He sends destructive passion for Chryseis into Agamemnon’s heart and sickness through the Greek army. The king’s attachment to the captive will emerge in his refusal to surrender her: what her father needs for his own happiness, the king wishes to keep for himself. This differs from Homer’s immediate explanation, in which Apollo sends the plague because Agamemnon has dishonored his priest (*Iliad* 1.9–12). The Latin retains that connection between an affront to Chryses and disaster for the army, but also makes the king’s consuming desire part of the god’s action.

Having announced the sickness, the narrative turns back to the father’s grief and his appeal. Here the Latin expands rather than abridges. Homer brings Chryses directly to the Greek ships with ransom and the emblems of Apollo’s priesthood. The Latin first imagines the days and nights of sorrow that drive him there. Both daylight and darkness have become hateful; time passes without bringing relief. His daughter was his comfort, and without her he feels that his own life cannot be sustained. This added scene makes the recovery of a captive not merely a transaction between priest and king, but a desperate attempt to restore a broken family.

Chryses’ sacred fillet identifies his religious office, yet his conduct stresses his helplessness. In Homer he carries Apollo’s sacred bands upon a golden scepter and addresses the Greeks, especially the two sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus. Here he falls at Agamemnon’s knees. The Latin concentrates the encounter upon the grieving father and the one ruler who can return his child. His appeal invokes both divine authority and the honor proper to kingship: compassion would uphold Agamemnon’s dignity, not diminish it.

The gifts he brings are a ransom. Homer gives Chryses a formal speech wishing the Greeks victory over Troy and a safe return home, then asking them to release his daughter and respect Apollo (*Iliad* 1.17–21). The Latin compresses this into an indirect plea and gives greater force to his tears. Those won over by them, named in the next verse, are the soldiers, not the king. Their sympathy prepares a contrast between the response of the army and that of its commander.
