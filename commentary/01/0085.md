### 85 (*Iliad* 1.422, 1.496–497)

> 85 abstineat dextram ac congressibus; inde per auras
> (he should keep his hand from weapons and combat; then through the air…)

The line completes Thetis’ advice to Achilles: he is to withhold his strength from battle. In Homer, she also explicitly tells him to remain angry with the Greeks (*Iliad* 1.422). The Latin keeps the practical consequence of that anger—his refusal to fight—before turning from the son’s withdrawal to the mother’s journey.

“Then through the air” begins Thetis’ ascent to seek divine help for Achilles. She will appeal to Jupiter, the king of the gods, whom Homer calls Zeus. Homer separates her counsel from this journey by a twelve-day wait: Zeus and the other gods are away visiting the Ethiopians, a distant people, and she must await their return. When the time comes, she rises from the sea and ascends at dawn to heaven and Olympus, the gods’ home (*Iliad* 1.496–497). The Latin moves directly from her advice to her departure, compressing the wait and bringing Achilles’ grievance swiftly before the gods.
