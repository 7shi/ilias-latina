### 83–84 (*Iliad* 1.357–360, 1.421–422)

> 83 At Thetis audita nati prece deserit undas
> (But Thetis, hearing her son’s prayer, leaves the waves,)
>
> 84 castraque Myrmidonum iuxta petit et monet armis
> (and makes for the Myrmidons’ camp nearby, and warns him from arms)

Thetis answers the appeal at once, coming out of the sea to her son Achilles. The Myrmidons are the warriors he has brought to Troy and commands within the larger Greek army. Their camp places the meeting close to Achilles’ own followers, among whom he will remain while refusing to take part in the fighting. The advice begun here continues into the next verse.

In Homer, Thetis hears Achilles while sitting in the depths of the sea beside her aged father. She rises “like mist” and sits before her weeping son (*Iliad* 1.358–360). The Latin preserves her swift response but leaves out the underwater scene, the mist and Achilles’ tears. Where Homer brings mother and son face to face in his grief, the abridgement moves directly from the prayer to her arrival and counsel.

A long conversation in Homer separates that arrival from her advice to withdraw. Achilles recounts his quarrel with Agamemnon and asks Thetis to seek Zeus’s help; only later does she tell him to remain beside the ships, maintain his anger against the Greeks and abstain entirely from battle (*Iliad* 1.421–422). The Latin draws these moments together. Her counsel does not reconcile Achilles with Agamemnon: it supports his refusal to fight. Prevented from avenging the insult by violence, he can still make his absence felt by the army that depends on him.
