### 93–96 (*Iliad* 1.517, 1.522–523, 1.531–532)

> 93 Iuppiter haec contra: "Tristes depone querelas,
> (Jupiter replied: “Set aside your sorrowful complaints,)
>
> 94 magni diua maris, mecum labor iste manebit.
> (goddess of the mighty sea; I shall take care of this matter.)
>
> 95 Tu solare tui maerentia pectora nati."
> (Comfort the grieving heart of your son.”)
>
> 96 Dixit. At illa leues caeli delapsa per auras
> (He spoke. And she, gliding down through the light air of heaven,)

Jupiter answers Thetis’ demand for vengeance with reassurance. His address to her as “goddess of the mighty sea” acknowledges her divine standing, while his promise makes Achilles’ grievance his own responsibility. The matter will remain in his care: Thetis can leave its resolution to him and turn to comforting her son. Yet, as in her appeal, the means of redress remain unstated.

The Latin makes this exchange much smoother than it is in Homer. There Zeus responds with a deep groan, troubled by the quarrel that helping Thetis may provoke with his wife, Hera. He tells Thetis to leave before Hera notices her, then promises that “these things shall be my concern until I bring them to pass” (*Iliad* 1.523). The Latin preserves that assurance but removes the anxiety surrounding it. It also omits the solemn nod with which Zeus confirms his promise in Homer, making Olympus itself tremble (*Iliad* 1.528–530). Here Jupiter’s brief declaration is enough.

The instruction to comfort Achilles has no counterpart in Homer’s reply. It sustains the emphasis on the bond between mother and son established by Thetis’ appeal: Jupiter takes charge of the wrong, while she is entrusted with the sufferer. She then begins her descent from heaven. Homer sends her plunging from Olympus into the deep sea; the Latin leaves her here still passing through the air, with her movement continuing into the next passage.
