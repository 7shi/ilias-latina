### 44 (*Iliad* 1.43)

> 44 Dixerat. Ille sui uatis prece motus acerbis
> (He had spoken. The god, moved by his priest’s prayer, with bitter)

Chryses has finished his appeal, and the story turns from the suffering father to Apollo’s response. “His priest” recalls the bond on which Chryses has placed his hopes: he is not merely a distressed petitioner but someone devoted to the god’s service. After his anguished questions about divine neglect, the statement that Apollo is moved begins to answer his fear that his prayer will go unheeded.

Homer marks the same turning point more simply: “So he spoke in prayer, and Phoebus Apollo heard him” (*Iliad* 1.43). The Latin makes the prayer’s effect explicit: Apollo is not only listening but moved to respond. Yet this does not mean that he accepts Chryses’ desperate invitation to strike him rather than let his daughter suffer. The sentence continues beyond this verse, leaving the form of the god’s intervention still to be revealed.
