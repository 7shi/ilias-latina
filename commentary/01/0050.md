### 50–51 (*Iliad* 1.54, 1.62–64)

> 50 cum Danaum proceres in coetum clarus Achilles
> (when renowned Achilles calls the leaders of the Danai to an assembly)
>
> 51 conuocat et causas hortatur pestis iniquae
> (and urges the causes of the cruel plague)

On the tenth day, the army begins to respond to its suffering. Achilles, a leading warrior among the Greeks, takes the initiative by calling a meeting. The “Danai” are the Greek forces besieging Troy, but here it is specifically their leaders who gather. In Homer, Achilles summons the people as a whole to the assembly (*Iliad* 1.54); the Latin concentrates the response in the hands of the chiefs.

The search for the plague’s causes means discovering why a god is angry and how that anger can be appeased. Homer gives Achilles a direct speech proposing that they consult “a seer or a priest, or even an interpreter of dreams,” who might explain Apollo’s wrath (*Iliad* 1.62–64). The Latin reduces these proposals to a brief report of his appeal, leaving out the different kinds of religious expert.

For the reader, the cause is already clear: Apollo has answered the prayer of Chryses, whose daughter Agamemnon refused to release. The army must now uncover that connection for itself. Achilles’ intervention turns the story from the devastation in the camp toward a public inquiry—one that will bring the commander’s conduct under scrutiny.
