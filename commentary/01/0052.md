### 52–57 (*Iliad* 1.62–64, 1.68–72, 1.74–91, 1.93–100)

> 52 edere Thestoriden. Tunc Calchas numina diuum
> (Thestorides to reveal. Then Calchas the will of the gods)
>
> 53 consulit et causam pariter finemque malorum
> (consults, and both the cause and the remedy for their troubles)
>
> 54 inuenit effarique uerens ope tutus Achillis
> (discovers; and, afraid to speak out, but safe under Achilles’ protection,)
>
> 55 haec ait: "Infesti placemus numina Phoebi
> (he says: “Let us appease the divine power of angry Phoebus)
>
> 56 reddamusque pio castam Chryseida patri,
> (and return chaste Chryseis to her devout father,)
>
> 57 si uolumus, Danai, portus intrare salutis."
> (if we wish, Danaans, to enter the harbors of safety.”)

Achilles’ appeal now singles out the man who can explain the plague. “Thestorides” means “son of Thestor”: this is Calchas, the Greek army’s seer, whose knowledge allows him to interpret the gods’ wishes. In Homer, Achilles initially proposes consulting a religious expert without naming anyone, and Calchas then rises to speak. The Latin makes the approach more direct and replaces Homer’s account of the seer’s extraordinary knowledge—of past, present, and future—and his guidance of the fleet to Troy with the simple statement that he consults the gods.

Knowing the answer, however, does not make it safe to speak. Calchas must expose the wrongdoing of Agamemnon, the army’s commander. Homer develops his fear through an exchange with Achilles: Calchas asks for protection against a powerful king’s resentment, and Achilles promises that no one will harm him, even if he names Agamemnon (*Iliad* 1.74–91). The Latin compresses this negotiation into “afraid to speak, but safe under Achilles’ protection.” It preserves the essential tension: religious authority needs a warrior’s support before it can challenge political power.

Calchas’ speech concentrates on the remedy rather than the accusation. Phoebus is Apollo, who sent the plague after Agamemnon refused to release Chryseis to her father Chryses, Apollo’s priest. Calling the father “devout” recalls his relationship with the offended god, while “chaste” presents the captive daughter as deserving respect. Homer’s Calchas explicitly blames Agamemnon and requires the girl’s return without payment, together with a sacrifice at Chryse (*Iliad* 1.93–100). Here those details disappear. The seer instead addresses the Danaans—the Greeks—as a community: “Let us appease” the god and “return” the girl.

The “harbors of safety” are not a destination for the army’s ships, but an image of deliverance from the plague. For an army encamped beside its fleet, the metaphor makes recovery imaginable as reaching shelter after danger at sea. Calchas has shown the way to safety; the question is now whether the commander will surrender the captive whose detention endangers them all.
