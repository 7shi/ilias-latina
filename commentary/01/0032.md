### 32–43 (*Iliad* 1.37, 1.39–42)

> 32 "Quid coluisse mihi tua numina, Delphice, prodest
> (“What good has it done me to worship your divinity, Delphic god,)
>
> 33 aut castam uitam multos duxisse per annos?
> (or to have led a pure life for so many years?)
>
> 34 Quidue iuuat sacros posuisse altaribus ignes,
> (What good is it to have kindled sacred fires on your altars,)
>
> 35 si tuus externo iam spernor ab hoste sacerdos?
> (if I, your priest, am now scorned by a foreign enemy?)
>
> 36 En, haec desertae redduntur dona senectae?
> (Look, are these the gifts bestowed on my forsaken old age?)
>
> 37 Si gratus tibi sum, sim te sub uindice tutus.
> (If I am dear to you, let me be safe under your protection.)
>
> 38 Aut si qua, ut luerem sub acerbo crimine poenas,
> (Or if, so that I must pay the penalty on a bitter charge,)
>
> 39 inscius admisi, cur o tua dextera cessat?
> (I have unwittingly done wrong, why does your right hand stay idle?)
>
> 40 Posce sacros arcus, in me tua derige tela:
> (Call for your sacred bow; aim your arrows at me:)
>
> 41 auctor mortis erit certe deus. Ecce, merentem
> (at least a god will be the cause of my death. Look, strike)
>
> 42 fige patrem; cur nata luit peccata parentis
> (the father who deserves it; why does the daughter pay for her parent’s sins)
>
> 43 atque hostis duri patitur miseranda cubile?."
> (and, poor girl, endure the bed of a cruel enemy?”)

Chryses’ grief now becomes a challenge to the god he has faithfully served. The “Delphic god” is Apollo, whose sanctuary at Delphi was famous for its oracle. After Agamemnon’s rejection, the priest asks whether a lifetime of worship, purity, and sacrifice counts for anything. His appeal joins two claims upon Apollo: he is the god’s priest, whose humiliation should concern his divine protector, and an old father left desolate by the loss of his daughter. His “forsaken old age” means more than loneliness in the present; captivity has deprived him of the daughter whose presence would sustain his remaining years.

The Latin preserves the underlying exchange of Homer’s prayer: Chryses recalls his service to Apollo and asks the god to act in return. But its tone and its desired outcome are strikingly different. In Homer, the priest recalls roofing Apollo’s temple and burning offerings of bulls and goats, then asks, “May the Danaans pay for my tears with your arrows” (*Iliad* 1.42). The Danaans are the Greeks, whose army holds his daughter captive. Here the particular offerings become sacred altar fires, while the added claim to a pure life makes the appeal a question about the justice of his suffering.

Most importantly, Chryses turns Apollo’s arrows upon himself. If he still enjoys the god’s favour, he asks for protection; if he has unknowingly offended, he asks the god to kill him directly. This is not a clear admission of guilt. It is the desperate argument of someone unable to reconcile faithful service with abandonment: if there must be punishment, let it fall upon the supposed offender. Death at Apollo’s hands would at least give his suffering a divine author, rather than leave him helpless before a human enemy.

The prayer ends not with the priest’s own humiliation but with his daughter’s captivity. The cruel enemy is Agamemnon, who has refused to release her. To “endure” his bed presents her sexual subjection as suffering imposed upon her, not as a willing union. The Latin thus replaces Homer’s explicit demand for vengeance on the Greek army with a father’s anguished question: why should an innocent daughter suffer for whatever wrong her father may have done?
