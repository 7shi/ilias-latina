### 58–64 (*Iliad* 1.101–108, 1.131–147, 1.173–187, 1.149–171, 1.225–244, 1.304–305, 1.116–117, 1.308–311, 1.440–441)

> 58 Dixerat. Exarsit subito uiolentia regis:
> (He had spoken. Suddenly the king’s fury flared up:)
>
> 59 Thestoriden dictis primum compellat amaris
> (first he addresses Thestorides with bitter words)
>
> 60 mendacemque uocat. Tum magnum incusat Achillem
> (and calls him a liar. Then he accuses great Achilles)
>
> 61 inque uicem ducis inuicti conuicia suffert.
> (and in turn endures the undefeated leader’s insults.)
>
> 62 Confremuere omnes. Tandem clamore represso
> (Everyone raised an uproar. At last, when the shouting was quieted,)
>
> 63 cogitur inuitos aeger dimittere amores
> (he is forced, in anguish, to release his unwilling beloved)
>
> 64 intactamque pio reddit Chryseida patri
> (and returns Chryseis untouched to her devout father.)

Calchas’ fear is immediately justified. The king is Agamemnon, commander of the Greek army, and his first response to the proposed remedy is to attack the man who announced it. Thestorides is Calchas, named once again as the son of Thestor. Although the seer’s speech avoided directly accusing Agamemnon, its demand was unmistakable: the king must surrender Chryseis if the army is to escape the plague. In Homer, Agamemnon rises with eyes like blazing fire and calls Calchas a “prophet of evils” who has never told him anything welcome (*Iliad* 1.101–108). The Latin concentrates that anger into a sudden flare of fury and sharpens the complaint into an accusation of lying.

The quarrel then spreads from the seer to his protector, Achilles, the “undefeated leader.” A long and consequential confrontation becomes a brief exchange of accusations and insults. In Homer, the dispute concerns not only Agamemnon’s desire to retain Chryseis but also the standing of the two warriors. Captive women were allotted as prizes of war, publicly marking the recipients’ honor. Agamemnon demands compensation for losing his prize and threatens to take Achilles’ captive, Briseis, instead. Achilles protests that he bears the greater burden of fighting while Agamemnon receives the greater rewards. Those arguments, threats, and competing claims to authority disappear here into the bare fact that Achilles answers the king’s abuse with his own. Agamemnon can command the army, but he cannot silence its greatest fighter.

The surrounding crowd also becomes more audible. Homer describes the two men ending their bitter exchange and dismissing the assembly (*Iliad* 1.304–305); the Latin gives everyone a collective uproar before quiet returns. It does not specify what they shout or whose side they take. Nevertheless, the movement from public turmoil to the king’s forced surrender makes his decision seem anything but freely generous. Homer allows Agamemnon to say that he would rather see his people safe than destroyed (*Iliad* 1.116–117). Here the emphasis falls instead on compulsion and personal anguish.

“His unwilling beloved” means Chryseis herself: Agamemnon desires her, but she does not return his love. His distress is thus set against the captive’s lack of consent. The final line answers Calchas’ demand almost word for word, replacing “chaste” with the more explicit “untouched” and again calling her father, Chryses, “devout.” Her release restores a daughter to her father as well as addressing the offense against Apollo. The immediate remedy has been secured, but the clash between Agamemnon and Achilles remains unresolved.
