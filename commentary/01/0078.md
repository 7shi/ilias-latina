### 78–80 (*Iliad* 1.194–200)

> 78 Quod nisi casta manu Pallas tenuisset Achillem,
> (But had chaste Pallas not held Achilles back with her hand,)
>
> 79 turpem caecus amor famam liquisset in aeuum
> (blind love would have brought lasting disgrace)
>
> 80 gentibus Argolicis. Contempta uoce minisque
> (upon the Argive peoples. With words and threats disregarded,)

Only divine intervention prevents the quarrel from becoming bloodshed. Pallas is Athena, the virgin goddess associated with wisdom and warfare, who physically restrains Achilles. The disgrace she averts would fall not merely on Achilles but on the “Argive peoples”: the Greeks who have come together to fight at Troy. Violence between their greatest warrior and their commander would stain the reputation of the whole expedition.

The Latin preserves Athena’s decisive intervention but greatly compresses her arrival. In Homer, Hera sends her down from heaven because she loves and cares for both men. Athena stands behind Achilles and catches him by his fair hair, appearing to him alone; no one else can see her. Startled, he turns and recognizes the goddess, whose eyes shine terribly (*Iliad* 1.194–200). Here those details disappear into the single action of her restraining hand. Following the altered sequence of the Latin narrative, she stops Achilles after Briseis has been taken from him, rather than while her seizure is still a threat.

“Blind love” also gives the confrontation a different emphasis. The preceding lines presented Achilles as defending the rewards and recognition earned by his service; now his attachment to Briseis becomes a passion that threatens to disgrace the Greeks. This explicit moral judgment is added by the Latin poet, not expressed in Homer’s account of Athena’s arrival. Later, Homer’s Achilles does declare, “I will not fight with my hands for the girl’s sake” (*Iliad* 1.298). That refusal anticipates the avoidance of further violence over Briseis, but it is not itself a condemnation of love as an unworthy motive. The Latin makes the contrast sharper: unchecked passion threatens catastrophe, and the goddess supplies the restraint Achilles lacks.
