### 1–8 (*Iliad* 1.1–7)

> 1 Iram pande mihi Pelidae, Diua, superbi
> (Goddess, tell me of the wrath of proud Pelides,)
>
> 2 Tristia quae miseris iniecit funera Grais
> (which inflicted grievous deaths on the wretched Greeks)
>
> 3 Atque animas fortes heroum tradidit Orco
> (and handed over the valiant souls of heroes to Orcus,)
>
> 4 Latrantumque dedit rostris uolucrumque trahendos
> (and gave to the jaws of barking dogs and the beaks of birds, to be torn,)
>
> 5 Illorum exsangues, inhumatis ossibus, artus.
> (their lifeless limbs, their bones unburied.)
>
> 6 Confiebat enim summi sententia regis,
> (For the will of the highest king was being fulfilled,)
>
> 7 protulerant* ex quo discordia pectora pugnas,
> (ever since their hostile hearts had stirred up strife—)
>
> 8 Sceptriger Atrides et bello clarus Achilles.
> (sceptre-bearing Atrides and Achilles, famed in war.)

The poem begins not with the outbreak of a war but with anger within an army. The Greeks have sailed to attack Troy, a city in northwestern Asia Minor. Their foremost warrior, Achilles, is introduced as Pelides, “son of Peleus.” His opponent in this quarrel is Atrides, “son of Atreus”: here this means Agamemnon, the king who commands the Greek expedition. The closing descriptions set their different claims to authority beside one another. Agamemnon carries the sceptre of kingship; Achilles possesses outstanding renown in battle. Their conflict will bring disaster upon their own side.

The goddess addressed at the opening is the Muse, the divine source of the poet’s knowledge and song. Like Homer, the Latin poet asks her to tell of Achilles’ anger and immediately reveals its terrible consequences. But the emphasis shifts slightly. Homer calls the anger “destructive” and speaks of the countless sufferings it brought the Greeks (*Iliad* 1.1–2); the Latin calls Pelides himself “proud” and concentrates on death. The contrast between the heroes’ valiant souls and their helpless bodies makes the loss especially stark. Orcus is the Roman name used here for the realm or power of the dead, corresponding to Homer’s Hades. While the souls pass below, the bodies remain exposed to scavengers. The Latin expands this physical horror with lifeless limbs and unburied bones: these warriors lose not only their lives but also the burial and honour their companions owe them.

Above the human quarrel stands the will of the “highest king”—not Agamemnon, but Jupiter, the Roman counterpart of Zeus. The Latin preserves Homer’s declaration that the god’s purpose was being fulfilled (*Iliad* 1.5), without yet explaining what that purpose is. This opening therefore gives the reader the shape of the coming tragedy before telling its causes: two powerful men fall into conflict, their followers suffer, and events unfold within a divine design whose workings remain to be revealed.
