### 74–77 (*Iliad* 1.188–194)

> 74 At ferus Aeacides nudato protinus ense
> (But fierce Aeacides, his sword at once drawn,)
>
> 75 tendit in Atriden et, ni sibi reddat honestae
> (advances on Atrides, and, unless he restores to him the honorable)
>
> 76 munera militiae, letum crudele minatur,
> (rewards of service, he threatens him with a cruel death,)
>
> 77 nec minus ille parat contra defendere se ense.
> (and the other likewise prepares to defend himself with his sword.)

Agamemnon’s seizure of Briseis now threatens to turn the quarrel into bloodshed. Aeacides is Achilles, here named as a descendant of his grandfather Aeacus; Atrides is Agamemnon, the son of Atreus. Achilles demands the return of his reward for military service: Briseis, the captive woman allotted to him from the spoils of war. The language of honorable service makes clear that his grievance concerns more than personal attachment. His prize represents recognition of what he has contributed to the army, and taking it away publicly denies him that recognition. Neither man is willing to yield: Achilles threatens to kill his commander, while Agamemnon prepares to meet force with force.

The Latin places this confrontation after Briseis has been taken, making it an immediate response to her loss. In Homer, it occurs earlier, during the assembly, when Agamemnon has threatened the seizure but has not yet carried it out. Homer also gives more space to Achilles’ inward struggle. He weighs two alternatives: draw his sword, scatter those around him and kill Agamemnon, or restrain his anger (*Iliad* 1.188–192). He is beginning to draw the weapon when Athena arrives (*Iliad* 1.193–194). The Latin replaces that hesitation with decisive outward action: Achilles has his sword drawn and advances, explicitly threatening death unless his prize is restored. Agamemnon’s preparation to defend himself is also added. The compressed scene thus brings the two leaders to the brink of an armed clash, turning a dispute over honor into an immediate danger to their own side.
