### 45–49 (*Iliad* 1.44–54)

> 45 luctibus infestat Danaos pestemque per omnes
> (griefs harries the Danaans, and a plague among all)
>
> 46 immittit populos: uulgus ruit undique Graium
> (the peoples he sends: the throng of the Greeks falls on every side.)
>
> 47 uixque rogis superest tellus, uix ignibus aer,
> (Scarcely is there earth enough for the pyres, scarcely air enough for the flames;)
>
> 48 deerat ager tumulis. Iam noctis sidera nonae
> (there was no land left for burial mounds. Already the ninth night’s stars)
>
> 49 transierant decimusque dies patefecerat orbem,
> (had passed, and the tenth day had revealed the world,)

Apollo’s answer to his priest’s prayer now becomes clear. Rather than strike Chryses himself, he sends a plague against the army whose commander has dishonoured him. “Danaans” is another name for the Greeks besieging Troy. The suffering spreads beyond the man responsible: the whole army pays for Agamemnon’s refusal to release the priest’s daughter.

The Latin moves directly from prayer to devastation. Homer first shows Apollo descending from Olympus in anger, his arrows rattling on his shoulders as he comes “like night” (*Iliad* 1.47). He sits apart from the ships and shoots; his arrows strike the mules and dogs before turning against the men. Here that ominous approach and the successive attacks disappear. The god’s action is named plainly as plague, and attention falls immediately on the people dying everywhere.

Yet the shorter account makes the aftermath more expansive. Homer says that “the crowded pyres of the dead burned continually” (*Iliad* 1.52). The Latin imagines the dead overwhelming the space available to receive them: earth can scarcely hold the funeral pyres, air can scarcely contain their flames, and no ground remains for burial mounds. This is a picture of loss beyond the survivors’ capacity to manage, not merely a count of casualties.

The passage then carries the story through the ninth night to the tenth morning. In Homer, the god’s arrows range through the camp for nine days; the Latin marks that interval through the passing stars. Daylight reveals the world again, but does not yet promise relief. The unfinished sentence leads onward to the army’s response.
