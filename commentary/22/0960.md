### 960–967 (*Iliad* 22.289–291)

> 960 Exclamant Danai. Contra Priameius heros
> (The Danai shout. In reply, the Priameian hero)
>
> 961 uibratum iaculum Vulcania torquet in arma.
> (brandishes his javelin and hurls it against the Vulcanian armor.)
>
> 962 Nec successus adest: nam duro inflectitur auro
> (But he has no success: against the hard gold the blade bends)
>
> 963 dissiluitque mucro. Gemuerunt agmina Troum.
> (and the sword blade shatters. The ranks of the Troes groan.)
>
> 964 Concurrunt iterum collatis fortiter armis
> (Again they charge bravely together, clashing their weapons,)
>
> 965 inque uicem duros euitant comminus enses.
> (and at close quarters each dodges the other’s hard sword.)
>
> 966 Nec sufferre ualet ultra sortemque supremam
> (Hector can no longer withstand his final fate)
>
> 967 stantemque Aeaciden defectis uiribus Hector.
> (or Aeacides standing before him, his strength exhausted.)

Hector answers Achilles’ unsuccessful cast with a throw of his own, but the two men survive for different reasons. Hector has escaped through alertness and agility; Achilles is protected by extraordinary armor. The “Priameian hero” is Hector, son of Priam, Troy’s king. His opponent is called Aeacides because Achilles is a descendant of Aeacus, his grandfather. The Vulcanian armor is the armor made for Achilles by Vulcan, the divine craftsman, and already described in the Latin poem as fashioned from gold. Its hard surface defeats Hector’s attack.

The cries surrounding this exchange make the duel a struggle watched by two peoples. The Danai are the Greeks, while the Troes are the Trojans, whose groan registers the failure of their champion’s weapon. These collective reactions are additions to the corresponding Homeric verses. In the *Iliad*, Hector’s spear strikes the middle of Achilles’ shield without missing, but rebounds far away, leaving Hector angry (*Iliad* 22.289–291). The Latin instead emphasizes the weapon’s failure against the armor and lets the Trojan ranks express the distress that Homer locates in Hector himself.

The shattered sword blade makes this failure more destructive than Homer’s rebound, though the transition from a hurled javelin to a sword is abrupt. The narrative then moves directly into another vigorous clash. Neither fighter immediately lands a wound: they exchange attacks at close range and evade one another’s swords. This sustained, mutual sword-fighting has no counterpart in the Homeric lines being retold. It briefly presents the opponents as evenly matched in courage and skill, even though Achilles’ armor has already exposed the inequality between them.

That appearance of balance cannot last. The final sentence brings Hector’s dwindling physical strength together with his approaching death. He faces both an armed opponent still standing before him and a fate he can no longer resist. The passage does not yet describe the fatal blow; it ends with the exhaustion that makes Hector’s earlier resourcefulness insufficient to save him.
