### 1002–1003 (*Iliad* 22.405–411)

> 1002 Laetantur Danai, plangunt sua uulnera Troes
> (The Danaans rejoice; the Trojans lament their wounds)
>
> 1003 et pariter captos deflent cum funere muros.
> (and, with his death, mourn their walls as captured too.)

The outrage against Hector’s body is followed by the opposing responses of the two armies. The Danaans, the Greeks besieging Troy, rejoice in their victory; the Trojans lament the hurt inflicted on them. Their “wounds” embrace the losses and suffering of the whole community. Hector’s death is more than the loss of one warrior: with their foremost defender gone, the Trojans mourn as though their city has already fallen. The walls have not yet been captured, but his death seems to seal their fate.

The Latin compresses Homer’s scene of mourning into a collective lament and adds the Greeks’ rejoicing as a sharp contrast. In the *Iliad*, Hector’s mother tears her hair and casts away her veil at the sight of her son’s body being dragged away; his father cries out, and the people throughout Troy join their grief. Their cries sound as though “all lofty Ilios were burning from top to bottom” (*Iliad* 22.410–411). The Latin leaves out the parents’ individual gestures and the image of fire, but preserves the connection between Hector’s death and Troy’s destruction. Mourning the man and mourning the city become one act.
