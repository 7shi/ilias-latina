Hector’s parents watch from Troy’s walls as their son approaches death, seeing their own doom bound up with his. Minerva, called Tritonia and Pallas, appears to Hector in the likeness of his brother Deiphobus. Deceived into believing that his brother is there to protect him, Hector faces Achilles, while the goddess turns her favor back to the Greeks. The two heroes exchange spear-casts and fight fiercely at close quarters with swords and shields. Hector dodges Achilles’ spear, but his own attack fails against Achilles’ divinely made, “Vulcanian” armor: the point bends against its hard gold and his sword blade shatters. They renew their close combat, but Hector’s strength gives out. Retreating, he looks for his brother’s help, finds none, and realizes the deception. Exhausted and with darkness covering his eyes, he nevertheless continues to fight, suppressing his groans. Achilles presses him back and drives a spear through the middle of his throat. The Greeks rejoice, while the Trojans lament.

The dying Hector asks Achilles to return his body to his grieving parents, promising that his father Priam will ransom it with abundant gold. He appeals to Priam’s greatness as a ruler feared by Greece and, above all, to Achilles’ sympathy for a bereaved father: Achilles should think of his own father Peleus when considering Priam, and of his own son Pyrrhus when considering Hector. Achilles refuses. He declares that he would tear Hector apart and eat him himself if nature permitted, and threatens to leave his body for beasts, birds, and dogs. This, he says, will bring joy to the spirit of Patroclus, if the dead can feel. Hector dies while Achilles is speaking. Still unsatisfied, Achilles ties the corpse to his chariot and drags it by the feet three times around Troy’s walls before taking the dust-defiled body back to the Greek army.

Hector is named directly and also called the “Priameian hero,” “Priamides,” and the “son of Priamus”; he is described as clever, ill-fated, and pitiable. His father appears as Priam or Priamus, “that leader of leaders,” while his mother remains unnamed. Achilles is called “Aeacides,” marking his descent from Aeacus, and the “Nereian hero,” referring to his mother, the sea-goddess Thetis; he is also described as fierce, great, mighty, and unyielding. The Greeks are called the Danai or Danaans, and Greece is called Graecia; the Trojans are called the Troes. Deiphobus is Hector’s brother, whose appearance Pallas assumes, not an actual helper in the combat. Peleus and Pyrrhus enter only through Hector’s appeal, and the dead Patroclus through Achilles’ explanation of his vengeance.

The book ends with Hector’s body in Greek hands. The Danaans celebrate, while the Trojans mourn Hector and, in his death, lament their city’s walls as though Troy itself had already fallen.
