### 980–987 (*Iliad* 22.338–343, 24.486–487)

> 980 "En concede meos miseris genitoribus artus,
> (Come, give my body to my wretched parents,)
>
> 981 quos pater infelix multo mercabitur auro:
> (which my unhappy father will ransom with much gold:)
>
> 982 dona feres uictor. Priami nunc filius orat,
> (you will receive gifts as victor. Now the son of Priamus begs you,)
>
> 983 te Priamus, dux ille ducum, quem Graecia solum
> (Priamus himself begs you, that leader of leaders whom alone Graecia)
>
> 984 pertimuit: si, nec precibus nec munere uictus,
> (greatly feared. If, won over neither by prayers nor by gifts,)
>
> 985 nec lacrimis miseri nec clara gente moueris,
> (you are moved neither by a wretched man’s tears nor by noble lineage,)
>
> 986 afflicti miserere patris: moueat tua Peleus
> (have pity on a stricken father: let Peleus move your)
>
> 987 pectora pro Priamo, pro nostro corpore Pyrrhus."
> (heart for Priamus, and Pyrrhus for me, Priamus’ son.”)

Hector’s last appeal is not for his life but for the return of his body. He asks Achilles to let his parents recover their dead son, offering the gold his father will pay as ransom. “You will receive gifts as victor” acknowledges that the contest is over: Achilles can grant this request without surrendering any part of his triumph.

In Homer, Hector explicitly begs Achilles not to leave him for the dogs beside the Greek ships. He asks that his body be sent home so that the Trojan men and women can give him funeral rites (*Iliad* 22.338–343). The Latin leaves those horrors and ceremonies unspoken, concentrating instead on the bereaved family. Both parents appear at first, but the father soon dominates the appeal. Priamus is Troy’s king as well as Hector’s father; Graecia means Greece, here standing for the Greek forces. The added description of him as the “leader of leaders,” feared by Greece, sets his former power against his present helplessness.

The plea rises from “the son of Priamus begs you” to “Priamus himself begs you.” Hector makes his father’s imagined voice audible through his own. Royal standing, wealth, tears, and prayer are all brought before Achilles, but the appeal finally reaches beyond them: even if none of these can move him, perhaps he can pity another father’s suffering.

Here the Latin brings into Hector’s dying speech an appeal that Homer gives to Priamus when he later begs Achilles for his son’s body: “Remember your own father,” a man like himself on the threshold of destructive old age (*Iliad* 24.486–487). Peleus is Achilles’ father; Pyrrhus is Achilles’ son. Hector asks his enemy to recognize both sides of the broken family bond: to think of Peleus when he imagines Priamus, and of Pyrrhus when he looks at the dying son before him. The final reference to Hector’s own “body” thus presents him not merely as remains to be recovered, but as a father’s child. Against the division between victor and defeated, he sets the relationships they share.
