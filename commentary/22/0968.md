### 968–976 (*Iliad* 22.294–299, 22.317–320)

> 968 Dumque retro cedit fraternaque rebus in artis
> (And while he gives ground and, in his straits, for his brother’s)
>
> 969 respicit auxilia et nullam uidet esse salutem,
> (help looks back, and sees there is no safety,)
>
> 970 sensit adesse dolos. Quid agat? quae numina supplex
> (he perceives that deceit is at hand. What is he to do? What gods, as a suppliant,)
>
> 971 inuocet? et toto languescunt corpore uires
> (is he to invoke? And throughout his body his strength grows faint)
>
> 972 auxiliumque negant; retinet uix dextera ferrum,
> (and denies him help; his right hand scarcely holds his blade,)
>
> 973 nox oculos inimica tegit nec subuenit ullum
> (hostile night covers his eyes, and there comes to his aid no)
>
> 974 defesso auxilium; pugnat moriturus et alto
> (help in his exhaustion; doomed to die, he fights and deep)
>
> 975 corde premit gemitus. Instat Nereius heros
> (in his heart stifles his groans. The Nereian hero bears down)
>
> 976 turbatumque premit procul undique; tunc iacit hastam
> (and presses him, driven far in confusion, on every side; then he hurls his spear)

Hector’s exhaustion now becomes complete isolation. As he retreats before Achilles, he looks for the brother whose support he had expected, only to discover that no rescue is coming. The background is a divine deception: in Homer, Athena, the goddess supporting Achilles, has approached Hector in the likeness of his brother Deiphobus and encouraged him to stand and fight. Hector therefore believes that he has an ally beside him. When he needs another spear, he calls to Deiphobus, but no one is there. He understands what has happened: “He is within the wall, and Athena has deceived me” (*Iliad* 22.299).

The Latin compresses that discovery into a backward glance and a sudden recognition of treachery. It leaves the brother and the deceiving goddess unnamed here, and replaces Hector’s spoken explanation with two urgent questions: what can he do, and which gods can he ask for help? These questions expose more than a lack of practical options. Once divine intervention has brought him into this trap, even prayer offers no clear refuge.

The prolonged description of Hector’s failing body is an addition to the corresponding Homeric verses. His strength no longer serves him, his blade almost slips from his grasp, and “hostile night” closes over his eyes. This is not the fall of night over the battlefield, but a darkness experienced by the exhausted fighter, anticipating death without yet announcing it. The repeated absence of help joins his physical collapse to his abandonment: neither his expected companion nor his own body can sustain him. Yet the passage preserves his courage. He continues to fight, and suppresses rather than voices his groans.

Against this failing resistance stands the “Nereian hero,” Achilles, so called because his mother, Thetis, is one of the Nereids, the sea goddesses. He keeps pressing Hector back until he casts his spear. Homer gives the weapon a moment of brilliant visibility, comparing its gleam to the evening star, “the loveliest star that stands in heaven,” as Achilles brandishes it with Hector’s death in mind (*Iliad* 22.317–320). The Latin omits that luminous comparison and moves straight to the throw. Its attention remains on Hector’s distress and Achilles’ relentless pressure; the spear is released, but its effect is left for the next passage.
