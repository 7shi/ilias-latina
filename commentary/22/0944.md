### 944–946 (*Iliad* 22.25–35)

> 944 Spectant de muris miseri sua fata parentes
> (From the walls his wretched parents watch their own fate)
>
> 945 pallentemque uident supremo tempore natum
> (and see their son pale in his final hour,)
>
> 946 quem iam summa dies suprema luce premebat.
> (upon whom his last day already bore down with its final light.)

The view shifts from the pursuit outside Troy to the parents watching helplessly from its walls. They are Priam, Troy’s king, and Hecuba, his queen; their son is Hector, now fleeing before Achilles. In watching him, they watch “their own fate”: Hector is not only their child but the defender on whom their survival and their city’s safety depend. His threatened death brings their own ruin into view.

Homer introduces this scene through Priam’s sight of Achilles approaching, his bronze armor shining like the star called Orion’s Dog, whose brilliance is an omen of suffering for mortals (*Iliad* 22.25–32). Priam then cries out, strikes his head, and begs his son to take shelter; Hector is still outside the gates (*Iliad* 22.33–35). The Latin leaves out the star comparison and the father’s urgent gestures, making both parents silent witnesses instead. Its attention rests not on the terrifying radiance of the attacker but on the pallor of the son they may lose.

Hector has not yet been killed. Yet “final hour,” “last day,” and “final light” repeat the same forewarning so insistently that his death already seems present. The chase continues, but the narrative gives his parents—and the reader—little room for hope.
