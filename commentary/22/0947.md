### 947–950 (*Iliad* 22.226–231, 22.247, 22.296–303)

> 947 Huic subito ante oculos similis Tritonia fratri
> (Suddenly before his eyes, Tritonia, in his brother’s likeness,)
>
> 948 occurrens iuuenem simulato decipit ore;
> (appears and deceives the young man with a disguised face;)
>
> 949 nam cum Deiphobi tutum se credidit armis,
> (for when he believed himself safe under the protection of Deiphobus’ arms,)
>
> 950 transtulit ad Danaos iterum sua numina Pallas.
> (Pallas turned her divine favor back to the Danaans.)

The death foretold in the parents’ watching now draws closer through deception. Tritonia and Pallas are names for the same goddess, Minerva, the Athena of Homer, who supports Achilles and the Danaans—the Greeks besieging Troy. She appears to Hector in the likeness of his brother Deiphobus. What seems to be an offer of family protection is therefore a trap: Hector believes that he no longer faces Achilles alone.

In Homer, Athena copies both Deiphobus’ body and his voice. She urges Hector to stop fleeing: “Come, let us stand and defend ourselves” (*Iliad* 22.231). Her apparent readiness to fight beside him gives him the confidence to turn and face his pursuer. The Latin condenses their conversation into the disguised appearance and Hector’s misplaced trust in his brother’s arms. After the helpless parents on the walls, another close relative seems to offer the support Hector needs—but this brother is only an illusion.

The final line presents the collapse of that hope as Pallas turning her favor back to the Greeks. This is not a genuine change of allegiance: her seeming assistance to Hector has served Achilles all along. Homer makes the discovery Hector’s own. When he needs another spear and calls for Deiphobus, there is no brother beside him. He understands that Athena has deceived him and declares, “Now evil death is close to me, no longer far away, and there is no escape” (*Iliad* 22.300–301). The Latin states the goddess’s change of side at once, before the fight begins; Hector’s own discovery of the deceit comes only later, as he retreats (968–970).
