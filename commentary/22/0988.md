### 988–995 (*Iliad* 22.344–347, 22.354)

> 988 Talia Priamides. Quem contra durus Achilles:
> (So spoke Priamides. Unyielding Achilles answered him:)
>
> 989 "Quid mea supplicibus temptas inflectere dictis
> (“Why do you try with pleading words to soften my)
>
> 990 pectora, quem possem direptum more ferarum,
> (heart—you whom, torn apart in the manner of wild beasts, I could)
>
> 991 si sineret natura, meis absumere malis?
> (if nature allowed it, devour with my own jaws?)
>
> 992 Te uero tristesque ferae cunctaeque uolucres
> (But you both grim beasts and all the birds)
>
> 993 diripient, auidosque canes tua uiscera pascent.
> (will tear apart, and your entrails will feed ravenous dogs.)
>
> 994 Haec ex te capient Patrocli gaudia manes,
> (This is the joy that Patroclus’ spirit will gain from you,)
>
> 995 si sapiunt umbrae." Dum talia magnus Achilles
> (if shades can feel.” While such things great Achilles)

Hector’s appeal to their shared family bonds meets an absolute refusal. Priamides means “son of Priam”: even the name introducing his final plea’s conclusion recalls the father whose grief he has asked Achilles to pity. But Achilles remains “unyielding.” Hector has tried to make him see the body before him as a beloved son; Achilles answers by imagining it as flesh to be torn apart and eaten.

The Latin preserves the most terrible image in Homer’s reply. There Achilles wishes that his anger could drive him “to cut away your flesh and eat it raw, for what you have done” (*Iliad* 22.346–347). Here the impulse becomes conditional on nature allowing it. What precisely nature forbids is left unexplained, but the comparison with wild beasts makes the boundary clear: Achilles imagines a violence beyond human combat. He will not himself consume Hector, yet the beasts, birds, and dogs will carry out the destruction he threatens. The return of the body, which Hector sought for his parents, is denied in the starkest possible terms.

The retelling also changes the shape of the refusal. Homer’s Achilles begins, “Do not entreat me by my knees or by my parents, you dog” (*Iliad* 22.345). The Latin drops that direct insult and the specific gestures of supplication, answering instead Hector’s attempt to soften his heart. It then expands Homer’s brief declaration that “dogs and birds will divide you entirely among them” (*Iliad* 22.354) into a more graphic picture of Hector’s entrails feeding hungry animals.

The final thought gives this threatened desecration its purpose: revenge for Patroclus, Achilles’ beloved companion, whom Hector killed. The Latin adds the claim that Patroclus’ spirit will take pleasure in what happens to his killer. Yet “if shades can feel” leaves that consolation uncertain. The shades are the spirits of the dead; Achilles cannot be sure that Patroclus can experience the satisfaction he promises him. Hector’s appeal has looked toward the living father who will mourn him. Achilles thinks instead of the dead companion whose loss still governs his actions.
