### 951–958 (*Iliad* 22.248, 22.273)

> 951 Concurrunt iactis inter se comminus hastis
> (They rush together at close quarters, having hurled their spears at each other,)
>
> 952 inuicti iuuenes: hic uastis intonat armis,
> (the unconquered young men: one thunders in his mighty armor,)
>
> 953 ille hostem ualidum nequiquam umbone repellit
> (the other vainly thrusts back his powerful enemy with his shield-boss)
>
> 954 alternisque ferox mutat congressibus ictus.
> (and fiercely varies his blows as they clash again and again.)
>
> 955 Sudor agit riuos, ensem terit horridus ensis
> (Sweat runs in streams; savage sword grinds against sword,)
>
> 956 collatusque haeret pede pes et dextera dextrae.
> (and, pressed close together, foot holds against foot and right hand against right hand.)
>
> 957 Hastam iam manibus saeuus librabat Achilles
> (Now fierce Achilles was poising a spear in his hands)
>
> 958 inque uirum magnis emissam uiribus egit,
> (and hurled it at his opponent with great force,)

The goddess’s deception gives way to the clash it has made possible. Hector, Troy’s defender, now faces Achilles, the strongest warrior among the Greeks. Calling both men “unconquered” briefly places them on equal terms: each enters the encounter with his martial greatness intact. Yet “vainly” already undermines that balance. Hector’s resistance is fierce and resourceful, but it cannot secure the protection he had expected from his supposed brother.

The Latin makes their meeting into a sustained struggle at close quarters. The shield-boss—the raised central part of the shield—becomes a means of pushing an opponent away, while changing blows, streaming sweat, scraping swords, and bodies pressed together give the fight weight and noise. Hector is not simply overwhelmed without resistance. He keeps adapting his attack, even as the poem signals that his efforts will fail.

This crowded exchange substantially reshapes Homer’s encounter. In the *Iliad*, the warriors first approach one another and speak: Hector proposes that the victor return the defeated man’s body to his people, but Achilles refuses any agreement. Their subsequent attacks unfold separately, with spear-casts and pauses for speech, rather than through the prolonged sword-fighting described here. The Latin removes that negotiation and adds an almost bodily deadlock, bringing the enemies so close that hand meets hand and foot meets foot.

The final movement returns attention to Achilles alone. Homer likewise describes him brandishing and casting his spear (*Iliad* 22.273), but here that action follows the added close combat. The Latin does not pause to explain the change back from swords to a spear: its emphasis is on the force of the attack, carrying the narrative forward with the weapon already in flight.
