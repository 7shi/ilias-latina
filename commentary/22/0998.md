### 998–1001 (*Iliad* 22.396–398, 24.16, 22.399–404)

> 998 deligat ad currum pedibusque exsanguia membra
> (binds to his chariot, and drags the lifeless limbs by the feet)
>
> 999 ter circum muros uictor trahit; altius ipsos
> (three times around the walls as victor; even the horses)
>
> 1000 fert domini successus equos. Tum maximus heros
> (are lifted higher by their master’s triumph. Then the mighty hero)
>
> 1001 detulit ad Danaos foedatum puluere corpus.
> (brought the body, befouled with dust, back to the Danaans.)

Achilles’ unsatisfied anger now turns against Hector’s corpse. The opening completes the sentence begun in the preceding passage: Achilles binds the dead man to his chariot and drags him around Troy’s walls. Hector can no longer suffer or resist, but his body can still be dishonoured. The victor then returns with it to the Danaans, the Greeks besieging Troy.

The Latin shortens Homer’s grim account of how Achilles prepares the body for dragging. In the *Iliad*, he pierces the tendons behind Hector’s ankles, threads leather straps through them, and fastens these to the chariot, leaving the head to trail along the ground (*Iliad* 22.396–398). Here the attachment is stated briefly, while the three circuits around the walls give the outrage its shape. These circuits differ from Homer’s account: there Achilles later drags Hector three times around the burial mound of Patroclus, the companion whose death he is avenging (*Iliad* 24.16). The Latin brings that later action forward and relocates it, making the humiliation of Troy’s defender take place around the city he protected.

Even the horses seem raised by their master’s triumph, as though his elation passes into their movement. Homer describes them eagerly drawing the chariot, but places greater weight on the ruined appearance of the man behind it: Hector’s dark hair spreads out, and his head, “once beautiful,” lies in the dust (*Iliad* 22.400–403). The Latin gathers that sorrowful picture into the final phrase, “the body, befouled with dust.” Its language of victory and heroic greatness stands beside the degradation of a helpless corpse: Achilles has won the fight, but his anger continues beyond it.
