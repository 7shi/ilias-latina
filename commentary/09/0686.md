### 686–687 (*Iliad* 9.1–3, 9.90–92)

> 686 Attoniti Danaum proceres discrimine tanto
> (Stunned by so great a crisis, the leaders of the Danai)
>
> 687 nec dapibus releuant animos nec corpora curant,
> (neither refresh their spirits with food nor care for their bodies,)

The Danai are the Greeks, now trapped in their fortified camp while the Trojans keep watch outside. These lines turn from the victorious Trojans, who ended the preceding book drinking wine and easing their cares, to the defeated Greek leaders, too shaken to find relief in food or attend to their physical needs. Their distress shows how deeply Hector’s assault has undermined their confidence: even those responsible for directing the army are overwhelmed.

The Latin preserves the mood of the opening of Homer’s ninth book, where the Greeks are seized by panic and their leaders afflicted with unbearable grief (*Iliad* 9.1–3). But it sharpens that distress by reversing a later moment in Homer’s narrative. There, the leaders gather in the tent of Agamemnon, the commander of the Greek army, and eat and drink their fill before discussing what to do (*Iliad* 9.90–92). Here, instead, the crisis interrupts even the ordinary acts that would sustain them. The contrast with the Trojans makes the reversal of the Greeks’ fortunes especially stark.
