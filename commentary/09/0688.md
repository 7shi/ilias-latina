### 688 (*Iliad* 9.93–94)

> 688 sed miseri sua fata gemunt. Mox Nestore pulsi
> (but in their misery they lament their fate. Soon, urged on by Nestor,)

The Greek leaders’ distress now finds expression in lamentation: unable to take comfort in food or care for themselves, they can only mourn their plight. Nestor, an elderly Greek king whose experience gives his advice particular authority, begins to move them from grief toward action. The unfinished phrase leaves his intervention poised to take effect in what follows.

In Homer, Nestor is the first to begin “weaving a plan,” and the narrator recalls that his counsel had proved best before (*Iliad* 9.93–94). The Latin compresses that introduction into “urged on by Nestor.” It leaves his reputation unstated and emphasizes instead the immediate force of his counsel upon the dispirited leaders.
