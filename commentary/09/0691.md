### 691–693 (*Iliad* 9.307–310, 9.378–380, 9.132–134)

> 691 nec Danaum capit aure preces nec munera regis
> (he gives no ear to the prayers of the Danai, nor does he wish to accept the king’s gifts)
>
> 692 ulla referre cupit; non illum redditus ignis
> (of any kind; neither the return of his beloved)
>
> 693 aut intacta suo Briseis corpore mouit.
> (nor Briseis, untouched in body, moved him.)

Achilles rejects the embassy’s appeal. The Danai are the Greeks, and the king is Agamemnon, their commander, whose seizure of Briseis caused Achilles to withdraw from battle. Neither the army’s distress nor the offer of compensation can overcome his resentment. “He gives no ear” describes his refusal to be persuaded, not a refusal to let the envoys speak.

Briseis is a captive woman awarded to Achilles as a prize of war and then taken from him by Agamemnon. Her loss wounded both his affection and his standing among the warriors. The Latin calls her his “fire,” presenting her as the object of his love. The return of his beloved and the return of Briseis are therefore not separate inducements: the wording dwells on the same offer, adding the assurance that Agamemnon has not had sexual relations with her. “Untouched in body” means untouched by Agamemnon, not necessarily virginal.

Homer gives Achilles a long speech explaining his rejection. His contempt for compensation is explicit: “His gifts are hateful to me,” he says, before declaring that even ten or twenty times Agamemnon’s present wealth would not persuade him (*Iliad* 9.378–380). The Latin compresses that sustained refusal into a few categorical statements. It also brings into Achilles’ response the assurance Agamemnon had given when proposing the embassy: he would swear that he had never shared Briseis’ bed (*Iliad* 9.132–134). Although the Latin speaks of his beloved as “returned,” her restoration is still an offer. The passage presents even that promised reconciliation as powerless to move him.
