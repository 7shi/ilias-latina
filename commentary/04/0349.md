### 349–350 (*Iliad* 4.138, 4.150, 4.210–212)

> 349 dissecat. Excedit pugna gemebundus Atrides
> (cuts through it. Groaning, Atrides withdraws from the fighting)
>
> 350 castraque tuta petit, quem doctus ab arte paterna
> (and seeks the safe camp; him, taught by his father’s art,)

The arrow cuts through the armored tunic described in the preceding verse. Atrides—“the son of Atreus,” here meaning Menelaus—withdraws in pain and heads for the camp. The forceful “cuts through” makes the penetration of his armor vivid, before attention shifts from the missile to the wounded man.

The Latin turns Homer’s account into a swift movement from injury toward treatment. In the *Iliad*, the arrow penetrates another protective layer beneath the breastplate, and Menelaus shudders at the wound (*Iliad* 4.138, 4.150). He does not retreat to camp: the healer Machaon comes to where he stands, surrounded by the Greek leaders (*Iliad* 4.210–212). Here, groaning and withdrawal replace that stationary scene. The sentence running on introduces the healer through a skill learned from his father, Asclepius, the legendary healer; his name follows in the next verse.
