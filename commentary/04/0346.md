### 346–347 (*Iliad* 4.104–105, 4.124–127, 4.134)

> 346 foederaque intento turbauit Pandarus arcu,
> (and Pandarus broke the treaty with his drawn bow,)
>
> 347 te, Menelae, petens; laterique uolatile telum
> (aiming at you, Menelaus; and on the side the winged missile)

The council of the gods is followed abruptly by the act that destroys the prospect of peace. Pandarus, an archer fighting on the Trojan side, shoots at Menelaus, the Greek king whose wife, Helen, Paris has taken to Troy. The treaty had made the duel between Menelaus and Paris a means of settling the conflict without further battle. By attacking Menelaus after that duel, Pandarus violates the agreement and puts its promised settlement beyond reach.

Homer supplies the connection between divine deliberation and this human betrayal. Athena, acting to bring about a breach of the truce, approaches Pandarus in disguise and tempts him with the glory and rewards he could gain by killing Menelaus. The Latin passes over that persuasion, along with Homer’s elaborate account of the bow, the archer’s preparations, and the shot. A single phrase, “with his drawn bow,” carries the action straight to its decisive consequence: the treaty is broken.

The direct address to Menelaus briefly brings the threatened man closer to the reader. Homer also addresses him, but with reassurance: “Nor did the blessed gods forget you, Menelaus” (*Iliad* 4.127). Athena, who prompted the attack, nevertheless turns the arrow aside to prevent a fatal wound; it strikes his closely fitted belt (*Iliad* 4.134). Here the address instead emphasizes Pandarus’s aim, and the sentence running on leaves the winged missile in flight.
