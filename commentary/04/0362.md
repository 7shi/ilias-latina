### 362–365 (*Iliad* 4.473–474, 4.480–481)

> 362 Inde manu forti Graiorum terga prementem
> (Then with a strong hand, him pressing hard on the backs of the Greeks,)
>
> 363 occupat Anthemione satum Telamonius Aiax
> (Anthemion’s son, Telamonian Ajax overtakes)
>
> 364 et praedurato transfixit pectora telo:
> (and pierces his breast with a hardened weapon:)
>
> 365 purpuream uomit ille animam cum sanguine mixtam,
> (he spews out his purple life, mingled with blood,)

Another Greek champion now comes forward: Telamonian Ajax, son of Telamon. His victim, identified here only as Anthemion’s son, is the young Trojan Simoeisius. The Latin presents him pressing upon the Greeks from behind when Ajax overtakes and kills him. In Homer, he is advancing into battle when Ajax’s spear strikes him in the chest beside the right nipple and passes through his shoulder (*Iliad* 4.480–481).

The Latin preserves the father’s name but omits the family history that gives this death its particular poignancy in Homer. Simoeisius was named after the river Simoeis, beside which his mother gave birth to him while returning from tending sheep with her parents. His life ends before he can repay his parents for raising him. Here that brief glimpse of a life outside battle disappears, and attention falls instead on the violence of the killing.

The hardened weapon is an added detail, as is the final image of life pouring out with blood. After the preceding warrior’s departure from the “longed-for light,” this death is starkly physical: the victim’s life seems inseparable from the blood he spills, its purple colour making the invisible loss of life visible.
