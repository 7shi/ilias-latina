### 355–356 (*Iliad* 4.446–451)

> 355 Bellum ingens oritur multumque utrimque cruoris
> (A great battle breaks out, and much blood on both sides)
>
> 356 funditur et totis sternuntur corpora campis
> (is shed, and bodies are strewn across all the fields.)

The anger and shared grief of the Greek army now give way to the fighting itself. Greeks and Trojans clash after the broken truce, and the view widens from the wounded Menelaus to a battlefield covered with bodies. Neither side is singled out as victorious: the emphasis falls on the scale of the fighting and the blood shed by both armies.

Homer describes the encounter through movement and sound: the armies converge, shields and spears collide, and a great din rises. The groans of the dying mingle with the triumphant cries of those who kill them, while “the earth ran with blood” (*Iliad* 4.446–451). The Latin compresses this crowded scene into two stark facts—bloodshed and fallen bodies. It keeps the violence of the encounter but omits the clashing weapons and opposing cries, replacing them with a sweeping view of the dead across the plain.
