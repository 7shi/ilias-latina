### 377–379 (*Iliad* 4.517–520, 4.524–526)

> 377 Iamque Amarynciden saxi deiecerat ictu
> (And now Amaryncides with the blow of a stone had been struck down)
>
> 378 Pirous Imbrasides dederatque silentibus umbris;
> (by Pirous Imbrasides, and given to the silent shades;)
>
> 379 dumque auidus praedae iuuenem spoliare parabat,
> (and while, eager for booty, he prepared to strip the young man,)

The scene shifts to another encounter, this time with a Greek as the victim. Amaryncides means “son of Amarynceus”: he is Diores, one of the Greek leaders. His killer, Pirous Imbrasides, is Pirous, son of Imbrasus, whom Homer introduces as a leader of the Thracians fighting on the Trojan side. The battle continues through individual deaths on either side, rather than through a sustained account of one warrior’s advance.

The Latin compresses a killing that Homer unfolds in stages. In the *Iliad*, Pirous first strikes Diores on the right shin near the ankle with a jagged stone, then runs up and finishes him with a spear-thrust beside the navel. His entrails spill onto the ground, and darkness covers his eyes. Here the stone-blow and death are brought together, with no separate finishing stroke. Instead of the exposed body, we hear that the victim has been sent to the “silent shades”—the company of the dead in the underworld.

The desire for plunder is added to this encounter. Pirous prepares to strip the dead young man, taking his armour and equipment as the spoils of victory. The unfinished “while” carries the action forward: the killing is complete, but the victor has not yet secured his reward.
