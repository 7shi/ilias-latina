### 373–376 (*Iliad* 4.495–499, 4.501–504)

> 373 Democoonta petit teloque aduersa trabali
> (He attacks Democoonta and with a spear thick as a beam)
>
> 374 tempora transadigit uaginaque horridus ensem
> (pierces his temples head-on; then, fearsome,)
>
> 375 eripit; ille suis moriens resupinus in armis
> (he snatches his sword from its sheath; the other, dying in his armour,)
>
> 376 concidit et terram moribundo uertice pulsat.
> (falls backwards and strikes the earth with his dying head.)

The retaliation begun in the preceding passage now finds its victim. Atrides, stirred by Leucon’s death, attacks Democoonta, another son of the Trojan king Priam. Homer identifies him as a son born outside marriage. He is not the man who killed Leucon: vengeance falls on another member of the enemy’s ranks rather than on the killer himself.

In Homer, the avenger is Odysseus, whose advance receives more attention. He moves through the front-line fighters in gleaming bronze, looks around, and throws from close range; the Trojans recoil before him. The Latin leaves out these movements and reactions, bringing attacker and victim together almost immediately. It preserves the terrible wound through the temples, while the description of the spear as thick as a beam emphasizes the force of the attack. The drawn sword is an addition: it makes the avenger look ready to continue killing, though no sword-blow follows here.

The ending also changes the emphasis. Homer describes darkness covering the wounded man’s eyes, followed by the thud of his fall and the clatter of his armour. Here the armour remains, but attention settles on the dying head striking the ground. After Leucon’s teeth plucking the grass, this is another close view of a fallen man’s body against the earth. The revenge answers one death with another, equally physical and final.
