### 373–376 (*Iliad* 4.495–499, 4.501–504)

> 373 Democoonta petit teloque aduersa trabali
> (attacks Democoon, and with a spear thick as a beam the facing)
>
> 374 tempora transadigit uaginaque horridus ensem
> (temples he pierces through, and, fearsome, his sword from its sheath)
>
> 375 eripit; ille suis moriens resupinus in armis
> (he snatches; the other, dying, on his back in his own armor)
>
> 376 concidit et terram moribundo uertice pulsat.
> (falls and strikes the earth with his dying head.)

The retaliation begun in the preceding passage now finds its victim. Atrides, stirred by Leucus’ death, attacks Democoon, another son of the Trojan king Priam. Homer identifies him as a son born outside marriage. He is not the man who killed Leucus: vengeance falls on another member of the enemy’s ranks rather than on the killer himself.

In Homer, the avenger is Odysseus, whose advance receives more attention. He moves through the front-line fighters in gleaming bronze, looks around, and throws from close range; the Trojans recoil before him. The Latin leaves out these movements and reactions, bringing attacker and victim together almost immediately. It preserves the terrible wound through the temples, while the description of the spear as thick as a beam emphasizes the force of the attack. The drawn sword is an addition: it makes the avenger look ready to continue killing, though no sword-blow follows here.

The ending also changes the emphasis. Homer describes darkness covering the wounded man’s eyes, followed by the thud of his fall and the clatter of his armor. Here the armor remains, but attention settles on the dying head striking the ground. After Leucus’ teeth plucking the grass, this is another close view of a fallen man’s body against the earth. The revenge answers one death with another, equally physical and final.
