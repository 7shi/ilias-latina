### 360–361 (*Iliad* 4.457–461)

> 360 Occidit Antilochi rigido demersus in umbras
> (Plunged into the shades by Antilochus’ hard sword,)
>
> 361 ense Thalysiades optataque lumina linquit.
> (Thalysiades falls and leaves the cherished light.)

From the general slaughter, the narrative turns to a single death. Antilochus, a Greek warrior and son of Nestor, kills a Trojan whom the Latin calls Thalysiades: “son of Thalysius.” This is Echepolus, whom Homer describes as a brave, helmeted fighter among the foremost ranks.

The Latin compresses Homer’s detailed account of the fatal wound into a sword stroke and a passage from light into darkness. In the *Iliad*, Antilochus strikes Echepolus on the ridge of his horsehair-crested helmet; the bronze spear point pierces his forehead and penetrates the bone, until “darkness covered his eyes” (*Iliad* 4.459–461). Here the sword replaces the spear, and the wound itself disappears from view. Being “plunged into the shades” evokes death and the underworld, while leaving the “cherished light” presents life as something precious that the dying warrior must surrender.
