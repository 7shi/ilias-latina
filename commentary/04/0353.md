### 353–354 (*Iliad* 4.223–225, 4.221–222)

> 353 Armauit fortes Agamemnonis ira Pelasgos
> (Agamemnon’s anger armed the brave Pelasgians,)
>
> 354 et dolor in pugnam cunctos communis agebat.
> (and shared grief drove them all into battle.)

The treatment of Menelaus gives way to the response of the whole army. Agamemnon, his brother and the commander of the Greek forces, turns his anger into a force that mobilizes his men. “Pelasgians” here means the Greeks besieging Troy. Their shared grief follows the treacherous attack on Menelaus: although he has survived, the broken truce has given the army a common injury to avenge.

Homer presents this return to battle more concretely. The Trojan ranks advance, and the Greeks put on their armour again and turn their thoughts to fighting. Attention then shifts to Agamemnon, who is neither lingering nor shrinking from combat, but eagerly pressing toward battle (*Iliad* 4.221–225). The Latin reverses that order and compresses the action into two emotional causes: the leader’s anger and the army’s grief. It leaves out the approaching Trojans and makes Agamemnon’s readiness an explicitly rallying force. After Menelaus’ individual return to combat, these lines bring the entire Greek army back into the war.
