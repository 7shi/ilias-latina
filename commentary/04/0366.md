### 366–372 (*Iliad* 4.482, 4.489–494)

> 366 ora rigat moriens. Tum magnis Antiphus hastam
> (As he dies, he drenches his face. Then Antiphus,)
>
> 367 uiribus aduersum conatus corpore toto
> (straining with all his strength and his whole body,)
>
> 368 torquet in Aeaciden; telumque errauit ab hoste
> (hurls his spear at Aeacides; the weapon missed its foe)
>
> 369 inque hostem cecidit, transfixit et inguina Leucon:
> (and struck another foe, piercing Leucon’s groin:)
>
> 370 concidit infelix prostratus uulnere forti
> (the unfortunate man fell, laid low by a grievous wound,)
>
> 371 et carpit uirides moribundus dentibus herbas.
> (and, dying, plucks the green grass with his teeth.)
>
> 372 Impiger Atrides casu commotus amici
> (Tireless Atrides, stirred by his friend’s fall,)

The opening words complete the death of Anthemion’s son: the blood he pours out drenches his face as he dies. Homer compares the fallen young man to a poplar cut down by a craftsman; the Latin leaves out that comparison and moves directly to the next spear-cast. Antiphus, a son of the Trojan king Priam, attacks Aiax. Here Aiax is called Aeacides, “descendant of Aeacus,” after his grandfather.

Antiphus throws with his whole body behind the weapon, but strength does not ensure accuracy. The spear misses its intended enemy and finds another: Leucon, the Greek warrior called Leucos in Homer. The repeated “foe” catches the grim accident of battle—a failed attack still brings death. In Homer, Leucos is dragging a corpse away when he is struck in the groin; he collapses over it, and the body drops from his hands (*Iliad* 4.491–493). The Latin retains the wound but removes this action, replacing it with the dying man’s mouth against the grass.

That final image makes his death painfully physical. Warriors elsewhere in epic bite the earth as they fall; here the teeth pluck living, green grass. The movement resembles grazing, but belongs to a man in his death throes.

The unfinished closing line begins a response to this loss. Atrides means “son of Atreus,” a title shared by the Greek leaders Agamemnon and Menelaus; this line alone does not distinguish them. The Latin departs from Homer here: Leucos is Odysseus’ companion, and it is Odysseus who becomes enraged at his killing (*Iliad* 4.494). Though the avenger’s identity changes, the narrative impulse remains the same: one warrior’s death draws another into action.
