### 344–345 (*Iliad* 4.1–4)

> 344 Dumque inter sese proceres certamen haberent,
> (And while the chiefs contended with one another,)
>
> 345 concilium omnipotens habuit regnator Olympi
> (the almighty ruler of Olympus held a council.)

The scene shifts from the unresolved confrontation before Troy to the gods above. Menelaus has defeated Paris, but Paris has disappeared through Venus’s intervention, and the Greeks’ demand that the Trojans honor the treaty remains unanswered. The chiefs’ continuing contention provides the background to a council on Olympus, the mountain conceived as the gods’ dwelling. Its “almighty ruler” is Jupiter, the Roman counterpart of the Greek Zeus.

The Latin preserves Homer’s gathering of the gods but greatly compresses its setting. In the *Iliad*, they sit beside Zeus on a golden floor while Hebe, the goddess of youth, pours nectar, their divine drink. They salute one another with golden cups and look toward Troy (*Iliad* 4.1–4). Those details create a striking distance between the gods’ comfortable gathering and the dangerous conflict below. Here, the luxury and ceremony disappear: what matters is that the supreme god holds council while the human leaders contend. The transition brings divine deliberation to the foreground, reminding the reader—after Venus’s rescue of Paris—that human victory alone may not settle the war.
