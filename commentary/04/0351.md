### 351–352 (*Iliad* 4.213–219)

> 351 Paeoniis curat iuuenis Podalirius herbis,
> (the young Podalirius treats with Paeonian herbs,)
>
> 352 itque iterum in caedes horrendaque proelia uictor.
> (and he goes again, the victor, into slaughter and dreadful battles.)

The wounded Menelaus receives treatment and returns to the fighting. His healer is named here as Podalirius, not Machaon, to whom Homer assigns this task. Both are sons of Asclepius, the legendary healer, so the reference to skill learned from a father fits either brother. “Paeonian” associates the healing herbs with Paeon, the physician of the gods.

The Latin compresses a careful treatment into a single phrase. In Homer, Machaon extracts the arrow, loosens Menelaus’ protective clothing, sucks blood from the wound, and applies soothing medicines. These remedies had been given to his father by Chiron, the wise centaur renowned for his knowledge of healing. The Latin retains the herbs and the inherited medical skill but omits the physical details of tending the wound.

Menelaus’ immediate return to battle is an addition to Homer’s account. It briskly closes the episode: the wounded man who withdrew groaning now goes back with renewed strength. Yet “slaughter and dreadful battles” makes clear that healing restores him not to safety, but to the violence of war.
