### 770–771 (*Iliad* 12.471)

> 770 et scandunt puppes. Vrget Troiana iuuentus
> (and climb aboard the ships. The young Trojan warriors press on)
>
> 771 telaque crebra iacit: resonat clamoribus aether.
> (and hurl volleys of missiles; the sky resounds with shouts.)

The fleeing Pelasgi—the Greeks of the preceding passage—now climb aboard their ships, with the Trojans pressing after them. These are the vessels in which the Greeks sailed to Troy, drawn up along the shore behind their fortified camp. With the defenses breached, the fighting has reached the fleet on which their return home depends.

The Latin completes its picture of the rout by bringing the ships into view. Homer describes the Greeks fleeing “among the hollow ships,” while “an unceasing uproar arose” (*Iliad* 12.471); the Latin makes their retreat more specific by having them climb aboard. It also adds the pursuing Trojan warriors and their volleys of missiles, keeping the attackers in view rather than ending with the Greeks’ flight alone. Homer’s continuous din becomes a clamor that fills the sky, closing the scene with the overwhelming noise of battle.
