### 760–761 (*Iliad* 12.445, 12.453, 12.457–462)

> 760 obicibus muros. Tum saxo Martius Hector
> (their walls with bars. Then with a rock warlike Hector)
>
> 761 perfringit portas ferrataque robora laxat.
> (breaks through the gates and forces their iron-bound timbers apart.)

The opening words complete the Greeks’ hurried effort to secure their defenses in the preceding passage. Their retreat behind the walls brings no safety: Hector, the leading Trojan warrior, smashes through the gates with a rock. The bars and iron-bound wood emphasize the strength of the barrier he overcomes. “Warlike” renders *Martius*, an epithet linking Hector with Mars, the Roman god of war; it presents him as a figure of overwhelming martial force.

The Latin compresses Homer’s extended account into a single decisive action. In the *Iliad*, Hector picks up the stone, carries it toward the gates, plants his feet firmly, and strikes their center. The impact breaks the gate’s fittings; the stone crashes inward, the gates roar, and neither the bars nor the wooden leaves can withstand it. Hector then leaps through the opening (*Iliad* 12.445, 453, 457–462). Here the preparation and successive stages of destruction disappear, leaving Hector, his rock, and the shattered defense. The repeated **r** sounds give the Latin a rough, forceful texture suited to the crash. What the Greeks have just secured is immediately broken: the fighting can now reach inside their camp.
