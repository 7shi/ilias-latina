### 767 (*Iliad* 12.287–289)

> 767 Saxa uolant, subeunt acta testudine Troes
> (Stones fly; the Trojans come up, the tortoise driven forward,)

The Trojans press forward against the defenses as stones fly through the air. Their formation, called a *testudo*, or “tortoise,” protects the advancing soldiers with overlapping shields held overhead and toward the enemy. The image continues the preceding picture of a siege: while the Greeks fight from the walls and towers, the attackers move up under cover.

Homer describes an exchange of missiles rather than this protected advance: “Stones flew thickly on both sides, some toward the Trojans, others from the Trojans toward the Achaeans, as they hurled them; a din rose over the whole wall” (*Iliad* 12.287–289). The Latin compresses that scene into “Stones fly,” leaving their direction unstated and omitting the noise. It then adds the shield formation, a feature familiar from Roman siege warfare but absent from these Homeric lines. The emphasis shifts from the two sides exchanging blows to the Trojans’ organized advance beneath the barrage.
