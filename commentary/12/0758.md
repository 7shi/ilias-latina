### 758–759 (*Iliad* 12.37–39)

> 758 Incumbunt Troes, fugiunt in castra Pelasgi
> (The Troes press forward; the Pelasgi flee into their camp,)
>
> 759 uiribus exhaustis et uastis undique firmant
> (their strength exhausted, and on every side they secure [the gates] with massive [bars].)

The Troes are the Trojans; the Pelasgi here are the Greeks, previously called the Danai. The Trojan pressure that closed the preceding book now drives the Greeks back into their fortified camp beside the ships. Their strength spent, they turn from fighting in the open to securing their defenses. The fastening of the gates, whose wording continues beyond this excerpt, makes the retreat concrete: the Greeks now depend on barriers to hold their attackers back.

The Latin compresses Homer’s account and shifts its emphasis. In the *Iliad*, the Greeks are “overpowered by the lash of Zeus,” crowded back beside their ships and afraid of Hector, “the mighty master of rout” (*Iliad* 12.37–39). Zeus, king of the gods, thus stands behind their defeat, while Hector embodies the immediate danger. Here neither is named. Instead, the Trojans advance collectively, and the Greeks retreat through exhaustion. The barred gates give this brief transition an urgent defensive focus: the fighting has reached the Greeks’ refuge.
