### 768–769 (*Iliad* 12.469–471)

> 768 ascenduntque aditus et portis uiribus instant.
> (They climb the approaches and press against the gates with force.)
>
> 769 Turbati fugiunt omnes iam castra Pelasgi
> (In panic, all the Pelasgi now flee the camp.)

The Trojans carry their assault up to the gates, and the defenders break and run. “Pelasgi” here means the Greeks, whose fortified camp protects their ships on the shore. The movement follows directly from the preceding picture of the Trojans advancing beneath overlapping shields: their organized pressure now gives way to the enemy’s panic.

The Latin has passed over much of Homer’s struggle for the wall, including the decisive moment when Hector, the leading Trojan warrior, smashes a gate with a great stone. Homer then describes the breakthrough: “At once some crossed over the wall, while others poured through the constructed gates; the Danaans fled in terror among the hollow ships, and an unceasing din arose” (*Iliad* 12.469–471). The Danaans are, like the Latin’s Pelasgi, the Greeks.

The Latin preserves the assault and rout but makes the action less explicit: instead of showing separate groups crossing the wall and streaming through the gates, it describes the attackers climbing and pressing forward. It also leaves out the continuing uproar and the ships toward which Homer’s Greeks retreat. What remains is a swift reversal: the Trojans advance together, while all the Greeks abandon the camp in confusion.
