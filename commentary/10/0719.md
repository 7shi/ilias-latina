### 719–727 (*Iliad* 10.391–399, 10.405–414)

> 719 maxima Troia mihi currum promisit Achillis
> (Mighty Troia promised me the chariot of Achilles)
>
> 720 si uestras cepisset opes. Haec dona secutus
> (if it captured your wealth. Pursuing this reward,)
>
> 721 in dubios casus, coram quod cernitis ipsi,
> (I fell into peril, as you yourselves see before you,)
>
> 722 infelix cecidi. Nunc uos per numina diuum,
> (wretched as I am. Now, by the powers of the gods,)
>
> 723 per mare, per Ditis fluctus obtestor opaci,
> (by the sea, by the rivers of dark Dis, I implore you)
>
> 724 ne rapere hanc animam crudeli caede uelitis.
> (not to snatch away my life in cruel slaughter.)
>
> 725 Haec pro concessa referetis dona salute:
> (These are the gifts you will receive in return for sparing my life:)
>
> 726 consilium Priami regis remque ordine gentis
> (I shall explain in order the plans of King Priamus and the affairs)
>
> 727 expediam Phrygiae." Postquam quid Troia pararet
> (of the Phrygian people.” When what Troia was planning…)

Dolon now explains the reward that brought him into danger: the chariot of Achilles, one of the Greeks’ greatest warriors. “Troia,” the city of Troy, stands here for its leaders, who have promised him this prize if they capture the Greeks’ wealth. In Homer, Dolon names Hector as the man who led him astray and promised him Achilles’ horses and bronze-adorned chariot. The Latin leaves Hector unnamed and concentrates on the contrast between the magnificent reward Dolon hoped to win and the helpless position in which he now finds himself.

His explanation also omits the precise purpose of his mission. Homer’s Dolon says that Hector sent him to discover whether the Greeks were still guarding their ships or, exhausted and defeated, were planning their escape. Here the promised reward leads directly to capture and another plea for life. Dolon invokes the gods, the sea, and the rivers of the underworld ruled by Dis, the god of the dead. This solemn appeal, added by the Latin, gives greater weight to his fear of being killed.

Dolon then offers something more useful to his captors than the glory they might gain from his death: knowledge of the enemy’s plans. Priamus is Troy’s king, and the “Phrygian people” here means the Trojans. The reward Dolon once hoped to receive is thus replaced by the “gifts” he can offer Vlixes and Diomedes in exchange for survival.

In Homer, this information is elicited through questions: Odysseus, called Vlixes in Latin, asks where Hector is, how the Trojans are stationed, and whether they intend to remain near the Greek ships or withdraw into their city. The Latin condenses that interrogation into Dolon’s sweeping offer to explain everything. He presents disclosure as a bargain for his life, but his captors have not yet agreed to its terms.
