### 734–736 (*Iliad* 10.436–437, 10.513–514, 10.526–531)

> 734 Thracas equos rapiunt, quos nec praecederet Eurus
> (they seize the Thracian horses, which Eurus could not outrun,)
>
> 735 nec posset uolucri cursu superare sagitta.
> (nor could an arrow outstrip in its flying course.)
>
> 736 Inde iterum Argolicas primae sub tempore lucis
> (Then, at the first light of dawn, back to the Argive…)

The horses complete the picture begun in the preceding passage: gleaming white, they are now carried off as the chief prize of the raid. They belonged to Rhesus, the Thracian king whom Vlixes and Diomedes have just killed in his sleep. Their capture turns the slaughter into a conspicuous success, giving the two Greeks something extraordinary to bring home.

In Homer, the horses’ beauty and speed are described earlier, when the captured scout Dolon tells the raiders where to find Rhesus. He calls them “whiter than snow, and like the winds in running” (*Iliad* 10.437). The Latin transfers this description to the moment of capture and heightens it: these horses do not merely match the winds; even Eurus, the East Wind, could not overtake them. The flying arrow adds another measure of speed beyond ordinary experience.

The unfinished final verse begins the return to the Greek camp as dawn breaks. “Argive” is another designation for the Greeks fighting against Troia. Homer gives the journey more practical detail: Vlixes urges the horses on with his bow, and the pair stop where they killed Dolon to retrieve his bloodied equipment before continuing towards the ships. Here the narrative moves swiftly from the seizure of the horses towards home. The approaching daylight marks the close of an expedition whose success has depended on darkness, secrecy, and sleeping enemies.
