### 712–714 (*Iliad* 10.351–359, 10.374–377)

> 712 Post, ubi transierat fidens animoque manuque,
> (Then, when he had passed them, trusting in his courage and strength,)
>
> 713 prosiluere uiri iuuenemque euadere cursu
> (the men sprang out, and the young man, to escape at a run)
>
> 714 conantem capiunt ferroque manuque minantur.
> (trying, they seized, and threatened him with steel and hand.)

The ambush succeeds as soon as Dolon has passed the hiding place. Ulysses and Diomedes spring out behind him, cutting off his retreat towards the Trojans. His confidence gives way to flight, but the Latin allows scarcely any interval between his attempt to escape and his capture. The young scout who trusted in his own courage and strength is now at the mercy of two armed enemies.

Homer draws out this reversal. Hearing footsteps, Dolon initially stops: he hopes that the approaching men are fellow Trojans, sent by Hector to call him back. Only when they come within a spear’s throw does he recognize them as enemies and run. A pursuit follows, and Diomedes finally halts him by deliberately casting a spear past him. Dolon stands trembling, pale with fear and with his teeth chattering, while the two Greeks catch up, breathless, and seize his hands. The Latin compresses this whole sequence into a sudden attack, an attempted escape, and a capture. It omits both Dolon’s mistaken hope of friendly company and the physical details of his terror, retaining the essential change in his position: the would-be spy has become a prisoner under threat.
