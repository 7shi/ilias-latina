### 715–718 (*Iliad* 10.376–381, 10.384–389)

> 715 Ille timore pauens: "Vitam concedite" - dixit -,
> (Trembling with fear, he said, “Spare my life;)
>
> 716 "hoc unum satis est; quodsi perstatis in ira,
> (that alone is enough; but if you persist in anger,)
>
> 717 quanta ex morte mea capietis praemia laudis?
> (what rewards of glory will you gain from my death?)
>
> 718 At si cur ueniam tacitis exquiritis umbris:
> (But if you ask why I come through the silent shadows:)

Captured by Ulysses and Diomedes, Dolon immediately pleads for his life. The fear only implicit in the preceding account of his capture now becomes explicit, though the Latin still avoids Homer’s fuller description of his bodily terror. His request is reduced to the simplest possible terms: survival is all he wants. He then challenges his captors’ reason for killing him. What glory could they gain by putting a helpless prisoner to death? The question appeals to their concern for honor rather than to their pity alone.

In Homer, Dolon’s plea takes a more practical form. He offers ransom, listing the bronze, gold, and worked iron stored at home, from which his father would pay generously if he learned that his son was alive among the Greek ships. The Latin omits both this wealth and the prospect of a father recovering his son, replacing the offer with a question about the value of Dolon’s death to his captors.

The movement toward interrogation is also compressed. In Homer, Odysseus asks why Dolon is approaching the ships alone at night: has he come to plunder corpses, has Hector sent him to spy, or is he acting on his own initiative? Here Dolon himself introduces the question of his mission. His unfinished sentence turns the scene from a plea for survival toward the information he can provide.
