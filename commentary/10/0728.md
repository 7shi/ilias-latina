### 728–729 (*Iliad* 10.454–456, 10.469–470)

> 728 cognouere uiri, fauces mucrone recluso
> (the men had learned, with the blade drawn, the throat)
>
> 729 detrudunt iuuenis. Post haec tentoria Rhesi
> (of the young man they sever. After this, the tents of Rhesus)

Once Ulysses and Diomedes have learned what Troy is preparing, they kill Dolon. His offer of information has proved useful to them, but it has not secured his life. The speed of the account makes the outcome particularly stark: the disclosure and the killing follow one another without any further exchange.

In Homer, Diomedes alone strikes the fatal blow. Dolon is about to grasp his chin in supplication—a gesture of urgent personal appeal—when Diomedes cuts through his neck. The Latin omits this last attempt to plead and attributes the killing collectively to “the men,” the two Greek captors. Severing the throat, it keeps the beheading but removes the gestures that prolong Dolon’s final moment.

The narrative immediately turns toward another target. Rhesus is a Thracian king who has recently brought troops to support the Trojans. His tents mark the next stage of the Greeks’ night expedition. Homer describes the pair advancing through scattered weapons and dark blood before reaching the Thracian contingent; the Latin passes directly from Dolon’s death to the enemy camp. What Dolon hoped would purchase his release instead helps his captors continue their raid.
