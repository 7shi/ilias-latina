Late in the night, with a third of it still remaining, Diomedes, called “the Aetolian hero,” leaves the Greek camp at the command of the Danai. He chooses Ulixes, also spelled Vlixes and identified as “the companion of Diomedes,” to accompany him on a reconnaissance mission: they are to discover the Trojans’ confidence, plans, and preparations for battle.

On their way, they encounter Dolon, whom the Trojan warriors have sent to investigate the strength of the Danai and the counsels of their leaders and soldiers. Ulixes sees him approaching, and the two Greeks hide behind thick bushes until he has passed, cutting off his easy retreat to the Trojan camp. They then spring out, capture him as he tries to flee, and threaten him with their weapons. Dolon, also called “the Trojan Eumediades,” begs for his life. He explains that mighty Troia has promised him Achilles’ chariot if it captures the Greeks’ wealth. Invoking the gods, the sea, and the rivers of dark Dis, he offers to disclose the plans of King Priamus and the affairs of the “Phrygian people” in exchange for his safety. Once the Greeks have learned the Trojan plans, however, they behead him.

Diomedes and Ulixes next enter the tents of Rhesus. They kill and strip the king, who lies overcome by sleep and wine, and slaughter his companions as they lie on the grass. They take plunder and seize the brilliant white Thracian horses, whose speed surpasses both Eurus, the east wind, and a flying arrow.

At dawn they return to the Argive fleet. “Aged Nestor” hears them and welcomes them through the gates. Back in their own camp, they report their exploits to their leader, Agamemnon, here called “the Pelopeian hero,” who praises them. The book ends with the two returning warriors giving their weary bodies over to welcome rest.
