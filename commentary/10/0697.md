### 697–703 (*Iliad* 10.252–253, 10.219–222, 10.242–247, 10.204–211, 10.272–275, 10.297–298)

> 697 restabatque super tacitae pars tertia noctis,
> (and a third of the silent night still remained,)
>
> 698 cum Danaum iussu castris Aetolius heros
> (when, at the command of the Danai, the Aetolian hero)
>
> 699 egreditur sociumque sibi delegit Vlixem,
> (leaves the camp and chooses Ulixes as his companion,)
>
> 700 qui secum tacitae sublustri noctis in umbra
> (so that, with him in the faintly lit shadow of the silent night,)
>
> 701 scrutetur studio quae sit fiducia Troum
> (he might diligently discover how confident the Trojans are,)
>
> 702 quidue agitent quantasue parent in proelia uires.
> (what they are planning, and how great a force they are preparing for battle.)
>
> 703 Dumque iter horrendum loca pernoctata pauentes
> (And as they make their dreadful journey, fearing the places shrouded in night …)

The last third of the night remains, and the Greeks now send two men out to investigate the enemy’s plans. The “Danai” are the Greeks; the “Aetolian hero” is Diomedes, whose father, Tydeus, came from Aetolia in western Greece. His chosen companion, Ulixes, is the Latin name for Odysseus, king of Ithaca—the resourceful warrior who has recently returned from the unsuccessful appeal to Achilles. With Achilles still refusing to fight, the Greeks need to learn what danger the coming day may bring.

The Latin brings together several stages of Homer’s preparations for this expedition. In the *Iliad*, Nestor proposes sending a scout, Diomedes volunteers, and several warriors offer to accompany him. Given the choice, Diomedes selects Odysseus for his courage, intelligence, and the favour Athena shows him. His confidence is striking: “With him beside me, we could both return even from blazing fire, for he excels in understanding” (*Iliad* 10.246–247). Here, the discussion becomes a swift sequence of command, departure, and choice of companion.

The mission also becomes more general. Homer’s Nestor particularly wants to know whether the Trojans intend to remain near the Greek ships or withdraw to their city after their success in battle. The Latin instead asks about their confidence, plans, and military strength. Its closing, unfinished sentence turns from these practical objectives to the travellers’ apprehension. Homer sends the pair forward “like two lions through the black night,” among corpses, weapons, and blood (*Iliad* 10.297–298); the Latin foregrounds the dread of moving through places obscured by darkness. The night offers concealment, but also makes every step uncertain.
