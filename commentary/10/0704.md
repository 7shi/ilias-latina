### 704–706 (*Iliad* 10.314, 10.318–319)

> 704 carpebant, uenit ecce Dolon, quem Troia pubes
> (they were making, lo, Dolon comes, whom the Trojan youth)
>
> 705 miserat, ut Danaum sollerti pectore uires
> (had sent, so that with a shrewd mind the strength of the Danaans)
>
> 706 perspiceret sensusque ducum plebisque referret.
> (he might examine, and report back the counsels of the leaders and of the common people.)

As Diomedes and Ulysses advance through the darkness, a Trojan scout comes towards them on a matching mission. Dolon has been sent to investigate the Greeks, just as the two Greek heroes have been sent to investigate the Trojans. Both sides want to discover the enemy’s strength and intentions before another day of fighting. Dolon’s task extends beyond observing military resources: he must also learn what the commanders and ordinary soldiers are planning.

The Latin introduces Dolon abruptly, bringing the two scouting expeditions together without pausing over his preparations. In Homer, he is the son of the herald Eumedes, rich in gold and bronze, and volunteers after Hector, the Trojan commander, asks for someone to reconnoitre the Greek camp. Dolon demands an extraordinary reward: Achilles’ horses and chariot. Hector promises them, and Dolon sets out hoping to discover whether the Greeks are keeping watch or preparing to flee. Here the narrator says only that the Trojan youth have sent him; his father is named in passing, and the reward he was promised is left for him to tell when he is caught. The emphasis falls on the parallel missions and the impending meeting in the dark: each side seeks knowledge of the other, but neither expedition yet knows whom it is about to encounter.
