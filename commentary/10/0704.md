### 704–706 (*Iliad* 10.314, 10.318–319)

> 704 carpebant, uenit ecce Dolon, quem Troia pubes
> (… they were making their way, when suddenly Dolon came, whom the Trojan warriors)
>
> 705 miserat, ut Danaum sollerti pectore uires
> (had sent to examine with a shrewd mind the strength of the Danai)
>
> 706 perspiceret sensusque ducum plebisque referret.
> (and report back the counsels of their leaders and rank and file.)

As Diomedes and Ulixes advance through the darkness, a Trojan scout comes towards them on a matching mission. Dolon has been sent to investigate the Danai—the Greeks—just as the two Greek heroes have been sent to investigate the Trojans. Both sides want to discover the enemy’s strength and intentions before another day of fighting. Dolon’s task extends beyond observing military resources: he must also learn what the commanders and ordinary soldiers are planning.

The Latin introduces Dolon abruptly, bringing the two scouting expeditions together without pausing over his preparations. In Homer, he is the son of Eumedes, a wealthy herald, and volunteers after Hector, the Trojan commander, asks for someone to reconnoitre the Greek camp. Dolon demands an extraordinary reward: Achilles’ horses and chariot. Hector promises them, and Dolon sets out hoping to discover whether the Greeks are keeping watch or preparing to flee. Here, that fuller account of his background, ambition, and instructions becomes a brief statement that the Trojan warriors have sent him. The emphasis falls on the parallel missions and the impending meeting in the dark: each side seeks knowledge of the other, but neither expedition yet knows whom it is about to encounter.
