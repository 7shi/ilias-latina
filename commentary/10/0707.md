### 707–711 (*Iliad* 10.339–341, 10.349–350, 10.314, 10.351, 10.346–348)

> 707 Quem procul ut uidit socius Diomedis Vlixes,
> (When Ulysses, the companion of Diomedes, saw him from afar,)
>
> 708 abdiderant occultantes sua corpora furtim
> (the two had stealthily concealed themselves)
>
> 709 post densos frutices, dum spe percussus inani
> (behind thick bushes, until, struck by vain hope,)
>
> 710 Tros Eumediades cursu praecederet illos,
> (the Trojan Eumediades ran past them,)
>
> 711 ne facile oppressus gressum in sua castra referret.
> (so that, overpowered, he might not easily carry his steps back to his own camp.)

Ulysses spots the approaching scout before the scout notices either Greek. He and Diomedes hide and let Dolon pass, placing themselves between him and the Trojan camp. Their purpose is not simply to surprise him, but to cut off his easiest route to safety. Eumediades means “son of Eumedes”: this is still Dolon, now identified by his father’s name. His “vain hope” carries him forward, unaware that the men he has been sent to spy on are already watching him.

The Latin preserves the essentials of Homer’s ambush while compressing the discussion that prepares it. In the *Iliad*, Odysseus points out the stranger to Diomedes and proposes letting him pass before pursuing him. If he proves too fast to catch, they are to use the threat of the spear to drive him toward the Greek ships, away from the Trojan army, and prevent his escape to the city. Here, that detailed plan becomes a brief explanation of why the two Greeks wait.

The hiding place also changes. Homer’s scouts lie down among the corpses beside the road; the Latin conceals them behind thick bushes. The ambush thus loses the grim detail of living men sheltering among the battlefield dead, but retains the contrast on which the episode turns: the Greeks watch and calculate, while Dolon hurries unsuspectingly into danger.
