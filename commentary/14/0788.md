### 788 (*Iliad* 14.476–477)

> 788 Boeotumque Acamas Promachum, quem sternit atrocis
> (and Acamas strikes Promachus the Boeotian; Acamas in turn is laid low …)

The fighting continues as one death calls forth another. Acamas is a Trojan and, like Archelochus, a son of Antenor: the man Aiax has just killed is his brother. His victim, Promachus, belongs to the Greek contingent from Boeotia, a region of central Greece.

Homer makes the encounter a struggle over the fallen brother’s body. Acamas stands over Archelochus and spears Promachus as the Greek tries to drag the corpse away by its feet (*Iliad* 14.476–477). Recovering a fallen warrior protected his body from enemy mistreatment and allowed his companions to bury him. Here that duty has an immediate personal force: Acamas is defending his own brother. The Latin omits the struggle over the corpse, keeping the killer, his victim, and the victim’s homeland within its rapid succession of blows. It then begins to turn from Acamas’s victory to his own fall, in a sentence that continues into the next verse.
