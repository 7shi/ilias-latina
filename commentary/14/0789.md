### 789 (*Iliad* 14.487–489)

> 789 Penelei dextra; inde cadit Priameia pubes
> (by the right hand of fierce Peneleus; then Priam’s young warriors fall.)

The sentence carried over from the preceding verse makes Acamas the next victim: having killed Promachus, he is himself struck down by Peneleus, one of the leaders of the Greeks from Boeotia. The death of a Boeotian warrior thus receives an immediate answer from a commander of his own people.

Here the Latin changes Homer’s outcome. In the *Iliad*, Peneleus charges at Acamas, but Acamas does not stand his ground; Peneleus strikes another Trojan, Ilioneus, instead (*Iliad* 14.487–489). The Latin turns that frustrated attack into a successful act of vengeance, making the succession of deaths more direct. Its closing words broaden the view from this individual encounter to the fall of Troy’s young fighting men. Priam is Troy’s king, and “Priam’s young warriors” identifies the men of his city, not necessarily his own sons.
