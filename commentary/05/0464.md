### 464–466 (*Iliad* 5.311–318, 5.330–333)

> 464 quem Venus aetherias genetrix delapsa per auras
> (whom his mother Venus, gliding down through the heavenly air,)
>
> 465 accipit et nigra corpus caligine condit.
> (gathers him up and hides his body in a dark mist.)
>
> 466 Non tulit Oenides animo nebulasque per ipsas
> (Oenides could not bear it, and through the clouds themselves)

Aeneas has been struck down, but his mother intervenes before his enemy can finish him. Venus, the Roman name used here for Homer’s Aphrodite, is the goddess of love; Aeneas is her son by the mortal Anchises. Her descent brings divine protection into the unequal struggle. Oenides is Diomedes, here named as the grandson of Oeneus. He cannot endure her intervention, and the unfinished sentence carries his pursuit towards the concealing clouds.

The Latin preserves the essential rescue while changing its appearance. Homer explicitly says that Aeneas would have died without his mother’s help. She wraps her white arms around him, shields him with a fold of her shining robe so that no Greek can strike his chest, and carries him out of battle (*Iliad* 5.311–318). The Latin condenses this tender, physical protection into the act of gathering him up and hiding him. Its dark mist replaces the bright robe, transferring to Venus a means of concealment that Homer gives Apollo later in the rescue (*Iliad* 5.345).

Diomedes’ reaction is also more compressed. In Homer, he pursues Aphrodite because he recognizes that she is no fighting goddess—not Athena or Enyo, who govern warfare (*Iliad* 5.330–333). Here that calculation becomes a brief expression of anger: he will not accept losing his defeated opponent, even to a goddess. The conflict is shifting from combat between warriors to a mortal’s pursuit of a divine protector.
