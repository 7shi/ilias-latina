### 446 (*Iliad* 5.152)

> 446 et notum bello Xanthum uastumque Thoonem.
> (and Xanthus, renowned in war, and huge Thoon.)

Diomedes’ assault continues against Xanthus and Thoon, two more Trojan warriors. Their names extend the preceding list of men he presses: neither Xanthus’ reputation in battle nor Thoon’s imposing size can stop him. Both descriptions are additions to Homer, who simply says that Diomedes “went after Xanthus and Thoon, the two sons of Phaenops” (*Iliad* 5.152).

In Homer, this pair introduces another brief glimpse of the family left behind. Phaenops is an old man with no other sons; Diomedes kills both, leaving their father to grief and distant relatives to divide his possessions (*Iliad* 5.153–158). As with the brothers in the preceding passage, the Latin leaves out this family history. Instead, its compact list keeps attention on Diomedes’ advance, presenting his opponents through the martial qualities that prove unable to save them.
