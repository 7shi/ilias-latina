### 405–412 (*Iliad* 5.11, 5.15–19)

> 405 Phegeaque Idaeumque simul; quem cuspide Phegeus
> (Phegeus and Idaeus together; him, with his spear, Phegeus)
>
> 406 occupat ante graui, sed uulnera depulit umbo
> (heavy, attacks first, but the shield-boss warded off the wound,)
>
> 407 uitatumque solo ferrum stetit. Haud mora: totis
> (and the iron, avoided, stood fixed in the ground. Without delay, with all)
>
> 408 ingentem torquet Tydides uiribus hastam
> (his strength Tydides hurls his huge spear)
>
> 409 transadigitque uiri pectus: pars cuspidis ante
> (and drives it through the man’s chest: part of the point in front)
>
> 410 eminet et prodit scapulis pars altera fossis.
> (stands out, and the other part emerges from the pierced shoulder blades.)
>
> 411 Hunc ubi fundentem calidum de pectore flumen
> (When him, pouring a hot stream from his chest,)
>
> 412 uersantemque oculos animamque per ora uomentem
> (and rolling his eyes and vomiting his life through his mouth,)

The two sons of Dares now receive their names: **Phegeus and Idaeus**. They stand together against Diomedes, here called **Tydides**, “son of Tydeus.” Phegeus attacks first, but his advantage lasts only a moment. His spear fails to wound Diomedes, whose answering cast drives straight through his chest. Idaeus has not yet taken part in the fighting; the focus remains on his brother’s death.

The exchange preserves Homer’s essential contrast between an unsuccessful first throw and a decisive reply. But the Latin changes how Diomedes escapes injury. In Homer, Phegeus’ spear passes “over the son of Tydeus’ left shoulder” without hitting him (*Iliad* 5.16–17). Here the raised central boss of his shield protects him, and the unsuccessful weapon ends planted in the earth. Coming immediately after the account of Pallas’ protection, this makes Diomedes seem almost unassailable, though the goddess does not intervene directly in the exchange.

The death is also much more prolonged and graphic. Homer says that Diomedes strikes Phegeus “in the chest between the nipples” and knocks him from his chariot (*Iliad* 5.19). The Latin leaves out that fall and instead follows the spear through the body, then lingers over the flowing blood, rolling eyes, and departing life. The “hot stream” is blood; vomiting his life makes his final breath part of the same violent outpouring. The passage ends with its sentence unfinished, holding this dying body before the eyes of a witness whose response is still to come.
