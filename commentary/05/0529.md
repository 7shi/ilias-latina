### 529 (*Iliad* 5.680)

> 529 Hinc pugnat patriae columen Mauortius Hector,
> (Then warlike Hector, pillar of his homeland, fights.)

After Vlixes’ slaughter of seven Lycian warriors, attention turns to Hector, Troy’s foremost defender and the son of its king, Priam. Calling him his homeland’s “pillar” presents him as the support on which the city depends, recalling his earlier description as “the sole hope of the Phrygians,” the Trojan people. His fighting is not merely a display of personal courage: it sustains his country’s resistance.

In Homer, Hector sharply notices the danger as Vlixes threatens to kill still more men (*Iliad* 5.680). The Latin replaces that moment of awareness with a direct statement that Hector fights, moving briskly from one champion to the other. *Mauortius*, translated here as “warlike,” associates Hector with Mars, the Roman god of war: the defender of Troy is both his people’s essential support and a warrior of formidable strength.
