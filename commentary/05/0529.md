### 529 (*Iliad* 5.680)

> 529 Hinc pugnat patriae columen Mauortius Hector,
> (On this side fights the pillar of his homeland, Mavortian Hector,)

After Ulysses’ slaughter of seven Lycian warriors, attention turns to Hector, Troy’s foremost defender and the son of its king, Priam. Calling him his homeland’s “pillar” presents him as the support on which the city depends, recalling his earlier description as “the sole hope of the Phrygians,” the Trojan people. His fighting is not merely a display of personal courage: it sustains his country’s resistance.

In Homer, Hector sharply notices the danger as Ulysses threatens to kill still more men (*Iliad* 5.680). The Latin replaces that moment of awareness with a direct statement that Hector fights, moving briskly from one champion to the other. “Mavortian,” from Mavors, a name of Mars, the Roman god of war, marks him as warlike: the defender of Troy is both his people’s essential support and a warrior of formidable strength.
