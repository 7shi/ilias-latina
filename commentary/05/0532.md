### 532–534 (*Iliad* 5.846, 5.850–858)

> 532 Pugnat bellipotens casta cum Pallade Mauors
> (Mighty in war, Mauors fights against chaste Pallas)
>
> 533 ingentemque mouet clipeum, quem sancta uirago
> (and brandishes his huge shield; the holy warrior-maiden)
>
> 534 egit et extrema percussum cuspide caedit
> (drove him back and strikes him, piercing him with her spear’s very tip.)

From the blood-soaked fields, the story turns to the gods who take sides in the fighting. Mauors is Mars, the Roman counterpart of the Greek war-god Ares, who is supporting the Trojans. Pallas is Athena, the goddess who protects Diomedes and helps the Greeks. The “holy warrior-maiden” is also Pallas: her chastity and her prowess in battle belong to the same divine identity. Here she confronts the war-god himself, undaunted by his strength and enormous shield.

The Latin makes this encounter much more directly a struggle between two gods than it is in Homer. In the *Iliad*, Ares attacks Diomedes with his spear, but Athena catches it and pushes it aside. Diomedes then thrusts in return, and Athena drives his weapon into Ares’ lower belly, where his protective belt lies (*Iliad* 5.850–858). The human champion delivers the wound, but only with decisive divine assistance.

Here Diomedes disappears from the encounter, and Pallas herself drives back and wounds Mauors. The exchange of spear-thrusts, Athena’s defensive intervention, and her guidance of Diomedes’ weapon are compressed into a few swift movements. What remains is the reversal of the war-god’s apparent supremacy: mighty though he is, Mauors can himself be wounded on the battlefield.
