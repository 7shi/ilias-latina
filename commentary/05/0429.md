### 429–430 (*Iliad* 5.43–44)

> 429 Hinc petit Idomeneus aduersa parte ruentem
> (Next Idomeneus attacks, rushing from the opposing side,)
>
> 430 Maeoniden Phaestum, cuius post funera laetus
> (Maeonides Phaestus, after whose death, rejoicing,)

The fighting passes from Agamemnon to **Idomeneus**, king of Crete and one of the leading Greek commanders. His opponent, **Phaestus**, comes rushing towards him from the enemy side. The Latin makes their encounter more vivid than Homer’s brief announcement that Idomeneus killed Phaestus: it shows the approach and attack, then immediately treats the victim’s death as accomplished. The closing phrase begins a thought that continues in the next verse.

**Maeonides** has the form of a patronymic, “son of Maeon,” but it answers Homer’s description of Phaestus as the son of Borus, a Maeonian, who came from “fertile Tarne” (*Iliad* 5.43–44), and may simply mark him as a Maeonian. Maeonia was a region of western Asia Minor, later associated with Lydia; Phaestus belongs to the forces supporting Troy. The Latin drops both his father’s name, Borus, and his home city, compressing Homer’s brief introduction into a name and an encounter. As with Odius just before him, a Trojan ally appears only long enough to become another Greek commander’s victim.
