### 473–482 (*Iliad* 5.512–513)

> 473 accenditque animos iterumque ad bella reducit.
> (and fires his spirit and leads him back to battle once more.)
>
> 474 Vndique consurgunt acies et puluere caelum
> (On every side the battle lines rise up, and with dust the sky)
>
> 475 conditur horrendisque sonat clamoribus aether.
> (is hidden, and the air rings with dreadful shouts.)
>
> 476 Hic alius rapido deiectus in aequora curru
> (Here one man, thrown from his speeding chariot onto the plain,)
>
> 477 proteritur pedibusque simul calcatur equorum
> (is crushed and trampled beneath the horses’ hooves;)
>
> 478 atque alius uolucri traiectus corpora telo
> (another, pierced through the body by a flying missile,)
>
> 479 quadrupedis tergo pronus ruit; illius ense
> (falls face down from his steed’s back; of that one, by a sword)
>
> 480 deiectum longe caput a ceruice cucurrit;
> (struck off, the head rolled far from the neck;)
>
> 481 hic iacet exanimis fuso super arma cerebro:
> (here a man lies lifeless, his brains spilled over his armour:)
>
> 482 sanguine manat humus, campi sudore madescunt.
> (the ground streams with blood, and the fields are drenched with sweat.)

Apollo’s protection of Aeneas now becomes a return to action. The god does more than save the wounded Trojan: he restores his fighting spirit and sends him back into battle. The opening continues directly from the preceding verse, with Apollo still the subject and Aeneas the man he helps. In Homer, Apollo “sent Aeneas out from the rich sanctuary and put strength into the breast of the shepherd of the people” (*Iliad* 5.512–513). That title describes Aeneas as a leader responsible for his warriors. The Latin leaves out the sanctuary and the intervening account of his healing, joining his rescue and return in a swift statement of divine help.

The view then widens from one protected hero to the armies fighting all around him. These deaths have no counterpart in Homer: the Latin adds a general picture of the renewed battle. No victim receives a name, ancestry, or final speech. Instead, “one man,” “another,” and “here a man” direct our attention from one sudden catastrophe to the next. Dust hides the sky, shouting fills the air, and the movement of weapons, horses, and chariots makes the battlefield dangerous even beyond the instant of a wound. A man thrown from his chariot is killed beneath the hooves of the horses around him.

The warrior falling from his horse’s back appears to be a mounted fighter, an unusual detail in this setting. Homer’s leading warriors normally use chariots to reach or move about the battlefield and dismount to fight, rather than fighting as cavalry. Here the Latin seems to imagine the action in terms more familiar from later warfare. Yet the passage’s main concern is not military equipment but the bodily cost of combat. Its closing images bring the gaze down from the dust-covered sky to the blood-soaked ground: Aeneas has a god to restore and protect him, while the unnamed men around him are crushed, pierced, and cut apart.
