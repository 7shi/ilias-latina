### 454–459 (*Iliad* 5.297–302)

> 454 Iamque manum Aeneas simul et Calydonius heros
> (And now Aeneas and the Calydonian hero)
>
> 455 contulerant, iactis inter se comminus hastis;
> (had joined battle, having hurled their spears at each other at close quarters;)
>
> 456 undique rimabant inimico corpora ferro,
> (on every side they sought a place to wound each other with hostile steel,)
>
> 457 et modo cedebant retro, modo deinde coibant.
> (now falling back, now closing in again.)
>
> 458 Postquam utrique diu steterant nec uulnera magnus
> (After both had long stood their ground, and mighty)
>
> 459 qua daret infesto Tydides ense uidebat,
> (Tydides could see no place to inflict wounds with his hostile sword,)

After Pandarus’ death, Aeneas, another leader on the Trojan side, confronts his killer. The “Calydonian hero” and “Tydides” both mean Diomedes: his father Tydeus came from Calydon in Aetolia, a region of Greece. The changing names thus refer to a single opponent, not to two men fighting Aeneas.

The Latin substantially reshapes Aeneas’ response to his companion’s death. In Homer, Aeneas leaps forward with shield and spear because he fears that the Greeks will drag away Pandarus’ body. He stands over it “like a lion trusting in its strength,” roaring and ready to kill anyone who approaches (*Iliad* 5.297–302). The Latin leaves out the threatened body, the protective stance and the lion comparison. Instead, it makes the encounter a prolonged, evenly contested fight between two warriors.

Their movements give this added combat its rhythm: they exchange spear casts, close in, retreat and engage again. The probing with steel describes their search for an exposed place where a wound can be inflicted, rather than a succession of successful blows. After the violent destruction of Pandarus, Diomedes now faces resistance. Even this “mighty” fighter cannot discover an opening for his sword. The passage ends with its sentence unfinished, holding the action at the point where his usual means of attack has failed to secure a victory.
