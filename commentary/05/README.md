The book centers on the battlefield exploits of Diomedes, called Tydides, the Calydonian hero or youth, and Oenides. Seeing the Greeks retreat as Mars’ fury increases, he plunges into the thickest fighting. Pallas, described as warlike, the armed maiden, and later the holy warrior-maiden, strengthens his arms and gives him courage. Protected by her divine power, he scatters the Trojans like a ravenous lioness attacking cattle. The Greeks are also called Danaans and Achaeans; the Trojans Phrygians and Teucrians.

Diomedes confronts Phegeus and Idaeus, sons of Dares. Phegeus’ spear fails to penetrate his defense, and Diomedes kills him with a spear through the chest. Idaeus attempts to avenge his brother but cannot withstand Diomedes; compared to a bird helpless before a hawk killing its chick, he eventually withdraws and escapes death. The other Atrides, Agamemnon, also rages against the Trojans and kills Odius. Idomeneus attacks Maeonides Phaestus and, rejoicing at his death, sends the son of Strophius to the shades. Meriones strikes Phereclus, Meges strikes Pedaeus, and Eurypylus kills Hypsenor and strips him of his armor.

Pandarus searches for Diomedes with his curved, horn bow and grazes his shoulder with an arrow. Enraged rather than checked, Diomedes charges like a lion and kills Astynous, mighty Hypiron, Polyidus, Abas, Xanthus, renowned in war, huge Thoon, and finally Chromius and Echemmon. He then kills Pandarus with a devastating wound to the face and head. Diomedes and Aeneas exchange spears and fight at close quarters without immediately gaining an advantage. Diomedes finally lifts a rock that twelve young men could scarcely move and strikes Aeneas down.

Aeneas’ mother Venus descends, gathers him up, and conceals him in dark mist. Diomedes pursues even through this divine concealment and wounds her heavenly hand with his mortal spear. Venus, also called Cytherea, flees to heaven and complains to her heavenly mother. Trojan Apollo protects Aeneas, who is also called Dardanian Aeneas and Venus’ most beautiful son, restores his fighting spirit, and returns him to battle.

Amid widespread slaughter, Aeneas presses the Greeks hard. Hector, called the bravest of men and the sole hope of the Phrygians, likewise drives them back. His assault is compared to a hungry wolf attacking sheep despite their shepherd and dogs. The Trojans gain confidence as the Greek line falters. Agamemnon, the king of the Danaans, rides around rallying his leaders, then, as fierce Atrides, charges like a Libyan lion. His example restores Greek courage and reverses the rout. Atrides then attacks Aeneas, but his javelin misses its intended target and kills the charioteer instead. Aeneas leaps down and kills Crethon and Orsilochus. The ensuing account records the Paphlagonian leader’s death at Menelaus’ hands and Mydon’s at the hands of Antilochus.

Sarpedon, Jupiter’s renowned son, enters the fighting and kills Tlepolemus, son of great Hercules, whose paternal strength and labors cannot save him. Sarpedon is himself wounded and withdraws. Ulysses, called the contriver of deceit, then kills seven unnamed brave young warriors. Hector, now called Mavortian and the pillar of his homeland, and Diomedes fight on opposite sides, covering the fields with bodies and blood.

The book ends with divine combat. Mars, called Mavors and mighty in war, confronts chaste Pallas with his huge shield. Pallas herself drives him back, wounds him with her spear, and forces him to retreat to heaven. There he complains to Jupiter, the king of heaven and his mighty father, but receives rebukes. The closing scene is thus the wounded war-god’s humiliation in heaven; the human battle remains unresolved.
