### 443–444 (*Iliad* 5.144–147)

> 443 fertur et Astynoum, magnum quoque Hypirona fundit,
> (he rushes on and lays low Astynous and mighty Hypiron too,)
>
> 444 comminus hunc gladio, iaculo ferit eminus illum;
> (he strikes the latter at close quarters with his sword, the former from afar with his javelin;)

Diomedes’ renewed fury now produces its first victims. Continuing the lion-like charge of the preceding verse, he kills Astynous and Hypiron, two warriors on the Trojan side. The balanced account of their deaths shows him equally dangerous at close quarters and from a distance: Hypiron falls to his sword, Astynous to his javelin.

Homer describes the wounds in much greater detail. One man is struck above the nipple by a bronze-tipped spear; the other receives a sword-blow beside the collarbone that severs the shoulder from the neck and back (*Iliad* 5.144–147). The Latin preserves the two victims and the different weapons but replaces this bodily detail with a swift contrast between close and distant fighting. It also reduces Homer’s description of Hypiron as a “shepherd of the people,” marking him as a leader, to “mighty.” The result keeps attention on the speed and range of Diomedes’ assault rather than on the men he kills.
