### 510–512 (*Iliad* 5.561–563)

> 510 conspicit Atrides: stricto concurrere ferro
> (Atrides sees: to clash with drawn sword)
>
> 511 comparat et iaculum, quantum furor ipse mouebat,
> (he prepares, and his javelin, as far as his very fury moved him,)
>
> 512 uiribus intorquet, quod detulit error ab illo
> (with all his strength he hurls; a miss carried it away from him)

The sentence begun in the preceding passage now reaches its action: Atrides sees Aeneas approaching in his chariot and prepares to confront him. The drawn sword promises close combat, but first comes a javelin cast. For all the force behind it, the throw misses Aeneas. The Greek advance has brought the opposing champions together without yet deciding their encounter.

Atrides means “son of Atreus” and can name either Agamemnon, the Greek commander, or his brother Menelaus, whose wife Helen is now with Paris in Troy. The preceding charge belonged to Agamemnon; in the Homeric scene followed here, however, it is Menelaus who moves against Aeneas. The shared title leaves that change less explicit in the Latin. Homer says that Menelaus “pitied the two fallen men,” then advanced through the foremost fighters in gleaming bronze, shaking his spear (*Iliad* 5.561–563). Those men were Greek warriors killed by Aeneas: pity for them motivates Menelaus’ intervention.

The Latin drops that personal motive and makes the encounter follow directly from the sight of Aeneas. It also turns Homer’s threatening movement into an actual, unsuccessful attack, adding both the drawn sword and the missed throw. Where Homer says that Ares, the god of war, “roused his fighting spirit” (*Iliad* 5.563), the Latin foregrounds the warrior’s own fury. Strength and anger propel the weapon, but neither guarantees its aim.
