### 469–470 (*Iliad* 5.336–339, 5.343, 5.352–367)

> 469 caelestemque manum mortali uulnerat hasta.
> (and wounds her heavenly hand with a mortal’s spear.)
>
> 470 Icta petit caelum terris Cytherea relictis
> (Struck, Cytherea leaves the earth behind and makes for heaven.)

Diomedes’ reckless assault now reaches its mark. The contrast between a heavenly hand and a mortal’s spear brings the transgression into sharp focus: a human warrior has wounded a goddess. Cytherea is another name for Venus, associated with the island of Cythera, one of her traditional places of worship. Having entered the battle to rescue her son Aeneas, she is herself driven from it.

Homer describes the injury in close detail: the spear passes through the robe made for Aphrodite by the Graces and pierces her delicate hand; the goddess’s immortal blood flows from the wound (*Iliad* 5.336–339). She cries out and lets her son fall (*Iliad* 5.343). The Latin strips away these physical details, preserving the wound and the startling opposition between mortal and divine.

Her departure is equally compressed. In Homer, she does not simply return to heaven: Iris, the divine messenger, leads her out of the fighting, and she borrows the war god Ares’ horses. Iris then drives the distressed goddess to Olympus, the home of the gods (*Iliad* 5.352–367). The Latin turns this extended, painful withdrawal into a single swift movement from earth to heaven. Venus’s retreat ends her encounter with Diomedes, leaving his violence momentarily triumphant.
