### 483–485 (*Iliad* 5.514)

> 483 Emicat interea Veneris pulcherrima proles
> (Meanwhile Venus’ most beautiful son springs forward)
>
> 484 densaque Graiorum premit agmina nudaque late
> (and presses hard upon the Greeks’ close-packed ranks; far and wide)
>
> 485 terga metit gladio funestaque proelia miscet.
> (he reaps their unprotected backs with his sword and stirs up deadly fighting.)

The warrior emerging from the general slaughter is Aeneas, the Trojan leader whom Apollo has just restored to battle. Calling him Venus’ “most beautiful son” recalls both his divine parentage and the goddess who tried to rescue him when he was wounded. His beauty now stands beside his destructive force: newly strengthened, he attacks the Greeks in their massed ranks, cutting down men whose exposed backs suggest that they are turning to flee. The image of reaping makes his sword’s work resemble a harvest.

In Homer, this moment centres on Aeneas’ reunion with his own men: “Aeneas took his place among his companions, and they rejoiced” (*Iliad* 5.514). The Latin keeps his return but replaces their welcome with an immediate display of violence. The assault on the Greeks has no counterpart in that Homeric line. Following the anonymous deaths of the preceding passage, Aeneas now becomes the named agent of further slaughter: Apollo’s protection has not merely saved his life but restored his power to take the lives of others.
