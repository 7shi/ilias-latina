### 504–509 (*Iliad* 5.533–534, 5.561)

> 504 sic ferus Atrides aduersos fertur in hostes
> (so fierce Atrides rushes against the opposing enemy)
>
> 505 infestasque Phrygum proturbat cuspide turmas.
> (and with his spear drives back the hostile ranks of the Phrygians.)
>
> 506 Virtus clara ducis uires accendit Achiuum
> (The leader’s shining courage kindles the strength of the Achivi,)
>
> 507 et spes exacuit languentia militis arma:
> (and hope sharpens the soldiers’ flagging weapons:)
>
> 508 funduntur Teucri, Danai laetantur ouantes.
> (the Teucri are routed; the Danai rejoice in triumph.)
>
> 509 Tandem hic Aenean immisso tendere curru
> (At last he [sees] Aeneas advancing in his speeding chariot—)

The lion comparison now resolves into Agamemnon’s charge. He is called Atrides, “son of Atreus,” a name also applicable to his brother Menelaus. As the lion rushes among cattle, so Agamemnon scatters the enemy with his spear. His courage also restores his own soldiers’ confidence: their weapons seem to regain their edge as hope replaces exhaustion. The passage ends the charge with a sharp contrast between the fleeing Trojans and the triumphant Greeks. Phrygians and Teucri designate the Trojan side here; Achivi and Danai are names for the Greeks.

The Latin turns a particular feat of arms into a sweeping account of leadership and renewed morale. In Homer, Agamemnon throws his spear and strikes “a foremost warrior, Deicoon, companion of great-hearted Aeneas” (*Iliad* 5.533–534). Here the named victim disappears, and the spear instead drives back whole enemy formations. The following description of courage spreading through the Greek army has no direct counterpart in those Homeric lines. It makes Agamemnon’s assault the cause of a collective recovery, carrying forward the rally begun before the lion comparison.

The final, unfinished sentence turns attention to Aeneas, a leading Trojan warrior, approaching at speed in his chariot; the verb supplied in brackets makes its opening readable in isolation. This transition compresses a more involved sequence in Homer, where Aeneas kills two Greek warriors and Menelaus feels pity for them when they fall (*Iliad* 5.561). Here Aeneas enters immediately after the announcement of the Trojan rout. His arrival gives the victory a provisional character: the enemy still has a powerful champion ready to fight.
