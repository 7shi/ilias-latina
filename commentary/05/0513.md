### 513–516 (*Iliad* 8.119–122)

> 513 pectus in aurigae stomachoque infigitur alto;
> (it lodges in the charioteer’s chest and deep in his stomach;)
>
> 514 ille ruens ictu media inter lora rotasque
> (falling at the blow, he tumbles amid the reins and wheels)
>
> 515 uoluitur et uitam calido cum sanguine fundit.
> (and pours out his life with his warm blood.)
>
> 516 Ingemit Aeneas curruque animosus ab alto
> (Aeneas groans and, undaunted, from his high chariot…)

The javelin that missed Aeneas has not flown harmlessly away: it strikes his charioteer instead. The driver falls among the reins and wheels, his death leaving the Trojan champion without the man who controls his horses. Aeneas groans at the loss, but remains ready for action. The unfinished sentence turns attention back to him, poised to respond from his chariot.

Here the Latin brings an incident from a later Homeric battle into the encounter between Atrides and Aeneas. In the corresponding passage of the *Iliad*, it is the Greek warrior Diomedes who misses Hector, Troy’s leading defender, and kills Hector’s charioteer Eniopeus instead. Homer describes the driver holding the reins when the spear strikes his chest; he falls from the chariot, and the horses recoil (*Iliad* 8.119–122). The Latin keeps the fatal consequence of a missed cast but transfers it to different combatants, leaving Aeneas’ driver unnamed.

The retelling also shifts the emphasis from the startled horses to the dying man. His body tumbles among the chariot’s equipment, and his life drains away with his blood. This brief, violent interruption gives the unsuccessful attack a real cost before the narrative returns to the confrontation between the champions.
