### 447–448 (*Iliad* 5.159–160, 5.163–164)

> 447 Post hos infestus Chromiumque et Echemmona telo
> (After these, hostile, both Chromius and Echemmon with his swift)
>
> 448 proturbat celeri pariterque ad Tartara mittit.
> (weapon he drives back and together sends to Tartarus.)

Diomedes’ advance now reaches Chromius and Echemmon, two sons of Priam, the king of Troy. In Homer, he catches them “together in a single chariot” (*Iliad* 5.159–160), then forces both violently to the ground and strips off their armor (*Iliad* 5.163–164). The Latin leaves out their royal parentage and shared chariot, continuing the rapid succession of victims without pausing over their circumstances.

It also leaves out here Homer’s comparison of Diomedes to a lion attacking cattle, which it later gives to another warrior. Instead, his ferocity appears directly in the speed and violence of his attack. Sending both men “to Tartarus” makes their death explicit: Tartarus here means the underworld, the realm of the dead, rather than implying any special punishment for these warriors. Like Xanthus and Thoon before them, the pair fall together, sustaining the impression that no opponent can check Diomedes’ onslaught.
