### 447–448 (*Iliad* 5.159–160, 5.163–164)

> 447 Post hos infestus Chromiumque et Echemmona telo
> (After these, he fiercely attacks Chromius and Echemmon,)
>
> 448 proturbat celeri pariterque ad Tartara mittit.
> (striking them down with his swift weapon and sending both to Tartarus.)

Diomedes’ advance now reaches Chromius and Echemmon, two sons of Priam, the king of Troy. In Homer, he catches them “together in a single chariot” (*Iliad* 5.159–160), then forces both violently to the ground and strips off their armour (*Iliad* 5.163–164). The Latin leaves out their royal parentage and shared chariot, continuing the rapid succession of victims without pausing over their circumstances.

It also omits Homer’s comparison of Diomedes to a lion attacking cattle. Instead, his ferocity appears directly in the speed and violence of his attack. Sending both men “to Tartarus” makes their death explicit: Tartarus here means the underworld, the realm of the dead, rather than implying any special punishment for these warriors. Like Xanthus and Thoon before them, the pair fall together, sustaining the impression that no opponent can check Diomedes’ onslaught.
