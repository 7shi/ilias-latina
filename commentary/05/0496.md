### 496–499 (*Iliad* 5.528–532)

> 496 rex Danaum sublimis equo uolat agmina circum
> (the king of the Danaans, high on his horse, speeds around the ranks)
>
> 497 hortaturque duces animosque in proelia firmat.
> (and urges on the leaders, steeling their courage for battle.)
>
> 498 Mox ipse in medios audax se proripit hostes
> (Soon he himself boldly rushes into the midst of the enemy)
>
> 499 oppositasque acies stricto diuerberat ense.
> (and cuts through the opposing ranks with his drawn sword.)

The king is Agamemnon, commander of the Greek expedition against Troy; “Danaans” is another name for the Greeks. The opening completes the thought begun in the preceding passage: seeing his comrades giving way before Hector’s assault, Agamemnon moves to restore their courage. He first rallies his leaders, then leads by example, throwing himself into the fighting.

In Homer, Agamemnon likewise moves among the troops, urging them on, but his encouragement takes the form of a direct speech. He calls for courage and appeals to the soldiers’ sense of responsibility toward one another: “Respect one another in the fierce fighting.” Standing firm together offers a better chance of survival, whereas flight brings “neither glory nor strength to resist” (*Iliad* 5.529–532). The Latin compresses this appeal into a brief statement that he encourages the leaders and strengthens their resolve. It preserves the speech’s purpose without explaining the bonds of mutual respect on which it rests.

The mounted king and his sweeping sword attack give the Latin scene its own vivid shape. The final charge has no counterpart in the Homeric lines rendered here: it turns encouragement immediately into action. After Hector’s rush among the Greeks, Agamemnon now rushes among their enemies. His intervention supplies an answering display of courage, though the passage does not yet say whether it succeeds in reversing the Greek retreat.
