### 389–393 (*Iliad* 5.1, 5.8)

> 389 Hic postquam Danaum longe cedentia uidit
> (Then, when he saw, giving way far back, the Danaans’)
>
> 390 agmina Tydides tumidumque increscere Martem,
> (ranks, Tydides, and swollen Mars growing greater,)
>
> 391 in medias acies, qua plurimus imminet hostis,
> (into the midst of the lines, where the enemy presses thickest,)
>
> 392 irruit et uersas prosternit caede phalangas;
> (rushes in and with slaughter lays low the routed phalanxes;)
>
> 393 huc illuc ensemque ferox hastamque coruscat.
> (fiercely he flashes sword and spear this way and that.)

The battle now finds a new centre in **Tydides**, “the son of Tydeus”: Diomedes, one of the leading Greek warriors. The Danaans are the Greek army fighting against Troy. Where the preceding book ended with the struggle still undecided, this passage shows the Greeks giving ground. Diomedes answers their retreat with a charge into the greatest concentration of enemies, turning the pressure back upon the Trojans.

Mars, the Roman god of war, here also gives his name to war itself. Mars swollen and growing conveys the battle’s mounting violence; it need not mean that the god personally confronts Diomedes. Against this rising danger, the warrior appears in almost continuous motion—charging, scattering formations, and brandishing both weapons.

The Latin preserves Diomedes’ plunge into the thick of combat but changes its immediate impetus. Homer opens this episode with Pallas Athena giving Diomedes strength and courage, and she then “urged him into the midst, where the greatest numbers were in turmoil” (*Iliad* 5.8). Here that divine encouragement comes only after his charge: Diomedes first sees his comrades retreating and acts. The compressed account thus presents his intervention as a direct response to the army’s danger, bringing one warrior’s ferocity into focus amid the mass fighting.
