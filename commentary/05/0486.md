### 486–495 (*Iliad* 5.494–497)

> 486 Nec cessat spes una Phrygum fortissimus Hector
> (Nor does Hector, bravest of men, the sole hope of the Phrygians, cease)
>
> 487 sternere caede uiros atque agmina uertere Graium.
> (to strike men down in slaughter and rout the ranks of the Greeks.)
>
> 488 Vt lupus in campis pecudes cum uidit apertis
> (As a wolf, when it sees sheep in open fields,)
>
> 489 (non actor gregis ipse, comes non horrida terret
> (—neither the shepherd himself nor the fierce attendant)
>
> 490 turba canum), fremit esuriens et neglegit omnes
> (pack of dogs frightens it—growls hungrily, disregards them all,)
>
> 491 in mediosque greges auidus ruit, haut secus Hector
> (and rushes ravenously into the midst of the flocks, so Hector)
>
> 492 inuadit Danaos et territat ense cruento.
> (assails the Danaans and terrifies them with his bloodstained sword.)
>
> 493 Deficiunt Graiorum acies, Phryges acrius instant
> (The Greeks’ battle lines falter; the Phrygians press harder)
>
> 494 attolluntque animos: geminat uictoria uires.
> (and raise their spirits: victory doubles their strength.)
>
> 495 Vt uidit socios infesto cedere Marte,
> (When he saw his comrades giving way as battle turned against them,)

Aeneas’ renewed assault is followed by the continuing onslaught of Hector, Troy’s foremost defender. “Phrygians” here means the Trojans, while “Danaans” is another name for the Greeks. Calling Hector their “sole hope” expresses his people’s dependence on him, rather than denying the courage of Aeneas or their other champions. Together, the two leaders turn the fighting in Troy’s favour.

The Latin now draws on a moment that comes before Aeneas’ return in Homer. There Hector leaps from his chariot and moves throughout the army, brandishing his spears and urging his men to fight; they rally and face the Greeks (*Iliad* 5.494–497). The Latin preserves his power to drive the Trojan attack but turns the emphasis from leadership to personal slaughter. Instead of showing him encouraging his soldiers, it shows him cutting down their enemies. The Greeks’ collapse and the extended wolf comparison are additions to those Homeric lines.

The wolf makes Hector’s attack seem driven by an appetite that neither resistance nor danger can check. The shepherd and dogs should protect the flock, but the hungry predator ignores them and plunges straight among its victims. So Hector’s bloodstained sword inspires terror as well as inflicting wounds. That fear spreads through the Greek ranks, while success gives the Trojans fresh confidence: “victory doubles their strength” captures how an advantage can feed upon itself. The final line begins to turn attention towards a response on the Greek side. Its reference to hostile Mars, rendered here as battle turning against them, describes their worsening fortunes in war rather than necessarily a separate intervention by the war god.
