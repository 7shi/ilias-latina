### 535–536 (*Iliad* 5.859–887)

> 535 attonitumque simul caelum petere ipsa coegit.
> (and, stunned, at once she herself forced him to make for heaven.)
>
> 536 Hic ille aetherio queritur sua uulnera regi
> (Here he complains of his wounds to the king of heaven,)

Pallas’ victory is complete: she not only wounds Mavors but drives him from the battlefield. The war-god, Mars—the Roman counterpart of Ares—now becomes a wounded complainant before the “king of heaven,” Jupiter, whom Homer calls Zeus. His appeal echoes in similar words Venus’s complaint to her mother, reinforcing the contrast between the gods’ power in battle and their vulnerability when they encounter divine opposition.

In Homer, Ares’ departure is much more spectacular. Wounded through the combined action of Diomedes and Athena, he roars as loudly as nine or ten thousand warriors, terrifying Greeks and Trojans alike. As he rises towards heaven, he appears to Diomedes like a dark haze gathering beneath the clouds (*Iliad* 5.859–867). The Latin removes both the thunderous cry and the ominous appearance. Instead, it carries forward the preceding passage’s direct contest between the gods: Pallas herself forces her stunned opponent to withdraw.

The complaint is compressed just as sharply. In the *Iliad*, Ares reaches Olympus, the gods’ home, sits beside Zeus, and shows him the immortal blood flowing from his wound. He then blames Zeus for indulging his daughter Athena, the goddess called Pallas here. She has encouraged Diomedes to attack immortals: first Aphrodite, the goddess of love, and now Ares himself. He claims that only his swift escape saved him from further suffering among the dead (*Iliad* 5.868–887). All this accusation and self-pity becomes a single statement in the Latin. The essential reversal remains: the god who brings violence to others must now seek redress for his own wounds.
