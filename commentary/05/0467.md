### 467–468 (*Iliad* 5.330, 5.334–335)

> 467 fertur et in Venerem flagrantibus irruit armis,
> (is borne on, and rushes at Venus with blazing arms,)
>
> 468 et neque quem demens ferro petat inspicit aruis
> (and does not, in his madness, look at whom he attacks with iron on the field,)

Diomedes presses through the mist concealing Aeneas and turns his attack upon Venus herself. The goddess has come to save her son, but her divine status does not check the warrior’s charge. His blazing arms sustain the fiery image of his prowess in battle; “in his madness” now gives that prowess a disturbing edge. The force that has made him victorious against men is being directed against a goddess.

Homer makes the pursuit more deliberate. Diomedes goes after Aphrodite, the Greek name for Venus, “with pitiless bronze” (*Iliad* 5.330), knowing that she is not a goddess of warfare. He catches her amid the crowd and reaches forward to strike (*Iliad* 5.334–335). The Latin compresses this pursuit into a headlong assault and adds its own judgment of his recklessness. It need not mean that Diomedes fails to recognize Venus: rather, he attacks without regard for whom he is striking. Where Homer emphasizes his calculation that this particular goddess is vulnerable, the Latin emphasizes his furious disregard for the boundary between mortal and divine.
