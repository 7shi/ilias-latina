### 523–525 (*Iliad* 5.628, 5.638–642, 5.657–659)

> 523 Tlepolemus magno satus Hercule, sed neque uires
> (Tlepolemus, born of great Hercules; but neither the strength)
>
> 524 hunc seruare patris nec tot potuere labores,
> (of his father could save him nor so many labors)
>
> 525 quin caderet tenuemque daret de corpore uitam.
> (from falling and yielding the faint breath of life from his body.)

The warrior facing Sarpedon is now named: Tlepolemus, a Greek commander and son of Hercules, the celebrated hero whose strength and extraordinary labors could not protect his mortal son. Both opponents have illustrious fathers—Sarpedon is a son of Jupiter—but ancestry offers Tlepolemus no escape from the death already foreshadowed by “ill-fated” in the preceding passage.

In Homer, Tlepolemus himself proudly invokes his father’s achievements while taunting Sarpedon. Hercules had attacked Troy in an earlier generation, after its king, Laomedon, withheld horses promised to him. With only six ships and a small force, Hercules captured the city. The Latin removes the warriors’ exchange of speeches and turns Tlepolemus’ boast into a reflection on the limits of inherited glory: his father’s victories cannot save him in this new war at Troy.

The death itself is also compressed. Homer describes Sarpedon’s spear passing through Tlepolemus’ neck, after which “dark night covered his eyes” (*Iliad* 5.659). The Latin replaces the wound and the enveloping darkness with life departing like a faint breath. This image may recall an ancient conception of each creature’s life as a small share of a universal life-spirit, released from the body at death. Here its immediate force is simple: despite his heroic parentage, Tlepolemus gives up his life.
