### 413–423 (*Iliad* 5.20–24)

> 413 conspexit frater, stricto celer aduolat ense
> (his brother sees him and swiftly rushes up with drawn sword,)
>
> 414 germanique cupit fatorum exsistere uindex.
> (eager to stand forth as the avenger of his brother’s death.)
>
> 415 Sed neque uim saeui nec fortia sustinet arma
> (But he cannot withstand the might or the powerful weapons)
>
> 416 Tydidae contraque tamen defendere temptat.
> (of savage Tydides, yet still tries to resist him.)
>
> 417 Vt uolucris, discerpta sui cum corpora nati
> (As a bird, when she sees her chick’s mangled body)
>
> 418 accipitrem laniare uidet nec tendere contra,
> (being torn by a hawk, cannot confront it)
>
> 419 auxilium neque ferre suo ualet anxia nato
> (or, in her anguish, bring help to her chick,)
>
> 420 quodque potest, leuibus plaudit sua pectora pennis,
> (and does what she can, beating her breast with light wings,)
>
> 421 sic hostem Idaeus germani caede superbum
> (so Idaeus watches the enemy exulting in his brother’s slaughter,)
>
> 422 spectat atrox miseroque nequit succurrere fratri
> (fierce, yet unable to come to his wretched brother’s aid;)
>
> 423 et, nisi cessisset, dextra cecidisset eadem.
> (and, had he not withdrawn, he would have fallen by the same right hand.)

The witness to Phegeus’ death is his brother **Idaeus**. Seeing him dying, Idaeus draws his sword and rushes forward to avenge him. But affection and anger cannot make him a match for **Tydides**, Diomedes, the son of Tydeus. His fierce determination gives way to helplessness: he cannot save his brother, and remaining beside him would only mean sharing his death.

This gives Idaeus a more active and sympathetic part than he has in Homer. In the *Iliad*, he leaps from the chariot and “did not dare stand over his slain brother” (*Iliad* 5.20–21). Standing over a fallen warrior meant defending his body against the enemy. The Latin replaces that immediate failure of nerve with an attempted resistance, then adds the comparison with a bird watching a hawk tear apart her young. The unequal struggle makes Idaeus’ inability to help seem less a matter of cowardice than of overwhelming odds. The bird’s beating wings also resemble the human gesture of beating one’s breast in grief: the impulse to protect survives when protection is no longer possible.

The ending changes the explanation of his escape. Homer says that Idaeus would not have escaped death either, but Hephaestus, the divine smith, “saved him by covering him in darkness,” so that his aged father would not be utterly overcome by grief (*Iliad* 5.22–24). Their father, Dares, is Hephaestus’ priest. Here that divine rescue is left out, and the closing emphasis falls on Idaeus’ withdrawal. Diomedes remains the irresistible victor, while the surviving brother retreats with both his grief and his frustrated desire for vengeance.
