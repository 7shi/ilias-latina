### 530–531 (*Iliad* 5.793)

> 530 illinc Tydides: sternuntur utrimque uirorum
> (on that, Tydides: laid low on both sides are men’s)
>
> 531 corpora per campos et sanguine prata rigantur.
> (bodies across the fields, and the meadows are watered with blood.)

Opposite Hector stands Tydides, “the son of Tydeus”: Diomedes, the Greek champion whose exploits have dominated this part of the battle. The two warriors represent the opposing armies, Hector defending Troy and Diomedes fighting among its attackers. Their juxtaposition does not mean that they meet in single combat. Instead, the view widens from the champions to the slaughter around them, with men falling on both sides.

The Latin moves rapidly over the intervening action in Homer. At the corresponding point in the *Iliad*, Athena, the goddess who supports Diomedes, rushes towards him (*Iliad* 5.793). Here the immediate focus remains on human combat rather than divine assistance. The closing image turns the battlefield into a landscape soaked with its fighters’ blood: the meadows are “watered,” but with blood rather than rain. After the prominence given to individual heroes, these anonymous bodies make the shared cost of their fighting visible.
