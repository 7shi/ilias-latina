### 433 (*Iliad* 5.69)

> 433 Pedaeumque Meges. Tum uastis horridus armis
> (and Meges Pedaeus. Then, fearsome in massive armour,)

The rapid succession of Greek victories continues: as Meriones strikes Phereclus, **Meges**, another Greek commander, strikes **Pedaeus**, a Trojan warrior. The Latin compresses this encounter still further, borrowing the action from the preceding sentence and giving only the names of the victim and his attacker.

Homer identifies Pedaeus as a son of **Antenor**, one of Troy’s elders (*Iliad* 5.69). He then adds a brief glimpse of the household behind the fallen warrior: although Pedaeus was born to another woman, Antenor’s wife Theano had lovingly raised him alongside her own children. The Latin omits both this family history and Homer’s description of the fatal wound. Instead, it moves immediately towards the next encounter, introducing a fearsome figure in massive armour whose identity the following verse will reveal.
