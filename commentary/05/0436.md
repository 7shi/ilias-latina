### 436–441 (*Iliad* 5.95–99, 5.134–135)

> 436 Parte alia uolitat sinuoso Pandarus arcu
> (Elsewhere Pandarus darts about with his curved bow)
>
> 437 Tydidenque oculis immensa per agmina quaerit;
> (and searches with his eyes for Tydides through the vast ranks;)
>
> 438 quem postquam Troum sternentem corpora uidit,
> (when he saw him laying Trojan warriors low,)
>
> 439 horrida contento derexit spicula cornu
> (he aimed his fearsome shafts from his drawn horn bow)
>
> 440 et summas umeri destringit acumine partes.
> (and grazes the surface of his shoulder with the arrow’s point.)
>
> 441 Tum uero ardescit iuuenis Calydonius ira
> (Then indeed the Calydonian youth blazes with anger)

The scene shifts from the brief Greek victories to an attack on **Tydides**, “the son of Tydeus,” meaning **Diomedes**. The “Calydonian youth” is the same warrior, identified through his father’s connection with Calydon in Aetolia, a region of western Greece. Diomedes has been cutting through the Trojan forces; now **Pandarus**, an archer fighting on the Trojan side, tries to stop him. The arrow merely grazes his shoulder, and the passage ends not with his defeat but with his anger flaring up.

The Latin makes Pandarus actively search for his opponent across the battlefield. In Homer, he notices Diomedes charging over the plain and driving the enemy ranks before him; here that picture becomes the more explicitly lethal one of Diomedes laying Trojans low. The curved bow and the shoulder wound remain, but the wound is reduced. Homer’s arrow passes through Diomedes’ breastplate and draws blood, whereas the Latin describes only a graze (*Iliad* 5.95–100).

A much longer sequence is then compressed into the warrior’s immediate anger. In Homer, Pandarus boasts of his successful shot, Diomedes has his companion Sthenelus remove the arrow, and he prays to Athena. The goddess renews his strength and gives him the power to distinguish gods from mortals on the battlefield before he returns to the fighting (*Iliad* 5.100–135). By passing over these exchanges here, the Latin makes the response swift and direct: the archer’s attempt to check Diomedes only inflames him.
