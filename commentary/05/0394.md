### 394–395 (*Iliad* 5.1–2, 5.4–7, 5.3)

> 394 Bellica Pallas adest flagrantiaque ignibus arma
> (Warlike Pallas is at hand, and his arms, blazing with fires,)
>
> 395 adiuuat atque animos iuueni uiresque ministrat.
> (she aids, and supplies the young man with courage and strength.)

Diomedes’ charge now receives divine backing. **Pallas** is Athena, the goddess who supports the Greeks and gives particular aid to Diomedes. The “young man” is Diomedes himself. Her presence strengthens both his fighting equipment and the warrior within it: his arms blaze with supernatural fire, while she supplies the courage and physical power needed to press his attack.

The Latin has delayed, rather than removed, the divine intervention with which Homer begins this episode. In the *Iliad*, Athena first empowers Diomedes so that he may stand out among the Greeks and win glory (*Iliad* 5.1–3); here he has already rushed forward to rescue a retreating army before her support is mentioned. This ordering lets his own initiative appear first, then reveals the divine power sustaining it.

Homer describes fire streaming from Diomedes’ helmet and shield, comparing its brilliance to an autumn star freshly bathed in the Ocean (*Iliad* 5.4–7). The Latin keeps the dazzling armour but omits the comparison and the explicit promise of glory. Its brief image makes Diomedes visibly exceptional amid the fighting; later, he will again charge in blazing arms.
