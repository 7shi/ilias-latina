### 836–838 (*Iliad* 17.132–137)

> 836 Vindicat exstincti corpus Telamonius Aiax
> (Telamonian Aiax defends the dead man’s body)
>
> 837 oppositoque tegit clipeo. Priameia pubes
> (and covers it with his shield held before him.)
>
> 838 laetitia exsultat, Danai sua uulnera maerent.
> (Priam’s young men exult in joy; the Danai mourn their wounds.)

Patroclus is dead, but his body remains on the battlefield and needs protection. Aiax, the son of Telamon and one of the strongest Greek warriors, steps forward to defend it. He is not Achilles, whose armour Patroclus wore into battle, but another champion of the same army. His intervention shifts the story from Patroclus’ fatal encounter with Hector to the struggle over the fallen man’s body. Hector’s victory has not left the Trojans in undisputed possession of the field.

The Latin preserves the central image of Homer’s account: Aiax standing over Patroclus behind a broad, protective shield. It leaves out the extended comparison that gives this stance its emotional force. In the *Iliad*, Aiax resembles a lion guarding its cubs when hunters encounter it in the woods, gathering its strength and lowering its brow threateningly (*Iliad* 17.132–137). Here, that fierce protectiveness is compressed into two actions: defending and covering.

In place of the lion’s threatening watchfulness, the passage closes with the opposing emotions of the armies. “Priam’s young men” are the Trojan warriors, named through Priam, their king and Hector’s father; the Danai are the Greeks. This contrast between Trojan rejoicing and Greek suffering has no direct counterpart in the Homeric lines rendered here. It broadens the scene from one warrior’s defence of a body to the consequences of Patroclus’ death for both sides.
