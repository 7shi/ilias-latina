### 136–138 (*Iliad* 2.212, 2.216, 2.213–215, 2.225–242)

> 136 Hic tunc Thersites, quo non deformior alter
> (Then Thersites—no uglier man)
>
> 137 uenerat ad Troiam nec lingua proteruior ulli,
> (had come to Troy, nor had anyone a more insolent tongue—)
>
> 138 bella gerenda negat patriasque hortatur ad oras
> (says they must not wage war and urges a return to their native shores.)

The agreement between Agamemnon and the Greek chiefs is immediately broken by a dissenting voice. Thersites, a man in the Greek army, opposes further fighting and urges the men to go home. Before we hear his proposal, however, the narrator has already discredited him: his exceptional ugliness is paired with exceptional insolence. Against the chiefs’ promises of united military strength, his intervention appears as a disruptive challenge.

In Homer, Thersites speaks before the assembled army, not during the council of chiefs. Between these scenes, Agamemnon tests his soldiers by proposing that they abandon the siege of Troy. They take him at his word and rush toward their ships, until Odysseus brings them back under control. Thersites then continues speaking when everyone else has settled down. By omitting this sequence, the Latin moves directly from the leaders’ unanimous support to one man’s opposition, without showing how eagerly the army itself had welcomed the prospect of departure.

The Latin also greatly reduces Thersites’ attack. Homer gives him a substantial speech accusing Agamemnon of greed: the king already has abundant bronze and captive women, yet apparently wants still more rewards from the fighting. Thersites urges the Greeks to sail home and leave him at Troy, so that he can discover whether he needs their help (*Iliad* 2.236–238). He also condemns Agamemnon’s treatment of Achilles, whose prize the king has seized. These accusations connect his protest with the quarrel that has already divided the Greek leadership. Here, only the practical demand remains: stop fighting and return home. The grievances behind it disappear, while the narrator’s hostile judgment of the speaker is retained.
