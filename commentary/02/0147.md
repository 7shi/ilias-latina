### 147–151 (*Iliad* 2.303–308, 2.311–317)

> 147 temporis illius, quo uisus in Aulide serpens
> (of that time when a serpent appeared at Aulis)
>
> 148 consumpsit uolucrum bis quattuor arbore fetus
> (and devoured twice four young birds in a tree,)
>
> 149 atque ipsam inualido pugnantem corpore contra
> (and, as she herself fought back with her feeble body,)
>
> 150 addidit extremo natorum funere matrem.
> (added the mother last to her young in death.)
>
> 151 Tunc "sic deinde" senex "moneo remoneboque, Achiui:
> (Then the old man said, “So I counsel you, and shall counsel you again, Achiui:)

Nestor recalls an omen witnessed at Aulis, the Greek port where the fleet gathered before sailing against Troy. His recollection takes the army back to the expedition’s beginning, when the Greeks had received a sign that would later give them reason to persevere. The eight young birds and their mother make nine victims: their number matters because Calchas, the army’s diviner, interpreted the event as a prediction of the war’s duration.

In Homer, the memory unfolds within a vivid scene of sacrifice. The Greeks are offering sacrifices to the gods beside a spring, beneath a plane tree, when the serpent appears. The nestlings cry as it eats them; their mother flies around them in distress until the serpent catches her by the wing. The Latin reduces this setting to Aulis and a tree, concentrating instead on the destruction of the nest. It also makes the mother’s helpless resistance explicit: she fights back, but her frail body cannot save either her young or herself. Her death completes both the family’s destruction and the ominous count.

The old man then turns from recollection to renewed advice. “Achiui” means the Achaeans, the Greeks fighting at Troy. Nestor continues to occupy the place held by Odysseus—Vlixes in the Latin—in Homer’s speech. His promise to counsel them again presents perseverance as a lesson that must be repeatedly impressed upon a discouraged army.
