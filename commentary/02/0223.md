### 223–225 (*Iliad* 2.786–787, 2.796–801, 2.807–808)

> 223 cum pater ad Priamum mittit Saturnius Irim,
> (when the father, Saturn’s son, sends Iris to Priam,)
>
> 224 quae doceat fortes uenisse ad bella Pelasgos.
> (to tell him that the brave Pelasgi have come to battle.)
>
> 225 Nec mora: continuo iussu capit arma parentis
> (Without delay, he at once takes up arms at his father’s command)

The story turns from the Greek army on the plain to the Trojans preparing to meet it. Saturn’s son is Jupiter, the Roman counterpart of Homer’s Zeus, here called “father” as ruler of gods and men. He sends the divine messenger Iris to Priam, the aged king of Troy. “Pelasgi” is a poetic name for the Greeks: her message is that they have come to battle.

The Latin compresses a much fuller scene. In Homer, Iris appears in the likeness of Priam’s son Polites, who has been watching for the Greeks’ movements. She rebukes the old king for continuing discussions as though it were peacetime and describes an approaching army as numerous as “leaves or grains of sand” (*Iliad* 2.800–801). Here the disguise, rebuke, and comparison disappear. The message becomes a plain announcement of the enemy’s approach, moving the story quickly toward the fighting.

The warrior taking up arms in the unfinished final sentence is Hector, Priam’s son and Troy’s foremost defender. Homer has Hector recognize the goddess’s message and immediately dismiss the assembly, after which the Trojans rush for their weapons (*Iliad* 2.807–808). The Latin instead presents his action as obedience to his father’s command. The urgency remains, but the response is concentrated in Hector himself, linking the king’s authority directly to his son’s readiness for battle.
