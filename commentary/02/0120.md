### 120–128 (*Iliad* 2.16–23, 2.26–29)

> 120 Nec mora: Somnus abit leuibusque per aera pennis
> (Without delay Somnus departs, and on light wings through the air)
>
> 121 deuolat in thalamos Agamemnonis: ille sopore
> (flies down into Agamemnon’s chamber: he lay stretched out,)
>
> 122 corpus inundatum leni prostratus habebat.
> (his body steeped in gentle sleep.)
>
> 123 Ad quem sic loquitur curarum operumque leuator:
> (The reliever of cares and labours speaks to him thus:)
>
> 124 "Rex Danaum, Atrida, uigila et mandata Tonantis
> (“King of the Danai, Atrides, awake and hear the Thunderer’s orders,)
>
> 125 quae tibi iussa simul delatus ab aethere porto,
> (which I bring you as commanded, having come down from heaven:)
>
> 126 accipe: cum primum Titan se emerserit undis,
> (as soon as Titan has risen from the waves,)
>
> 127 fortibus arma iube socios aptare lacertis
> (order your comrades to fit their armour to their strong arms)
>
> 128 et petere Iliacos instructo milite campos."
> (and make for the Ilian plains with troops drawn up for battle.”)

Jupiter’s command is carried out at once. Somnus, the god of sleep, flies down to Agamemnon and finds him already immersed in the gentle sleep over which he himself presides. The description preserves Homer’s image of sleep poured around the king: the dream finds Agamemnon sleeping in his shelter, with “immortal sleep poured about him” (*Iliad* 2.19). In the Latin, the king’s whole body seems steeped in rest. Calling Somnus the “reliever of cares and labours” deepens the contrast between his comforting presence and the dangerous business that brings him here.

Homer’s messenger approaches in a more personal disguise. The dream stands above Agamemnon’s head in the likeness of Nestor, an elderly Greek leader whom the king particularly respects (*Iliad* 2.20–22). The Latin leaves out that borrowed face and the trust it would inspire. Somnus speaks directly, grounding his authority in the god who sent him. The “Thunderer” is Jupiter; “Atrides” identifies Agamemnon as a son of Atreus, while the Danai are the Greeks whom he commands. These titles address him not merely as a sleeping man but as a king responsible for putting an army into action.

The message closely repeats the instructions Jupiter has just given. Titan is the sun, imagined as rising out of the sea; his appearance will be the signal to arm and advance onto the plains around Ilium, or Troy. Homer’s dream promises that Agamemnon can now capture the city (*Iliad* 2.28–29). Here the message is confined to a practical military order, without that explicit promise of victory. Nor does Somnus repeat Jupiter’s reference to catching the enemy unprepared: the emphasis falls on the Greeks’ own readiness and ordered advance. Yet the apparent help remains deceptive. Jupiter is fulfilling his promise to honour the absent Achilles, whose value the Greeks are to learn through suffering. The god who normally releases men from their troubles has arrived to summon them back into war.
