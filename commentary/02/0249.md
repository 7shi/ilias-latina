### 249–251 (*Iliad* 2.876)

> 249 Sarpedon claraque satus tellure Coroebus.
> (Sarpedon and Coroebus, born in a renowned land.)
>
> 250 His se defendit ducibus Neptunia Troia
> (With these leaders Neptune’s Troy defends itself)
>
> 251 uicissetque dolos Danaum, ni fata fuissent.
> (and would have overcome the deceit of the Danaans, had it not been for fate.)

Sarpedon completes the preceding verse’s announcement of the “illustrious son of Jupiter.” He is one of the commanders of the Lycians, allies who have come from southwestern Asia Minor to defend Troy. Homer pairs him with Glaucus at the close of the Trojan catalogue; the Latin ends with Coroebus, who does not appear in Homer’s list. In the wider story of Troy, Coroebus is a son of the Phrygian king Mygdon who comes to help the Trojans and dies during the city’s fall. The phrase “born in a renowned land” honours his homeland without naming it.

The closing sentence gathers all these commanders into a single picture of Troy’s defence. “Neptune’s Troy” recalls the tradition that Neptune, the sea god whom the Greeks call Poseidon, helped build the city’s walls. Its fortifications thus have a divine origin, just as one of its defenders has a divine father. Yet neither advantage can protect it against its appointed end.

The Danaans are the Greeks besieging Troy. Their “deceit” seems to look ahead to the stratagem of the wooden horse, through which they will finally enter and destroy the city. These concluding lines have no direct counterpart in Homer’s catalogue: the Latin poet adds a brief judgment on the whole war. Troy has formidable defenders and could have defeated even Greek cunning, but fate determines otherwise. The catalogue therefore ends not simply as a roll of names, but as a tribute to a defence already overshadowed by the city’s eventual fall.
