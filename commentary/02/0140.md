### 140–143 (*Iliad* 2.245–246, 2.265–266)

> 140 correptum dictis sceptro percussit eburno.
> (rebuked him with words and struck him with an ivory sceptre.)
>
> 141 Tum uero ardescit conceptis litibus ira:
> (Then indeed anger blazes up as quarrels arise:)
>
> 142 uix telis caruere manus, ad sidera clamor
> (hands were scarcely kept from weapons; to the stars a shout)
>
> 143 tollitur et cunctos pugnandi corripit ardor.
> (rises, and eagerness to fight seizes them all.)

Ulysses, the Greek leader known in Homer as Odysseus, answers Thersites’ call to abandon the war with both a rebuke and a blow. The sceptre is a symbol of authority, carried by leaders and speakers at an assembly; here it becomes an instrument of punishment. The Latin compresses a substantial confrontation into one line. In Homer, Odysseus sharply condemns Thersites for challenging the kings and threatens further humiliation if he persists. He then strikes him across the back and shoulders, making him double over and shed tears (*Iliad* 2.265–266). The Latin retains the essential combination of verbal correction and physical force, but omits the speech and the victim’s response.

The emphasis then shifts from Thersites to the whole gathering. Homer describes the Greeks laughing at Thersites and approving Odysseus’ intervention: the dissenter’s humiliation helps restore the leaders’ authority. These Latin verses instead introduce a surge of anger, quarrelling, and almost armed violence, culminating in a shout that reaches the stars. Everyone is caught up in the desire to fight, but the immediate target of that aggression is not specified. The quarrels suggest dangerous tension within the Greek gathering even as the final words evoke the fighting spirit needed to resume the war. Rather than lingering over one man’s disgrace, the retelling makes the encounter release a collective, volatile energy.
