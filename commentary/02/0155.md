### 155–160 (*Iliad* 2.398, 2.381–384, 2.442–444, 2.465)

> 155 conciliumque simul dimittitur. Arma parari
> (and at once the assembly is dismissed. Weapons to be prepared)
>
> 156 dux iubet atque animos aptare et pectora pugnae.
> (the leader orders, and minds and hearts to be fitted for battle.)
>
> 157 Postera lux tacitas ut primum dispulit umbras
> (As soon as the next day’s light drove away the silent shadows)
>
> 158 et nitidum Titan radiis caput extulit undis,
> (and Titan raised his shining head from the waves,)
>
> 159 protinus armari socios iubet acer Atrides
> (at once fierce Atrides orders his comrades to arm)
>
> 160 et petere Iliacos instructo milite campos.
> (and advance in battle order onto the plains of Ilium.)

Agreement gives way to action. With Nestor’s reminder of the promised victory accepted, the assembly disperses and the Greek commander resumes control. Atrides means “son of Atreus”: here it identifies Agamemnon, the leader of the expedition against Troy, also called Ilium. His order concerns both equipment and resolve. The troops must prepare their weapons, but they must also ready themselves inwardly for the fighting ahead.

The Latin gathers several moments of Homer’s narrative into a brief sequence. In the *Iliad*, Agamemnon gives practical instructions before dismissing the assembly: the men are to eat, sharpen their spears, check their shields and chariots, and feed their horses (*Iliad* 2.381–384). Here those particulars become a general command to prepare for battle. The meals and sacrifices that follow in Homer disappear, as do the heralds through whom Agamemnon later summons the army.

The Latin also introduces a new morning, whereas Homer’s preparations and advance belong to the same day. Titan is the sun god, pictured rising from the sea: his radiant emergence marks a fresh beginning after the army’s wavering. At once Agamemnon orders the advance onto the open ground before Troy, which Homer names as the plain of the river Scamander (*Iliad* 2.465). The closing line repeats the call to take the field already heard in verse 128; now it follows the army’s unanimous assent, carrying the story from debate toward battle.
