### 111–112 (*Iliad* 2.1–2)

> 111 Nox erat et toto fulgebant sidera mundo
> (It was night, and stars shone across the whole sky)
>
> 112 humanumque genus requies diuumque tenebat,
> (and rest held humankind and the gods,)

The story resumes in the quiet that closed the preceding book, when the gods retired after their feast. The star-filled sky extends over gods and mortals alike, briefly setting aside the quarrels that have disturbed both heaven and the Greek camp. Yet those quarrels remain unresolved: Achilles has withdrawn from battle, and Jupiter has promised his mother, Thetis, to avenge his dishonour.

The Latin poet adds the stars to Homer’s opening and replaces his more specific description of the mortal warriors with the broader “humankind.” These two lines thus present a sweeping picture of universal rest. Homer immediately makes an important exception: the other gods and the warriors sleep through the night, but “sweet sleep did not hold” the king of the gods (*Iliad* 2.1–2). That exception is not yet expressed here; for the moment, the Latin leaves everything beneath the night sky apparently at peace.
