### 236 (*Iliad* 2.819–821)

> 236 et sacer Aeneas, Veneris certissima proles,
> (and sacred Aeneas, Venus’s undoubted son,)

Aeneas joins the Trojan princes following Hector into battle. He is not another son of Priam, but a warrior of the wider Trojan royal family, distinguished here by his divine mother. Venus, the Roman goddess of love, corresponds to Homer’s Aphrodite. Calling Aeneas “sacred” marks his special connection with the gods; “undoubted son” emphatically affirms that divine descent.

Homer introduces Aeneas as commander of the Dardanians, a people closely connected with the Trojans. He names Aeneas’s mortal father, Anchises, and briefly recalls how Aphrodite bore him after lying with Anchises on the slopes of Mount Ida, near Troy. The Latin compresses this introduction into a single line, omitting Aeneas’s separate command, his father, and the circumstances of his birth. What remains is his name and his relationship to Venus.

For Roman readers, that relationship carried particular importance: Aeneas was remembered as the Trojan survivor whose journey to Italy would lay the foundations for Rome’s ancestry. Here, however, he appears simply among Troy’s defenders, his divine parentage adding distinction to the warriors gathered behind Hector.
