### 139 (*Iliad* 2.236, 2.244)

> 139 uertere iter, quem consiliis illustris Vlixes
> (to turn their course; but Ulysses, renowned for his counsel,)

The opening words complete Thersites’ appeal to turn homeward rather than continue the war. The sentence then turns to the man who will answer him: Ulysses, the Greek leader called Odysseus in Homer. The line ends before describing his response, but his introduction already sets him against Thersites. Where the dissenter has been marked by ugliness and an insolent tongue, Ulysses is distinguished by his counsel: his speech carries the authority of an established adviser.

In Homer, Odysseus promptly comes to stand beside Thersites after his attack on Agamemnon (*Iliad* 2.244). The Latin preserves that immediate confrontation while compressing the speech that provokes it. Homer calls Odysseus “godlike” at this point; “renowned for his counsel” instead foregrounds the intelligence and resourcefulness that characterize him throughout the poem. The contrast prepares the reader to hear his intervention not merely as one soldier contradicting another, but as an authoritative answer to a challenge against the Greek leadership.
