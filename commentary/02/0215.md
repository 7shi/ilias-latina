### 215–216 (*Iliad* 2.698, 2.704, 2.710, 2.534)

> 215 Protesilaus agit totidem fortisque Podarces
> (Protesilaus and brave Podarces lead)
>
> 216 instructas puppes, quot duxit Oileos Aiax;
> (as many fully equipped ships as Oileos Aiax led;)

Protesilaus and Podarces bring the men of Phylace and neighboring settlements in Thessaly. Their fleet numbers forty ships, matching that of Oileos Aiax—the son of Oileus, commander of the Locrians, and distinct from the other Aiax, son of Telamon. The comparison links this contingent to one already named in the catalogue.

The Latin places Protesilaus and Podarces together as leaders, but Homer tells a story of loss and succession. Protesilaus was the first Greek to leap ashore at Troy and was killed there, leaving his wife and an unfinished house behind. His younger brother Podarces then took command. Though the men had a capable leader, they still mourned Protesilaus. The abridgment leaves out this glimpse of the war’s human cost, retaining the two commanders’ names, Podarces’ bravery, and the size of their fleet. What Homer presents as a command interrupted by death becomes another compact entry in the muster of ships and fighting men.
