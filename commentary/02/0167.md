### 167–170 (*Iliad* 2.494–495, 2.509–510)

> 167 Peneleos princeps et bello Leitus acer,
> (Peneleos the leader and Leitus fierce in war,)
>
> 168 Arcesilaus atrox Prothoenorque Cloniusque
> (fierce Arcesilaus, Prothoenor, and Clonius,)
>
> 169 Boeoti decies quinas egere carinas
> (all Boeotians, brought fifty ships)
>
> 170 et tumidos ualido pulsarunt remige fluctus.
> (and with strong rowers beat the swelling waves.)

The catalogue begins with the Boeotians, the people of Boeotia in central Greece. The five men named here are their commanders, jointly leading a contingent of fifty ships. As the Greek army prepares for battle before Troy, the account looks back to the voyage that brought its separate forces together.

The Latin preserves Homer’s five leaders in their original order and retains the number of their ships, but greatly shortens the description of their contingent. Homer names the many towns and districts from which the Boeotians came; here, their shared regional identity takes the place of that detailed geography. The promised account of homelands thus begins in a much compressed form.

Homer also specifies that each ship carried 120 young Boeotians (*Iliad* 2.509–510). The Latin omits this precise measure of manpower and instead closes with a picture of powerful rowing through swelling seas. Together with the descriptions of Leitus and Arcesilaus as fierce warriors, this added image gives energy to what might otherwise be a bare list of names and numbers.
