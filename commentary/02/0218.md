### 218–222 (*Iliad* 2.731–733)

> 218 quem sequitur iuxta Podalirius atque Machaon,
> (whom Podalirius and Machaon follow close behind,)
>
> 219 altaque ter denis sulcarunt aequora proris.
> (and they plowed the deep seas with thirty ships.)
>
> 220 His ducibus Graiae Troiana ad litora puppes
> (Under these leaders to the shores of Troy the Greek ships)
>
> 221 bis septem uenere minus quam mille ducentae.
> (came, fourteen fewer than twelve hundred.)
>
> 222 Iamque citi appulerant classes camposque tenebant,
> (And now they had swiftly brought their fleets ashore and held the plains,)

Podalirius and Machaon follow Philoctetes in the list of commanders. They are brothers, sons of Aesculapius, the god of healing, and both are physicians as well as leaders of troops. Homer introduces them as “two sons of Asclepius, skilled physicians” (*Iliad* 2.731–732). The Latin keeps their names and their thirty ships but leaves out their parentage and medical skill. Like the preceding entry for Philoctetes, this reduces a distinctive pair of warriors to their place in the fleet. The image of ships plowing the deep sea repeats wording used earlier in the Latin catalogue, giving these successive contingents a familiar rhythm.

With these brothers, the Latin brings its catalogue to an end, although Homer continues with several more contingents. “These leaders” therefore gathers up all the commanders already named, not just Podalirius and Machaon. The total of 1,186 ships is the traditional sum drawn from Homer’s full catalogue; it conveys the scale of the Greek expedition even though the Latin has greatly shortened the list. Homer does not give this total in a corresponding closing statement.

The final line turns from counting ships to placing the army on Trojan soil. Read on its own, it describes the fleets landing and taking the plain, as if the expedition had only now arrived, although Nestor has just spoken of the war’s tenth year. It may recall the arrival that the catalogue has been describing, or refer to the army now taking the field; either way it returns the reader from the catalogue to the setting of the fighting.
