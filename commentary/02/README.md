The book opens at night, while human beings and gods are at rest. The “almighty father,” also called the “Thunderer,” sends Somnus to Agamemnon’s camp with orders for an attack at dawn. Somnus, the “gentlest of the gods” and “reliever of cares and labours,” flies to the sleeping king and tells him to arm his companions and advance onto the Ilian plains when Titan, the sun, rises from the waves. Agamemnon is addressed as “King of the Danai” and “Atrides”; the narrator also calls him the “Pelopeian hero.” Somnus then returns to heaven.

At daybreak Agamemnon summons the leaders to council. They pledge their united strength, and he thanks them for their courage. Thersites, described as the ugliest man at Troy and the most insolent in speech, argues against continuing the war and urges a return home. Vlixes, “renowned for his counsel,” rebukes him and strikes him with an ivory sceptre. The quarrel nearly leads to armed violence, until resourceful Nestor, whose wisdom comes from age, gently restores order. He recalls the omen at Aulis: a serpent devoured eight nestlings and then their mother. Invoking Calchas’s prophecy, Nestor declares that their appointed struggle will reach its fulfilment in the tenth year, when Ilion falls to Greek arms. The assembly approves his counsel and disperses. Agamemnon orders preparations, and at the next dawn commands his forces to arm and advance.

The poet invokes the Musae to recount the leaders, their ancestry, homelands, and ships, and asks Apollo to inspire and favour the undertaking. The Greek catalogue begins with the Boeotian leaders Peneleos, Leitus, Arcesilaus, Prothoenor, and Clonius. Agamemnon, born at Mycenae and chosen king by “warlike Greece,” brings the largest contingent, a hundred ships. Other leaders include fiery Menelaus; Agapenor, characterized by fierce fury; and trusty Nestor, keen-minded and powerful in counsel, accompanied by two sons. Schedius and Epistrophus are called strong and mighty, “the glory of the Myrmidons” and “two bulwarks of savage war.” Polypoetes and Leonteus follow, then Euryalus, Sthenelus, and Tydides, brave in battle; mighty Ascalaphus and valiant Ialmenus; Aiax, bravest of the Locri, later called “Oileos Aiax”; and the son of Euhaemon.

Achilles is “the bulwark of the Greeks,” sailing across his mother’s seas. The catalogue also names the Thessalian youths Phidippus and Antiphus, Teucer, Tlepolemus of Rhodes, and Eumelus. Other commanders are Prothous, a Magnes and son of Tenthredon; Elephenor of Euboea; Meges of Dulichium, distinguished in courage and arms; Thoas the Aetolian, son of Andraemon; and the resourceful “Ithacan,” referring to Vlixes. Aiax of Salamis, son of Telamon, is distinguished from the Locrian Aiax and praised for outstanding courage. Guneus, the Cretans Idomeneus and Meriones, Menestheus the Athenian, and the Elean leaders Amphimachus, Thalpius, Polyxenus, and Diores also appear. Protesilaus and brave Podarces are followed by the son of Poeas, and finally Podalirius and Machaon. The total Greek fleet is given as fourteen fewer than twelve hundred ships, or 1,186.

Once the Greeks have brought their ships ashore and occupied the plains, the divine father, now called “Saturn’s son,” sends Iris to Priam to announce that the brave Pelasgi are coming to battle. Hector, called “Priamides,” immediately arms at his father’s command and leads the Trojan young men through the open gates. His equipment receives a full description: a gold-gleaming helmet, breastplate, shield, spear, sword, and shining greaves.

Paris follows, fairer in appearance and now brave in arms, but explicitly called “the cause of the war” and “his country’s deadly ruin.” With him come Deiphobus, Helenus, brave Polites, and sacred Aeneas, “Venus’s undoubted son.” The Trojan and allied leaders include Antenor’s sons Archelochus and fierce Acamas; Pandarus, noble son of Lycaon; Glaucus, valiant in battle; Amphius, Adrastus, Asius, Pylaeus, Amphimachus, and Nastes; great-hearted Odius and mighty Epistrophus; fierce Euphemus and Pyraechmes, famed for his years; Mesthles, Antiphus, Hippothous, Acamas, and Pirous; Chromius and Ennomus, sons of Arsinoous and men in their prime; Phorcus and mighty Ascanius; Sarpedon, illustrious son of Jove; and Coroebus, born in a renowned land.

The Greeks are variously called Danai, Achiui, Argives, and Pelasgi, while Troy is also Ilion, Ilium, and Pergama. The book ends with Hector’s forces advancing and the Trojan catalogue complete, before any combat is narrated. Its final reflection is that “Neptune’s Troy,” defended by these leaders, would have overcome the deceit of the Danai had fate not prevented it.
