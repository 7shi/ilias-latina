### 113–119 (*Iliad* 2.3–9, 2.19, 2.10–13)

> 113 cum pater omnipotens Somnum uocat atque ita fatur:
> (when the almighty father calls Somnus and speaks thus:)
>
> 114 "Vade age per tenues auras, lenissime diuum,
> (“Go now through the thin air, gentlest of the gods,)
>
> 115 Argolicique ducis celeri pete castra uolatu
> (and seek the Argive leader’s camp in swift flight,)
>
> 116 dumque tuo premitur sopitus pondere dulci,
> (and while he lies asleep, pressed down by your sweet weight,)
>
> 117 haec illi mandata refer: cum crastina primum
> (give him these orders: as soon as tomorrow’s day)
>
> 118 extulerit Titana dies noctemque fugarit,
> (has raised Titan and driven night away,)
>
> 119 cogat in arma uiros incautumque occupet hostem."
> (let him call his men to arms and take the unwary enemy by surprise.”)

The stillness is broken by Jupiter, the “almighty father,” who sets the next stage of the action in motion while the Greek army sleeps. The Argive leader is Agamemnon, commander of the Greeks gathered outside Troy—the same king whose quarrel with Achilles has caused the greatest Greek warrior to withdraw from battle. Jupiter now sends him an apparently helpful command: attack at dawn, before the Trojans are prepared. “Titan” names the sun, imagined as a divine figure whose rising drives away the night.

Behind this military advice lies Jupiter’s promise to Thetis. Achilles’ absence must cost the Greeks dearly if they are to recognize how much they need him. Homer makes that purpose explicit here: Zeus considers how to honour Achilles and “destroy many of the Achaeans beside their ships” (*Iliad* 2.3–4). The Latin poet omits this deliberation and moves directly to the summons and command, leaving the earlier promise to explain why Jupiter is intervening. What sounds advantageous to Agamemnon therefore serves a purpose very different from his own.

The messenger also changes. Homer sends a destructive dream; the Latin gives the task to Somnus, the god of sleep. Calling him the “gentlest of the gods” and describing his “sweet weight” makes his presence seem soothing, even as he becomes the instrument of a dangerous deception. The Latin also draws forward Homer’s later picture of Agamemnon already asleep, so that Jupiter’s instructions themselves anticipate the messenger’s arrival at his bedside.

The message is shorter and more practical than Homer’s. In the *Iliad*, the dream is to order the whole Greek force into armour and claim that Troy can now be captured (*Iliad* 2.10–13). Here that sweeping prospect becomes a surprise attack on an unwary enemy. The added sunrise gives the order a clear deadline: the universal rest of the opening is about to give way to renewed fighting.
