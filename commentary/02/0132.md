### 132–135 (*Iliad* 2.53–56, 2.76–83)

> 132 in coetum proceres remque omnibus ordine pandit:
> (the chiefs into council and sets out the matter to them all in order:)
>
> 133 cuncti promittunt socias in proelia uires
> (all promise their united strength for battle)
>
> 134 hortanturque ducem. Quorum rex fortia dictis
> (and encourage their leader. The king, with words of praise)
>
> 135 pectora collaudans grates agit omnibus aequas.
> (for their brave hearts, gives equal thanks to all.)

The summons now brings together the Greek chiefs, rather than the whole army. Agamemnon tells them about the message received in his dream, and they unanimously pledge their support for battle. They also encourage Agamemnon himself: the king’s resolve is reinforced by the men he commands. His praise and even-handed thanks complete this picture of agreement between leader and council.

The Latin greatly shortens Homer’s council scene. In the *Iliad*, Agamemnon recounts his dream in a speech, and the elderly Nestor, king of Pylos, answers on the chiefs’ behalf. His support comes with a qualification: “If any other Achaean had told us this dream, we would call it false and turn away from it” (*Iliad* 2.80–81). Because the dream came to the army’s foremost king, however, Nestor urges the leaders to arm their men. The Latin replaces this individual response with a collective promise of military strength. It removes Nestor’s reservation and adds Agamemnon’s gracious acknowledgment, making the council appear wholly confident and united behind the proposed advance.
