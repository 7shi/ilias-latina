### 179–181 (*Iliad* 2.517, 2.524)

> 179 At Schedius uirtute potens et Epistrophus ingens,
> (But Schedius, strong in valour, and mighty Epistrophus,)
>
> 180 gloria Myrmidonum, saeui duo robora belli,
> (the glory of the Myrmidons, two bulwarks of savage war,)
>
> 181 longa quaterdenis pulsarunt aequora proris
> (beat the vast stretches of sea with forty prows.)

The catalogue continues with Schedius and Epistrophus, brothers who lead the men of Phocis, a region of central Greece near Delphi. After Nestor’s wisdom and experience, the emphasis returns to fighting strength: these two leaders are celebrated as sturdy supports of their army in the violence of battle. Their forty ships preserve Homer’s number: “With them followed forty black ships” (*Iliad* 2.524).

The title “glory of the Myrmidons,” however, confuses two distinct Greek contingents. Homer explicitly makes Schedius and Epistrophus commanders of the Phocians (*Iliad* 2.517); the Myrmidons are the warriors led by Achilles. The Latin thus retains the two leaders and their fleet while giving them the wrong people. It also replaces Homer’s fuller account of their family and the towns under their command with a brief, forceful tribute to their prowess, ending with the movement of their ships across the sea.
