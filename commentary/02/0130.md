### 130–131 (*Iliad* 2.48–53)

> 130 Interea lucem terris dedit ignea lampas.
> (Meanwhile the fiery lamp gave light to the earth.)
>
> 131 Conuocat attonitus iussis Pelopeius heros
> (Awestruck by the commands, the Pelopeian hero calls together)

Night gives way to morning, and Jupiter’s message begins to take effect. The “Pelopeian hero” is Agamemnon, commander of the Greek army and grandson of Pelops. Still awed by the divine commands delivered in his sleep, he moves to gather his men: the private encounter in the king’s chamber is about to shape the army’s public decisions.

The Latin moves directly from the messenger’s departure to daybreak, omitting Homer’s account of Agamemnon waking and dressing. Its “fiery lamp,” the rising sun, replaces Homer’s goddess Dawn, who climbs Olympus to announce the light to Jupiter and the other immortals (*Iliad* 2.48–49). The summons is likewise compressed. In Homer, Agamemnon orders heralds to call the Greeks to an assembly; the men gather quickly, but he first convenes a council of elders (*Iliad* 2.50–53). Here the emphasis falls instead on the king’s awestruck response and his immediate action.
