### 239 (*Iliad* 2.827, 2.876)

> 239 Pandarus et magnae Glaucus uirtutis in armis
> (Pandarus, and Glaucus, a man of great valor in battle.)

Pandarus’ name completes the preceding line’s introduction of the noble son of Lycaon. Homer adds that Apollo himself gave Pandarus his bow (*Iliad* 2.827), marking him out as an archer favored by the god; the Latin omits this distinction and moves straight to Glaucus.

Glaucus comes from Lycia, in southwestern Asia Minor, whose warriors have come to support Troy. Homer places him at the very end of the catalogue of Troy’s forces, sharing command of the Lycians with Sarpedon (*Iliad* 2.876). The Latin brings Glaucus forward and separates the two leaders, leaving Sarpedon until verse 249. Rather than retaining Homer’s general praise of Glaucus as “blameless,” it emphasizes his valor in battle, presenting another formidable warrior among Troy’s defenders.
