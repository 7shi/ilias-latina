### 247–248 (*Iliad* 2.862, 2.876)

> 247 florentes aetate uiri, quos Phorcus et ingens
> (men in the prime of life, followed by Phorcus and mighty)
>
> 248 Ascanius sequitur, simul et Iouis inclita proles
> (Ascanius, together with the illustrious son of Jupiter)

The description “men in the prime of life” completes the introduction of Chromius and Ennomus in the preceding verse. The catalogue then moves on to Phorcus and Ascanius, whom Homer names as commanders of the Phrygians, a people of inland Asia Minor. Here, as elsewhere in this shortened catalogue, the Latin preserves the leaders’ names but leaves their people unnamed. This Ascanius is not the young son of Aeneas, who bears the same name, but an adult warrior leading troops to Troy’s defence.

The “illustrious son of Jupiter” introduces Sarpedon, a son of the chief god, whom the Greeks call Zeus. Homer names Sarpedon and Glaucus together as leaders of the Lycians, from southwestern Asia Minor. Passing quickly from the Phrygian commanders to this Lycian prince, the Latin brings his divine parentage to the foreground: Troy’s allies include not only vigorous human warriors but a son of the ruler of the gods.
