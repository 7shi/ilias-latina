### 176–178 (*Iliad* 2.601–602)

> 176 quos iuxta fidus sollerti pectore Nestor
> (beside them trusty Nestor, keen of mind)
>
> 177 consilioque potens gemina cum prole suorum
> (and powerful in counsel, with his two sons,)
>
> 178 it ter tricenis munitus in arma carinis.
> (goes to war, backed by three times thirty ships.)

Nestor, the elderly king of Pylos in the western Peloponnese, joins the catalogue with ninety ships. An experienced warrior and a valued adviser among the Greek commanders, he brings a different kind of strength from the fierce fighting spirit just attributed to Agapenor: the Latin stresses his trustworthiness, intelligence, and persuasive counsel.

Homer names Nestor as the leader and gives him the same ninety ships (*Iliad* 2.601–602), but emphasizes his horsemanship rather than his wisdom. The Latin also adds the presence of his two sons, Antilochus and Thrasymedes. Both belong to the story of the Trojan expedition, though Homer does not mention them in this catalogue entry. Here their companionship presents Nestor not only as a seasoned counsellor but also as a father leading his family into war.
