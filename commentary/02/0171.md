### 171–173 (*Iliad* 2.569, 2.579–580, 2.576)

> 171 Inde Mycenaeis Agamemnon moenibus ortus,
> (Next Agamemnon, born within the walls of Mycenae,)
>
> 172 quem sibi bellatrix delegit Graecia regem,
> (whom warlike Greece chose as its king,)
>
> 173 centum egit plenas armato milite puppes;
> (brought a hundred ships full of armed soldiers;)

From the Boeoti, the catalogue moves directly to Agamemnon, passing over several contingents listed between them in Homer. Agamemnon is both the ruler of Mycenae, a powerful city in southern Greece, and the chief commander of the Greek forces gathered against Troy. His hundred ships belong to his own contingent, not to the expedition as a whole: they are twice the number brought by the Boeoti.

As in the preceding passage, the Latin replaces Homer’s detailed geography with a brief identification. Homer lists Mycenae among the places whose men Agamemnon commands; the Latin makes the city his birthplace and leaves out the other communities under his rule. It retains his hundred ships, the largest individual fleet in Homer’s catalogue, and emphasizes that they are filled with armed men.

The description of his authority also changes. Homer presents Agamemnon as conspicuous among the heroes because he ranks highest and leads by far the most men (*Iliad* 2.579–580). The Latin instead says that “warlike Greece” chose him as its king. This gives the many separate Greek peoples a shared voice and presents his supreme command as their common choice, rather than grounding his prominence chiefly in rank and military strength.
