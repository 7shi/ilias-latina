### 776–777 (*Iliad* 13.427–434)

> 776 Anchisae gener Alcathous, quem fuderat ense
> (Alcathous, son-in-law of Anchises, whom with his sword)
>
> 777 magnanimus ductor Rhytieus. Tunc feruidus hasta
> (the great-hearted leader Rhytieus had laid low. Then, fierce with his spear, …)

Alcathous is the warrior whose fall began at the close of the preceding verse. After Hector’s killing of Amphimachus, the narrative swings back to a Trojan loss. His killer, here called Rhytieus, is Idomeneus, the commander of the Cretan contingent fighting on the Greek side. The name evokes Rhytion, a town in Crete.

The Latin preserves one important family connection: Alcathous is the son-in-law of Anchises, the father of the Trojan hero Aeneas. Homer develops this connection into a brief portrait of Alcathous’s household. His wife is Hippodameia, Anchises’ eldest daughter, cherished by both her parents because she surpassed other young women in beauty, skill, and understanding. Her excellence explains her marriage to an outstanding Trojan warrior. The Latin reduces this affectionate account to a single phrase identifying Alcathous’s place within a prominent family.

Homer also makes divine intervention decisive: “Poseidon brought him low beneath Idomeneus” (*Iliad* 13.434). The sea god disables Alcathous, leaving him helpless before the fatal spear thrust. Here the god disappears, and Idomeneus receives direct credit for the kill, delivered with a sword rather than a spear. The result is another compressed battlefield success, with little pause before the next action. The unfinished phrase “Then, fierce with his spear” already carries the story onward to a warrior identified in the continuation.
