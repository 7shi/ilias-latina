### 775 (*Iliad* 13.183–187)

> 775 Amphimachum obtruncat nec non occumbit in armis
> (Amphimachus slays; and there falls too in arms)

Hector completes the answering Trojan success by killing Amphimachus, a Greek commander. Hector’s name and the description “fierce” stand in the preceding verse; the closing words already begin the account of another warrior’s death, whose identity is left to the continuation.

In Homer, Amphimachus is not Hector’s intended victim. Hector casts his shining spear at Teucer, a Greek warrior who sees it coming and narrowly dodges it. The spear instead strikes Amphimachus in the chest as he advances into battle. Homer names his father and grandfather, then makes his fall audible: “He fell with a thud, and his armour clattered upon him” (*Iliad* 13.185–187).

The Latin retains only Hector’s killing of Amphimachus. It omits the missed target, the family line, the wound, and the crash of armour, turning an accidental interception of Hector’s spear into a bare statement of victory. “Slays” does not specify how the fatal blow was delivered. The immediate movement toward another death keeps the emphasis on the succession of losses across the battlefield rather than on any one warrior’s final moments.
