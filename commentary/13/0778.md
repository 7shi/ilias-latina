### 778 (*Iliad* 13.516–520)

> 778 Deiphobus ferit Ascalaphum mergitque sub undas.
> (Deiphobus strikes Ascalaphus and plunges him beneath the waves.)

Deiphobus, a Trojan prince and brother of Hector, is the warrior introduced as “fiery with his spear” at the end of the preceding verse. His victim, Ascalaphus, is a leader fighting on the Greek side and a son of Mars, the god of war. After Idomeneus’s victory over Alcathous, the Latin immediately gives the Trojans a kill in return.

In Homer, the two deaths are separated by a longer struggle. Deiphobus aims his spear at Idomeneus as the Cretan commander withdraws, driven by a persistent resentment against him. He misses his intended target and strikes Ascalaphus instead. The Latin removes both the intended victim and the missed throw, making the blow read as a direct attack on the man it kills.

The ending also changes the scene. Homer’s spear passes through Ascalaphus’s shoulder, and he falls in the dust, clutching the earth in his hand (*Iliad* 13.519–520). The Latin instead plunges him beneath the waves. No water appears in the corresponding Homeric scene, and the Latin supplies no setting to explain it. This abrupt watery death replaces Homer’s image of the dying warrior grasping the ground.
