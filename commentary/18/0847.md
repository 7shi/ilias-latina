### 847–855 (*Iliad* 18.316–318, 18.323, 18.334–335)

> 847 et super exstincti prostratus membra sodalis
> (and stretched over the body of his dead companion,)
>
> 848 crudeles fundit questus atque oscula figit.
> (he pours out cruel laments and presses kisses upon him.)
>
> 849 Mox ubi depositi gemitus lacrimaeque quierunt:
> (Soon, when the groans for the dead man laid out there and the tears had ceased:)
>
> 850 "Non impune mei laetabere caede sodalis,
> (“Not unpunished will you rejoice in the slaughter of my companion,)
>
> 851 Hector" - ait - "magnoque meo, uiolente, dolori
> (Hector,” he says, “and to my great grief, reckless one,)
>
> 852 persolues poenas atque istis uictor in armis,
> (you will pay the penalty; and in that armor, a victor,)
>
> 853 in quibus exsultas, fuso moriere cruore."
> (in which you exult, you will die with your blood poured out.”)
>
> 854 Post haec accensus furiis decurrit ad aequor
> (After this, ablaze with fury, he runs down to the sea)
>
> 855 fortiaque arma Thetin supplex rogat: illa relictis
> (and, a suppliant, begs Thetis for strong armor: she, having left)

Achilles’ grief now turns outward, from the injuries he has inflicted on himself to the punishment he promises Hector. Yet the passage first returns to his tenderness toward Patroclus: he lies across his dead companion’s body and kisses him. In Homer, Achilles places his “man-slaying hands” on his companion’s chest and leads the lament, groaning like a lion whose cubs have been stolen (*Iliad* 18.316–322). The Latin leaves out that comparison and gives us instead the direct physical intimacy of the embrace and kisses.

The threat to Hector transforms Homer’s vow. In the *Iliad*, Achilles speaks to the dead Patroclus before his assembled followers, promising not to bury him until he has brought back “the armor and head of Hector, your great-hearted killer” (18.334–335). Here he addresses Hector as though his enemy were present. The promise of funeral offerings becomes a fierce prediction of Hector’s death. Calling him reckless challenges the confidence with which he enjoys his victory: his strength will not protect him from the consequences of killing Patroclus.

The armor makes the threat especially personal. Patroclus had entered battle wearing Achilles’ armor, and Hector stripped it from his body after killing him. Hector now exults in armor that belongs to the very man who threatens to kill him. Achilles, meanwhile, needs a replacement before he can return to battle.

That need carries the story swiftly to the shore and to Thetis, Achilles’ mother, a sea goddess. The Latin makes him run to her and beg for armor, turning his resolve into immediate action. Homer gives Thetis the initiative: she comes to comfort her grieving son and promises to obtain new armor for him (*Iliad* 18.65–69, 18.130–137). Here the movement from lament to threat to supplication tightly joins Achilles’ mourning to his coming return to war.
