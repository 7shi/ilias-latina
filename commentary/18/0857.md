### 857–858 (*Iliad* 18.468–470, 18.474–477)

> 857 Excitat Aetnaeos calidis fornacibus ignes
> (Stirs up Aetnaean fires in the hot furnaces)
>
> 858 Mulciber et ualidis fuluum domat ictibus aurum.
> (Mulciber, and with mighty blows tames the yellow gold.)

The divine smith begins work on Achilles’ new armor. Mulciber is another name for Vulcan, the god whom Thetis has come to ask for help. The heat of his furnaces and the force of his hammering turn her appeal into action. The Latin makes the work sound like a feat of strength: the god “tames” the gold, mastering the metal and giving it shape.

These two verses condense Homer’s fuller description of the workshop. In the *Iliad*, the smith turns toward his bellows and commands them to work; twenty bellows blow into the furnaces. He puts bronze, tin, gold and silver into the fire, then sets out his anvil and takes up hammer and tongs (*Iliad* 18.468–470, 18.474–477). The Latin keeps the fire and the vigorous craftsmanship, but singles out the gleaming gold and moves directly to the hammering.

The “Aetnaean fires” add a Roman coloring absent from Homer’s scene. Aetna, or Mount Etna, is the volcano in Sicily associated in Roman poetry with Vulcan’s forge. Homer places the god’s workshop on Olympus, not inside a volcano. Here the name evokes the fierce volcanic heat appropriate to a divine craftsman preparing armor for Achilles’ return to battle.
