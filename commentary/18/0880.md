### 880–884 (*Iliad* 18.593–594, 18.599–602, 18.569–571)

> 880 Parte alia castae resonant Paeana puellae
> (Elsewhere chaste maidens make the Paean resound)
>
> 881 dantque choros molles et tympana dextera pulsat;
> (and lead soft dances, and a right hand beats the tambourines;)
>
> 882 ille lyrae graciles extenso pollice chordas
> (he runs over the lyre’s slender strings with outstretched thumb)
>
> 883 percurrit septemque modos modulatur auenis:
> (and plays seven measures on the reeds:)
>
> 884 carmina componunt mundi resonantia motum.
> (they compose songs that echo the movement of the universe.)

From the calm administration of justice, the shield’s scenes turn to the shared pleasures of music and dancing. The maidens sing a paean—a song of praise or celebration, addressed above all to Apollo, who was himself called Paean—and their graceful movements are accompanied by percussion, strings and pipes. The player of the lyre and the reeds is only “he”: the verses do not say who he is. He may be Apollo himself, if Paean is taken as the god, or simply a musician set beside the hand that beats the tambourines. Alongside the community’s means of settling conflict, the shield displays its capacity for enjoyment and harmony.

The Latin brings together two separate scenes from Homer’s shield. In one, young men and women dance “holding one another’s hands at the wrist” (*Iliad* 18.593–594). Their skilled movements alternate between a swift circular dance, compared to a potter testing his wheel, and rows advancing toward one another (*Iliad* 18.599–602). The Latin keeps the graceful dancing but leaves out the young men, the changing formations and the potter’s-wheel comparison. Its player of the lyre recalls a different scene, the grape harvest, where a boy among the workers “played sweetly on a clear-toned lyre” and sang in a delicate voice (*Iliad* 18.569–571). Detached from the harvest, he becomes part of this combined musical performance. The tambourines and the seven measures played on the reeds are additions to these Homeric details.

The final verse gives the music a significance beyond human celebration. It evokes the ancient idea that the movements of the heavenly bodies form a cosmic harmony: the universe itself has a musical order, echoed here by the singers and players. Homer’s descriptions of the dancers and harvest musician make no such claim. The Latin thus closes this scene by linking the ordered pleasures of human life with the larger order of the heavens.
