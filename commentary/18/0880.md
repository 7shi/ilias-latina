### 880–884 (*Iliad* 18.593–594, 18.599–602, 18.569–571)

> 880 Parte alia castae resonant Paeana puellae
> (Elsewhere chaste maidens make a paean ring out)
>
> 881 dantque choros molles et tympana dextera pulsat;
> (and dance gracefully, while a right hand beats the tambourines;)
>
> 882 ille lyrae graciles extenso pollice chordas
> (a musician sweeps the lyre’s slender strings with outstretched thumb)
>
> 883 percurrit septemque modos modulatur auenis:
> (and plays seven modes on reed pipes:)
>
> 884 carmina componunt mundi resonantia motum.
> (they compose songs that echo the movement of the universe.)

From the calm administration of justice, the shield’s scenes turn to the shared pleasures of music and dancing. The maidens sing a paean—a song of praise or celebration, often associated with Apollo—and their graceful movements are accompanied by percussion, strings and pipes. Alongside the community’s means of settling conflict, the shield displays its capacity for enjoyment and harmony.

The Latin brings together two separate scenes from Homer’s shield. In one, young men and women dance “holding one another’s hands at the wrist” (*Iliad* 18.593–594). Their skilled movements alternate between a swift circular dance, compared to a potter testing his wheel, and rows advancing towards one another (*Iliad* 18.599–602). The Latin keeps the graceful dancing but leaves out the young men, the changing formations and the potter’s-wheel comparison. Its lyre-player recalls a different scene, the grape harvest, where a boy among the workers “played sweetly on a clear-toned lyre” and sang in a delicate voice (*Iliad* 18.569–571). Detached from the harvest, he becomes part of this combined musical performance. The tambourines and the seven modes played on reed pipes are additions to these Homeric details.

The final line gives the music a significance beyond human celebration. It evokes the ancient idea that the movements of the heavenly bodies form a cosmic harmony: the universe itself has a musical order, echoed here by the singers and players. Homer’s descriptions of the dancers and harvest musician make no such claim. The Latin thus closes this scene by linking the ordered pleasures of human life with the larger order of the heavens.
