Young Nestorides (Antilochus, son of Nestor) brings the pitiful body of Achilles’ companion Patroclus into camp with a grieving band of comrades. Achilles, called Pelides and Aeacides, turns pale at the news and loses the warmth from his bones. Weeping bitterly, he wraps his companion’s limbs in a robe from his mother, scratches his own face, fouls his well-groomed hair with dust, and tears the clothes from his strong chest. He stretches himself over the corpse, lamenting and kissing it. When his tears and groans subside, he vows that Hector will pay for killing his companion and will die in the very armour in which he now exults.

Ablaze with fury, Achilles runs to the sea and asks his mother Thetis for strong armour. She immediately seeks the help of Vulcan, also called Mulciber and Ignipotens. The god kindles Aetnaean fires and hammers yellow gold into armour of divine workmanship. Thetis carries it away, and mighty Achilles puts it on and turns his fierce gaze upon the shield.

The remainder describes the shield’s decoration: heaven, stars, earth, surrounding waters, and the ordered movements of the cosmos, including Arctos and Auster, Lucifer and Hesperus, and crescent Luna. Its seas contain mighty Nereus, aged Oceanus, shape-changing Proteus, wild Tritons, wave-loving Doris, and the Nereids. On land appear forests, monstrous beasts, rivers, mountains, and walled towns. Human scenes show an impartial judge settling a dispute, chaste maidens singing and dancing, a musician playing lyre and pipes, and agricultural work involving ploughing, reaping, and pressed grapes, alongside grazing flocks and cliff-clinging goats. The book ends within this description, with golden Mars standing at the shield’s centre, surrounded by seated divine Poetry and other figures, including mournful Clotho and snake-haired Lachesis. Achilles has received and donned his armour, but has not yet returned to battle.
