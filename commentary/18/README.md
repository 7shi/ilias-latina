Meanwhile young Nestorides, the son of Nestor, and the grieving youth of his people bear the pitiful body of Patroclus, Achilles’ companion, into the camp. When the dreadful news strikes the ears of Achilles, called Pelides and Aeacides, he turns pale and the warmth leaves his bones. Weeping bitterly, he wraps the limbs in a robe from his mother, tears his face with his nails, disfigures his well-groomed hair in the dust and rends the clothes from his strong chest. Stretched over the body, he pours out laments and kisses it. When the groans and tears have ceased, he vows that Hector, “reckless one,” will pay the penalty for killing his companion and will die, a victor, in the very armour in which he now exults.

Ablaze with fury, Achilles runs down to the sea and, a suppliant, begs his mother Thetis for strong armour. Leaving the waves, she at once begs for the help of Vulcan, also called Mulciber and Ignipotens. The god stirs up Aetnaean fires in the hot furnaces and tames the yellow gold with mighty blows, and soon the armour is finished with divine arts. Thetis flies off, and great Achilles puts it on and turns his fierce gaze to the shield.

The remainder describes the shield: the vault of heaven and the stars, the lands wreathed by the watery nymphs, Nereus set around, the cycles of the stars, the hours of night, the four quarters of the world (how far Arctos lies from Auster, and sunset from sunrise), Lucifer and Hesperus, both one, and the crescent Luna. In the seas are mighty Nereus, aged Oceanus, Proteus never the same, wild Tritons, wave-loving Doris and the Nereids. On land are forests, dreadful beasts, rivers, mountains and walled towns, where contending peoples practise the laws before a judge impartial to both. Elsewhere chaste maidens sing and dance to tambourines, while an unnamed player plays the lyre and seven measures on the reeds, songs echoing the movement of the universe; others plough, a reaper cuts the ripe grain, a vine-grower rejoices over the pressed grapes, flocks graze and goats hang on the cliff. The book ends within this description, with golden Mars standing in the middle of the shield and around him a goddess whose name the damaged verse does not preserve, and Clotho and Lachesis, mournful with snaky hair. Achilles has received and put on his armour, but has not yet returned to battle.
