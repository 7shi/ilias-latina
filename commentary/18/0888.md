### 888–891 (*Iliad* 18.587–589, 18.516–517)

> 888 tondent prata greges, pendent in rupe capellae.
> (flocks crop the meadows; little goats cling to the cliff.)
>
> 889 Haec inter mediis stabat Mars aureus armis,
> (Among these, in the middle of the shield, stood golden Mars,)
>
> 890 quem diua poesis reliquae* circaque sedebant
> (around whom the goddess … and the others were seated,)
>
> 891 anguineis maestae Clotho Lachesisque capillis.
> (Clotho and Lachesis, mournful with snaky hair.)

The picture of rural abundance continues from the grape harvest into the grazing grounds. Homer describes a great pasture for white sheep in a beautiful valley, together with shelters and sheepfolds (*Iliad* 18.587–589). The Latin reduces this settled landscape to its living inhabitants: flocks feeding in the meadows and goats poised on the rock above them.

The scene then turns sharply from peaceful labor to war and destiny. Mars, the Roman name for the war-god whom Homer calls Ares, stands in gold at the shield’s center. In Homer, he belongs to an earlier scene of a city at war: Ares and Pallas Athena, both golden, lead its warriors into action (*Iliad* 18.516–517). The Latin detaches Mars from that company and gives him a commanding position amid the shield’s varied scenes of human life.

Clotho and Lachesis are two of the Fates, the goddesses who determine the course and duration of human lives. They have no counterpart in Homer’s description of the shield. Their mournful appearance and snaky hair make them threatening companions for Mars; the snakes recall the terrifying appearance more commonly associated with the Furies, goddesses of vengeance. The verse before them is damaged: after “the goddess” stands a word that makes no sense, and who she was cannot be made out. Since “the others” seated around Mars are two of the three Fates, she may have been the third, Atropos, but this is only a conjecture. The closing image is nevertheless somber: the flourishing countryside now shares the shield with the powers of war and fate.
