### 841–844 (*Iliad* 18.22, 18.352–353)

> 841 Tunc ut Pelidae aures diuerberat horror,
> (Then, as the dreadful news strikes the ears of Pelides,)
>
> 842 palluit infelix iuuenis, calor ossa reliquit;
> (the unhappy young man grew pale; warmth left his bones;)
>
> 843 membra simul lacrimans materno innectit amictu,
> (at the same time, weeping, wraps the limbs in a robe from his mother,)
>
> 844 deflens Aeacides tristi de caede sodalis;
> (Aeacides, weeping bitterly over the sad slaughter of his companion;)

With Patroclus brought back to camp, the story turns to Achilles’ grief. Both “Pelides” and “Aeacides” name Achilles through his ancestry: he is the son of Peleus and grandson of Aeacus. The young warrior who has kept away from battle now confronts the death of his closest companion. The news strikes him with almost physical force, draining his face of color and his body of warmth.

Homer describes this first shock by saying that “a black cloud of grief covered him” (*Iliad* 18.22). The Latin poet replaces that enveloping darkness with the bodily signs of distress, then moves directly to mourning and the covering of the dead. Achilles seems here to wrap Patroclus’ limbs, though the Latin does not explicitly say whose limbs they are. On this reading, his tears accompany an act of care for the companion he could not save.

The robe received from Achilles’ mother, the sea-goddess Thetis, gives that act an intimate character. Homer’s account of the preparation of Patroclus’ body is different: his companions lay him on a bed and cover him from head to foot with fine linen, placing a white cloth over it (*Iliad* 18.352–353). No garment from Achilles’ mother is mentioned there. The retelling thus brings the first shock of loss and the later tending of the body together, concentrating both on Achilles and his personal sorrow.
