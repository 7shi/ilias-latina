### 864–875 (*Iliad* 18.607–608, 18.485–489, 18.484)

> 864 Oceani terras et cinctum Nerea circum
> (the lands of Oceanus, and Nereus set around,)
>
> 865 astrorumque uices dimensaque tempora noctis,
> (and the cycles of the stars and the measured hours of night,)
>
> 866 quattuor et mundi partes, quantum Arctos ab Austro
> (and the four quarters of the world: how far Arctos lay from Auster,)
>
> 867 et quantum occasus roseo distaret ab ortu,
> (and how far sunset lay from rosy sunrise;)
>
> 868 Lucifer unde suis, unde Hesperus unus uterque
> (whence Lucifer with his own, whence Hesperus, both one,)
>
> 869 exoreretur equis, et quantum in orbe mearet
> (horses rose, and how far along her circuit travelled)
>
> 870 Luna caua et nitida lustraret lampade caelum;
> (the crescent Luna, lighting heaven with her shining lamp;)
>
> 871 addideratque fretis sua numina: Nerea magnum
> (and he had added to the seas their own deities: mighty Nereus,)
>
> 872 Oceanumque senem nec eundem Protea semper,
> (and aged Oceanus, and Proteus, never the same,)
>
> 873 Tritonasque feros et amantem Dorida fluctus;
> (and wild Tritons, and Doris, who loves the waves;)
>
> 874 fecerat et liquidas mira Nereidas arte.
> (and with marvellous skill he had fashioned the watery Nereids.)
>
> 875 Terra gerit siluas horrendaque monstra ferarum
> (The earth bears forests and the dreadful monsters of wild beasts)

The opening completes the preceding passage: the lands on the shield are wreathed on every side by water, whether the watery nymphs belong to Oceanus or the lands are called his. In ancient Greek geography, Oceanus was imagined as a great river encircling the inhabited world, as well as the divine being who embodied it. Nereus, another sea god, here likewise appears to stand for the sea surrounding the land. The shield brings earth, water, and sky together within a single crafted surface. In Homer, Oceanus comes at the very end of the description, placed “along the outermost rim of the strongly fashioned shield” (*Iliad* 18.607–608). The Latin brings him forward into this opening survey of the universe.

The heavens display not only stars but order: the passage of night, the divisions of space, and the recurring movements of celestial bodies. Arctos, the Bear constellation, marks the north, while Auster, the south wind, marks the south; sunset and rosy dawn supply west and east. Homer names several constellations and lingers over the Bear, which turns in place and never bathes in Oceanus—that is, never sets below the horizon (*Iliad* 18.485–489). The Latin replaces much of that individual detail with a broader picture of the sky’s arrangements and cycles.

Lucifer and Hesperus are the morning and evening star, two names for the same heavenly body, the planet Venus. Their separate teams of horses give these two appearances the character of divine journeys across the sky. Luna is the Moon, imagined as a goddess carrying a bright lamp. Where Homer includes both “the tireless sun and the full moon” (*Iliad* 18.484), the Latin describes a crescent moon and leaves the sun unnamed. These choices make the heavenly scene a reshaping of Homer’s design rather than simply a shortened copy.

The sea then fills with divine inhabitants not listed in Homer’s corresponding description. Nereus is a venerable sea god, and Doris is his wife; the Nereids are their daughters, sea nymphs whose number includes Thetis, Achilles’ mother. Proteus, “never the same,” can change his shape, while the Tritons are wild, partly human sea creatures with fishlike lower bodies. Their presence turns the waters into a living realm, matching the ordered life of the heavens. Finally, “The earth bears” shifts the description into the present tense: the crafted landscape seems to stand before us, with its forests and formidable animals. The gaze is moving from the surrounding universe towards the life contained within it.
