### 862–863 (*Iliad* 18.483–485)

> 862 Illic Ignipotens mundi caelauerat arcem
> (There Ignipotens had engraved the vault of heaven,)
>
> 863 sideraque et liquidis redimitas undique nymphis
> (and the stars, and, encircled on every side by flowing waters, …)

Achilles’ gaze draws us into the world depicted on his shield. Ignipotens, “the master of fire,” is Vulcan, the divine smith who has just made the armour. The narrative now pauses over his craftsmanship: the shield is not merely protection for a warrior but a surface on which the wider universe can be represented. Its description begins with the heavens and their stars. The closing phrase, whose object belongs to the continuation, introduces something surrounded by water; “nymphs,” the female spirits associated with springs and streams, here stands poetically for the waters themselves.

In Homer, the shield’s decoration is described while the god is making it, before Thetis carries the armour to Achilles. The Latin postpones that description until Achilles looks at the finished shield, making his attention the occasion for ours. Homer begins with earth, heaven, and sea, then adds “the tireless sun and the full moon” and all the stars with which heaven is crowned (*Iliad* 18.483–485). The Latin moves more quickly through this cosmic opening, bringing the heavenly vault and the stars together. After the urgency of making and delivering the armour, the story opens briefly onto a scale far larger than Achilles and his approaching return to battle.
