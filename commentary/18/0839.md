### 839–840 (*Iliad* 18.2, 18.16, 18.231–233)

> 839 Interea iuuenis tristi cum pube suorum
> (Meanwhile the young man, with the grieving youth of his people,)
>
> 840 Nestorides in castra ferunt miserabile corpus.
> (Nestorides, bear the pitiful body into the camp.)

The body defended by Ajax at the close of the preceding book is now brought back to the Greek camp. It is the body of Patroclus, Achilles’ close companion, whose death will turn the story’s attention back to Achilles. “Nestorides” means “son of Nestor”: here it identifies Antilochus, a young Greek warrior whose father is one of the army’s oldest leaders. He and his sorrowing companions carry the dead man away from the battlefield. The contrast between Trojan rejoicing and Greek suffering now gives way to a scene wholly occupied by Greek grief.

These two verses bring together actions that Homer tells separately. In the *Iliad*, Antilochus goes to Achilles with news of Patroclus’ death; he does not carry the body. Menelaus and Meriones undertake that task, protected by other Greek warriors. Later, the Greeks draw Patroclus clear of the fighting and lay him on a bier, where his companions gather around him. The Latin retelling compresses the message and the recovery into a single homeward movement, giving Antilochus a role in bringing back the body itself. What remains central is the passage from desperate defence of the dead to mourning among friends.
