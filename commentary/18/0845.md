### 845–846 (*Iliad* 18.23–24, 18.27, 18.25)

> 845 unguibus ora secat comptosque in puluere crines
> (He gashes his face with his nails, and his well-groomed hair)
>
> 846 deformat, scindit firmo de pectore uestes
> (he fouls in the dust; he tears the clothes from his strong chest.)

Achilles’ grief for his dead companion Patroclus now turns against his own body. After the tears and the tender covering of the corpse, he scratches his face, spoils his carefully arranged hair, and tears his clothing. These are recognizable gestures of mourning in the ancient world: dust and torn garments make loss visible through the deliberate destruction of a person’s accustomed appearance. His strong chest, exposed by the tearing, recalls the warrior’s physical power even as he is overwhelmed by sorrow.

The Latin keeps the violence of Homer’s scene but reshapes its actions. In the *Iliad*, Achilles pours dark dust over his head and “disfigured his handsome face” (18.23–24); he also tears at his hair with his hands (18.27). Here the nails wound the face, while the dust disfigures the hair. The torn clothes are a further change: Homer describes ash settling on Achilles’ fragrant tunic, not his ripping it apart (*Iliad* 18.25). The retelling compresses these images into a rapid succession of gestures, making his grief intensely physical.
