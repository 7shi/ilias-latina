### 859–861 (*Iliad* 19.3, 19.12–13, 19.15–18)

> 859 Mox effecta refert diuinis artibus arma.
> (Soon he brings back the armour, finished with divine arts.)
>
> 860 Euolat inde Thetis; quae postquam magnus Achilles
> (Thetis flies off from there; after great Achilles)
>
> 861 induit, in clipeum uultus conuertit atroces.
> (has put it on, he turns his fierce gaze to the shield.)

The work is quickly completed, and the armour passes from Mulciber’s workshop to Achilles. Thetis, Achilles’ divine mother, flies off with what she has obtained for her son. He needs this new equipment because his earlier armour was lost when his companion Patroclus wore it into battle and was killed by Hector. The gift prepares Achilles to return to the fighting and seek revenge.

The Latin compresses the delivery and reception into a few swift movements. In Homer, Thetis reaches the ships carrying the god’s gifts and sets them before Achilles; the elaborate armour rings out as she lays it down. His companions are afraid even to look at it. Achilles reacts differently: the sight deepens his anger, his eyes blaze, and yet he delights in holding the splendid gifts (*Iliad* 19.12–18). The Latin omits the companions’ fear and Achilles’ pleasure, preserving the menace of his response in his “fierce gaze.”

It also brings the arming forward. At this point in Homer, Achilles looks at and handles the armour rather than putting it on; he will equip himself for battle later. Here he is already wearing the divine work when his attention settles on the shield. The rapid movement from the forge to the armed warrior makes the gift immediately effective, while the closing gesture singles out the shield for closer attention.
