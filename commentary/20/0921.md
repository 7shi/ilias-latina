### 921 (*Iliad* 21.330–333)

> 921 asseruit, rapidae quia cederet, ignibus, undae,
> (sustained, since he was yielding to the rushing wave, with fire,)

Juno now acts on the danger she has seen. Achilles, despite his strength, is losing ground to the river; the goddess sustains him by bringing fire to his aid. In Homer she does this through Vulcan, the Roman counterpart of Hephaestus, god of fire and metalworking, who sets the riverbanks and the plain ablaze. Against an opponent whom Achilles cannot overcome with weapons or bodily strength, divine fire supplies a different kind of defence.

Homer presents the intervention as a mother’s urgent command to her son. Hera—the Greek Juno—rouses Hephaestus to confront Xanthus, the river fighting Achilles: “Come to his aid at once, and make a great flame blaze” (*Iliad* 21.333). The Latin compresses that exchange into the statement that Juno sustained Achilles “with fire,” leaving the divine agent and his instructions unspoken. The struggle is thus turning from a mortal warrior’s resistance to the river into a contest between the river’s water and a god’s flame.
