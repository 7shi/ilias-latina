### 930 (*Iliad* 21.606–611)

> 930 confugiunt portasque obiecto robore firmant.
> (they flee for refuge and secure the gates with a timber bar.)

The Trojans take shelter inside Troy, completing the flight described in the preceding lines. Their last defence is now the city’s gates: instead of confronting Achilles in the open, they put a wooden barrier between themselves and their pursuer.

Homer dwells on the disorder of their escape. The city fills with fugitives, and no one dares remain outside the walls to wait for companions or discover who has survived and who has died. They pour inside, “all whose feet and knees had saved them” (*Iliad* 21.610–611). The Latin condenses this crowded, panicked retreat into a single action and adds the securing of the gates, which is not described in these Homeric lines. This gives the flight a decisive ending: the survivors are within the walls, but their safety depends on shutting the enemy out.
