### 922–923 (*Iliad* 21.385–391, 21.520–521)

> 922 sanctaque pugnarunt inter se numina diuum.
> (and the holy powers of the gods fought among themselves.)
>
> 923 Rursus agit Phrygias ingenti caede cateruas
> (Again drives the Phrygian companies with huge slaughter)

The struggle between divine fire and the river gives way to a wider conflict among the gods, whose loyalties are divided between the Greeks and the Trojans. The Latin gathers a long and sometimes comic sequence of confrontations into a single verse. In Homer, Mars, the god of war, attacks Minerva, the goddess of wisdom and warfare, who knocks him down with a stone. Juno humiliates Diana, the goddess of the hunt, by beating her with her own bow. Neptune, the sea god, challenges Apollo, but Apollo refuses to fight him for the sake of mortals. These divine quarrels echo the human war without sharing its deadly consequences: Homer’s Zeus watches from Olympus with delight as the gods clash (*Iliad* 21.385–390).

The narrative then returns abruptly to Achilles, named in the next verse as Aeacides, who “again drives” the enemy before him. Freed from the river’s threat, he resumes his devastating attack. The “Phrygian companies” are the Trojans: the Latin uses the name of a people of Asia Minor more broadly for Troy’s forces.

Homer likewise turns from the gods to Achilles, but first shows the immortals taking their seats beside their father in heaven. Below them, Achilles kills both Trojans and their horses (*Iliad* 21.520–521). The Latin omits that moment of divine repose and the detail of the horses, concentrating instead on the relentless forward movement of Achilles and the slaughter through which he drives his enemies back.
