### 928–929 (*Iliad* 21.528–529, 21.540–543)

> 928 Percussi dubitant trepida formidine Troes
> (Stricken with trembling fear, the Trojans waver)
>
> 929 atque intra muros exhausta paene salute
> (and within the walls, their safety almost spent,)

The Trojans’ resistance collapses before Achilles’ renewed assault. The contrast with the preceding passage is sharp: success gives their attacker fresh strength, while fear leaves them unable to stand firm. Troy’s walls offer refuge, but the Trojans have nearly perished, and their hope of safety is almost exhausted. The sentence runs on into the next verse, where their flight into the city is completed.

Homer likewise describes the Trojans being thrown into confusion, with no resistance possible (*Iliad* 21.528–529). His account then makes their retreat physically vivid: parched with thirst and covered in dust, they flee across the plain toward the city and its high walls. Achilles presses fiercely after them with his spear, still possessed by rage and eager for glory (*Iliad* 21.540–543). The Latin compresses this pursuit into the Trojans’ terror and desperate search for shelter. It omits their thirst and dust, while the preceding description of Achilles’ inexhaustible strength supplies the threatening presence behind their flight.
