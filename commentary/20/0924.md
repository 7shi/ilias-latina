### 924–927 (*Iliad* 21.520–525)

> 924 horridus Aeacides bellique ardore resumpto
> (the bristling Aeacides, and, his ardor for war renewed,)
>
> 925 funereas acies horrendaque proelia miscet.
> (throws the deadly lines into turmoil and stirs up dreadful battles.)
>
> 926 Non illum uis ulla mouet, non saeua fatigant
> (No force moves him, nor does his savage)
>
> 927 pectora bellando; uires successus adauget.
> (heart tire of fighting; success increases his strength.)

Aeacides is Achilles, named here as a descendant of his grandfather Aeacus. He is the warrior who, in the preceding verse, drives the Trojan companies before him. Now that he has escaped the river’s assault, his appetite for battle is renewed. The emphasis falls not on particular opponents or individual deaths, but on the terrifying momentum of his attack. Resistance cannot shake him, fighting cannot exhaust him, and success itself supplies fresh strength. This returns to an idea already heard in the poem: victory doubles a warrior’s strength.

Homer conveys the devastation through a different image. Achilles’ attack is compared to a city burning under the anger of the gods, its smoke rising into the sky and its destruction bringing hardship to everyone and grief to many. “So Achilles brought hardship and grief upon the Trojans” (*Iliad* 21.522–525). The Latin leaves out this comparison and turns attention from the suffering of the victims to the inexhaustible power of their attacker. Its repeated language of terror and death preserves the horror of the scene, while the claim that success strengthens Achilles makes his advance seem increasingly impossible to stop.
