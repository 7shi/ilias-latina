### 835 (*Iliad* 17.125)

> 835 traicit et uictor Vulcania detrahit arma.
> (he runs him through and, victorious, strips off Vulcan’s armour.)

Hector completes the killing stroke and takes the armour that Patroclus had borrowed from Achilles. The equipment that allowed Patroclus to appear as the Greeks’ greatest warrior now becomes the victor’s prize. Its loss compounds the disaster: Achilles has lost both his companion and the armour in which that companion went out to fight.

The Latin moves directly from the fatal blow to the stripping of the body. Homer separates these moments with Patroclus’ dying words and the opening struggle over his corpse; only later does he say that Hector “had stripped Patroclus of his glorious armour” (*Iliad* 17.125).

“Vulcan’s armour” identifies the equipment as the work of the divine smith, the Roman counterpart of Hephaestus. Homer does not explicitly attribute this particular suit to that god: it had passed to Achilles from his father, Peleus. The armour Homer describes Hephaestus making for Achilles comes later, to replace the equipment now lost to Hector.
