### 815–830 (*Iliad* 16.727–732, 16.787–789)

> 815 turbantemque acies respexit feruidus Hector,
> (fiery Hector saw him also throwing the battle lines into confusion,)
>
> 816 tollit atrox animos uastisque immanis in armis
> (fiercely he rouses his courage and, huge in his mighty armour,)
>
> 817 occurrit contra magnoque hunc increpat ore:
> (comes out to meet him and loudly challenges him:)
>
> 818 "Huc, age, huc conuerte gradum, fortissime Achilles:
> (“Here, come, turn your steps this way, bravest Achilles:)
>
> 819 iam nosces ultrix quid Troica dextera possit
> (now you will learn what an avenging Trojan right hand can do)
>
> 820 et quantum bello ualeat fortissimus Hector.
> (and how strong the valiant Hector is in battle.)
>
> 821 Nam licet ipse suis Mauors te protegat armis,
> (For even if Mauors himself protects you with his weapons,)
>
> 822 inuito tamen haec perimet te dextera Marte."
> (this right hand will kill you despite Mars’ will.”)
>
> 823 Ille silet spernitque minas animosaque dicta,
> (The other remains silent and scorns his threats and spirited words,)
>
> 824 ut quem mentitur uerus credatur Achilles.
> (so as to be taken for the true Achilles, whom he impersonates.)
>
> 825 Tunc prior intorquet collectis uiribus hastam
> (Then, gathering his strength, the first to hurl his spear is)
>
> 826 Dardanides, quam prolapsam celeri excipit ictu
> (Dardanides; as it comes, it is parried with a swift blow by)
>
> 827 Patroclus redditque uices et, mutua dona,
> (Patroclus, who repays him in kind with a gift in return,)
>
> 828 quod clipeo excussum uiridi tellure resedit.
> (which, knocked aside by the shield, lodges in the green earth.)
>
> 829 Tunc rigidos stringunt enses et comminus armis
> (Then they draw their hard swords and at close quarters)
>
> 830 inter se miscent, donec Troianus Apollo
> (clash in arms, until Trojan Apollo …)

Hector’s intervention turns Patroclus’ sweeping attack into a confrontation between two champions. The Trojan defender sees his side’s ranks falling into confusion and advances to stop the man responsible. He is also called Dardanides, “descendant of Dardanus,” after an ancestor of Troy’s royal family. The name places Hector within the long history of the people whose avenger he now claims to be.

The challenge depends on Patroclus’ borrowed identity. Wearing Achilles’ armour, he appears to be the Greeks’ greatest fighter, and Hector addresses him accordingly. Patroclus deliberately preserves the mistake by remaining silent. His silence is therefore more than contempt for an opponent’s threats: it sustains the terrifying appearance that has helped him drive the enemy back. Hector, meanwhile, boasts that he can overcome even divine protection. Mauors and Mars are names for the same god of war; Hector declares that his own right hand will prevail even against that god’s wishes.

This encounter substantially reshapes Homer’s narrative. Homer likewise has Hector leave the other Greeks alone and drive straight towards Patroclus, but does not give him this challenge to a supposed Achilles or stage this immediate spear-and-sword duel. Between Hector’s advance and Apollo’s decisive intervention, Homer includes Patroclus’ killing of Hector’s charioteer and a prolonged struggle over the body. The Latin removes those intervening episodes and brings the two leading opponents directly together. Their exchange of weapons becomes a bitter exchange of “gifts”: each tries to repay the other with injury. Neither opening cast settles the fight, and they close with swords.

The unfinished sentence then introduces a power beyond either warrior. “Trojan Apollo” means Apollo as Troy’s divine supporter, not a mortal Trojan. In Homer, he is already helping Hector and spreading confusion among the Greeks before he approaches Patroclus unnoticed in the fighting. Here his entrance abruptly interrupts the apparently balanced duel. Hector has just claimed that no god could prevent his victory; now the arrival of a god on his own side promises to determine what human weapons have not yet decided.
