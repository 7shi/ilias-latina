### 807–810 (*Iliad* 16.278–283, 16.294–296)

> 807 prouolat et falsa conterret imagine Troas.
> (He rushes forward and terrifies the Trojans with his deceptive appearance.)
>
> 808 Qui modo turbabant Danaos animoque fremebant,
> (Those who just now were throwing the Danaans into confusion and roaring defiantly,)
>
> 809 nunc trepidi fugiunt, fugientibus imminet ille
> (now flee in terror; he presses close upon the fugitives)
>
> 810 perturbatque ferox acies uastumque per agmen
> (and fiercely throws their ranks into confusion, and through the vast host…)

Achilles’ armour has the intended effect: the Trojans believe that the warrior advancing against them is Achilles himself. Patroclus’ appearance reverses the fortunes of the two armies. The Trojans, until now confidently driving back the Danaans—the Greeks—suddenly become the pursued. Patroclus gives them no respite, pressing forward as their ranks break apart. The unfinished movement through the enemy host carries the action into the next verse.

In Homer, the Trojans’ fear rests on more than recognition of the armour. They believe that Achilles has “cast aside his anger and chosen friendship” (*Iliad* 16.281–282): his quarrel with the Greek leadership must be over, and their most dangerous opponent must have returned to battle. The Latin compresses this mistaken conclusion into the phrase “deceptive appearance.” It also concentrates the action on Patroclus. Where Homer describes the Trojans fleeing with a tremendous uproar and the Greeks pouring forward among the ships, leaving a half-burnt vessel behind (*Iliad* 16.294–296), the Latin presents one fierce warrior driving the enemy before him. Achilles remains absent, but the sight of his armour already has the power to turn the battle.
