### 831–833 (*Iliad* 16.793–804, 16.818–819)

> 831 mentitos uultus simulati pandit Achillis
> (lays open the lying features of the counterfeit Achilles)
>
> 832 denudatque uirum, quem bello maximus Hector
> (and strips the man bare; him Hector, greatest in war,)
>
> 833 pugnantem falsis postquam deprendit in armis,
> (after he caught fighting in false armour,)

Apollo, the supporter of Troy introduced at the end of the preceding passage, now destroys Patroclus’ disguise. The “counterfeit Achilles” is Patroclus wearing his companion’s armour, not Achilles himself. The equipment is genuine, but its wearer has used it to claim another man’s identity. By stripping him, Apollo removes both his physical protection and the terrifying appearance that has helped him drive back the Trojans.

The Latin compresses Homer’s account of Patroclus’ disarming into a single swift revelation. In the *Iliad*, Apollo knocks the helmet from his head; his spear shatters, his shield falls, and the god loosens his breastplate. Homer dwells especially on Achilles’ helmet rolling beneath the horses and becoming stained with blood and dust. Here those separate losses become the decisive exposure of an imposture, completing the episode in which Hector challenges a supposed Achilles and Patroclus remains silent to preserve the mistake.

Hector’s discovery also changes the emphasis of the encounter. In Homer, he sees Patroclus already wounded by another Trojan, Euphorbus, and retreating. The Latin instead presents him catching Patroclus still fighting in borrowed armour: recognition, rather than retreat, defines the moment. Their apparently balanced duel has been broken by divine intervention. The sentence remains unfinished, leaving Hector’s response to the now-exposed warrior for the next passage.
