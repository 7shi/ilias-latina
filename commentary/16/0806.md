### 806 (*Iliad* 16.130–131)

> 806 Patroclus subitoque armis munitus Achillis
> (Patroclus, and, suddenly fortified with the armor of Achilles,)

Patroclus’ grief now turns into action. Achilles will not return to battle himself, but allows his companion to wear his armor and lead his men to the defense of the ships. The armor offers more than protection: the Trojans may mistake its wearer for Achilles and fall back, giving the hard-pressed Greeks some relief.

The Latin passes directly from Patroclus’ distress to his arming, leaving out the conversation in which he begs for help and Achilles agrees to send him. In Homer, Achilles also sets a limit: Patroclus must save the ships and then return, rather than pursue the Trojans toward their city. Homer introduces the arming with “Patroclus began putting on the gleaming bronze,” then describes him first fitting the greaves, the pieces protecting his lower legs (*Iliad* 16.130–131). The Latin condenses this deliberate preparation into a swift transformation, emphasizing whose armor Patroclus wears: Achilles remains behind, but his companion will enter battle in his place.
