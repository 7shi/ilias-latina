### 812–814 (*Iliad* 16.684–687, 16.731–732)

> 812 et nunc hos cursu nunc illos praeterit ardens
> (and now these, now those he passes in his course, burning,)
>
> 813 proeliaque horrendi sub imagine uersat Achillis.
> (and wages battle under the semblance of dread Achilles.)
>
> 814 Quem postquam socias miscentem caede cateruas
> (And when him, confounding the allied companies with slaughter)

After killing Sarpedon, Patroclus continues his headlong advance. He still wears the armor of Achilles, the Greeks’ greatest warrior, and so presents his terrifying appearance to the enemy. The “allied companies” are the forces fighting on Troy’s side, such as the Lycians whom Sarpedon led. The movement from one group of men to another makes Patroclus seem everywhere at once, spreading disorder wherever he attacks.

Yet this success carries him toward disaster. In Homer, Patroclus urges on his horses and his charioteer Automedon in pursuit of the Trojans and Lycians. The narrator immediately condemns his recklessness: “Had he kept the command of the son of Peleus, he would have escaped the evil doom of dark death” (*Iliad* 16.686–687). Achilles had instructed him to drive the enemy away from the Greek ships, not to press the pursuit toward Troy. The Latin leaves out that explicit warning here, sustaining the impression of an irresistible charge rather than interrupting it with a prediction of death.

The unfinished final sentence begins a turn toward the enemy’s response. At the corresponding point in Homer, Hector, Troy’s leading defender, leaves the other Greeks alone and drives his horses straight at Patroclus (*Iliad* 16.731–732). The sweeping attack is narrowing toward a decisive confrontation.
