### 812–814 (*Iliad* 16.684–687, 16.731–732)

> 812 et nunc hos cursu nunc illos praeterit ardens
> (and now he races past these men, now those, blazing with fury,)
>
> 813 proeliaque horrendi sub imagine uersat Achillis.
> (and wages battle in the guise of fearsome Achilles.)
>
> 814 Quem postquam socias miscentem caede cateruas
> (And when … saw him throwing the allied ranks into confusion with slaughter,)

After killing Sarpedon, Patroclus continues his headlong advance. He still wears the armour of Achilles, the Greeks’ greatest warrior, and so presents his terrifying appearance to the enemy. The “allied ranks” are the forces fighting on Troy’s side, including the Lycians whom Sarpedon led. The movement from one group of men to another makes Patroclus seem everywhere at once, spreading disorder wherever he attacks.

Yet this success carries him towards disaster. In Homer, Patroclus urges on his horses and his charioteer Automedon in pursuit of the Trojans and Lycians. The narrator immediately condemns his recklessness: “Had he kept the command of the son of Peleus, he would have escaped the evil doom of dark death” (*Iliad* 16.686–687). Achilles had instructed him to drive the enemy away from the Greek ships, not to press the pursuit towards Troy. The Latin leaves out that explicit warning here, sustaining the impression of an irresistible charge rather than interrupting it with a prediction of death.

The unfinished final sentence begins a turn towards the enemy’s response. At the corresponding point in Homer, Hector, Troy’s leading defender, leaves the other Greeks alone and drives his horses straight at Patroclus (*Iliad* 16.731–732). The sweeping attack is narrowing towards a decisive confrontation.
