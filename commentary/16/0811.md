### 811 (*Iliad* 16.477–482)

> 811 sternit et ingenti Sarpedona uulnere fundit
> (lays men low, and with a huge wound fells Sarpedon,)

Still driving through the Trojan ranks in Achilles’ armor, Patroclus brings down Sarpedon, a son of Zeus and the leader of the Lycians, who fight as allies of Troy. His death is a major victory: Patroclus has killed not merely another warrior, but one of the defenders’ foremost champions.

The Latin folds this encounter into the continuing sweep of Patroclus’ attack. Homer instead presents an exchange of spear-casts: Sarpedon misses, his spear passing over Patroclus’ left shoulder, and Patroclus answers with a fatal strike where the diaphragm surrounds the heart (*Iliad* 16.477–481). Homer then begins to compare Sarpedon’s fall to that of an oak or a poplar (*Iliad* 16.482), giving the death weight and dignity. Here, the single phrase “with a huge wound” replaces the precise account of the injury, while Sarpedon’s fall becomes the climax of a rapid sequence of destruction. The emphasis remains on Patroclus’ seemingly irresistible advance.
