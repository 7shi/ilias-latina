### 683–685 (*Iliad* 8.554–563, 8.543–547)

> 683 excubituque premunt muros flammisque coronant.
> (and beset the walls with sentries and encircle them with watch-fires.)
>
> 684 Cetera per campos sternunt sua corpora pubes
> (The rest of the warriors stretch themselves out across the fields)
>
> 685 indulgentque mero curas\<que> animosque resoluunt.
> (and indulge in wine, putting aside their cares and relaxing their spirits.)

The Trojans settle down for the night outside the Greek defences. Their fires do not burn the walls: they mark the watch maintained around the camp, keeping the Greeks hemmed in. While sentries remain on duty, the rest of the army reclines on the plain and drinks. The contrast continues the reversal of fortunes described just before: the Greeks are confined behind their rampart, while their enemies occupy the open ground and can afford some ease.

Homer closes this part of the battle with a much broader night scene. The Trojan fires shine between the ships and the river Xanthus like stars around a bright moon in a windless sky; a shepherd rejoices at the sight. A thousand fires burn on the plain, with fifty men beside each (*Iliad* 8.554–563). The Latin leaves out the comparison, the landscape, and the numbers, turning this expansive picture into a compact image of military pressure: a ring of watch-fires around the Greek walls.

It then returns to preparations that Homer describes earlier. There the Trojans unharness their sweating horses and bring cattle, sheep, wine, and bread from the city, gathering wood as well (*Iliad* 8.543–547). Here these practical arrangements become a brief scene of men lying down and drinking. The easing of their cares is the Latin’s own emphasis: for the moment, the army outside the walls enjoys a respite, though its watch ensures that the siege continues.
