### 679–681 (*Iliad* 8.343–345)

> 679 Se rursus Danai turbati caede suorum
> (Back again the Danaans, shaken by the slaughter of their own,)
>
> 680 conuertunt iterumque leues in castra cateruae
> (turn, and again the swift bands into the camp)
>
> 681 confugiunt portasque obiecto robore firmant.
> (flee, and secure the gates with an oak bolt thrown across.)

Hector’s assault sends the Danaans, as the Greeks are called here, back into the shelter of their fortified camp beside the ships. The slaughter of their companions has shaken them, and their renewed flight shows that they can no longer hold their ground outside the defences. The passage ends with a concrete act of protection: an oak bolt is thrown across the gates to bar the enemy’s way.

In Homer, the Greeks flee through the stakes and across the ditch, while many fall at Trojan hands; the survivors then halt beside their ships (*Iliad* 8.343–345). The Latin keeps the losses and the retreat but makes the slaughter the explicit cause of the Greeks’ alarm. It also passes over the obstacles they must cross, taking them directly into camp. The barring of the gates is an addition: where Homer ends with the fugitives coming to a standstill, the Latin closes on a barrier secured between the Greeks and their pursuers.
