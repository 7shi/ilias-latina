### 663–664 (*Iliad* 8.218–229)

> 663 Hortatur socios muris inclusus Atrides
> (Atrides, confined within the walls, urges on his comrades)
>
> 664 languentesque animos iuuenum in certamina firmat.
> (and steels the young men’s flagging spirits for battle.)

As Hector’s attack throws the Greek camp into confusion, Atrides tries to restore his men’s courage. The name means “son of Atreus” and here identifies Agamemnon, the commander of the Greek army. The walls are the defences around the Greeks’ ships and camp, not the walls of Troy. Behind them, Agamemnon must rally an army driven back upon its own stronghold.

The Latin reduces Homer’s elaborate scene to the commander’s effort to renew his men’s fighting spirit. In Homer, Hera puts it into Agamemnon’s mind to rouse the Greeks. He moves through the camp carrying a great purple cloak and takes his stand on the centrally placed ship of Odysseus, where his shout can reach both ends of the fleet. These details, including the goddess’s prompting, disappear from the abridgment. So does the sharpness of his opening rebuke: “Shame, Argives, objects of disgrace, admirable only in appearance! Where have your boasts gone, when we declared ourselves the best?” (*Iliad* 8.228–229). The Latin preserves the purpose of this appeal rather than its words: Agamemnon counters the spreading panic by strengthening his companions’ resolve.
