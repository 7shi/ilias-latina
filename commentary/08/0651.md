### 651–654 (*Iliad* 8.2–12, 8.41–51)

> 651 conuocat in coetum superos Iouis et monet, armis
> (Iouis calls the gods to an assembly and warns them)
>
> 652 ne contra sua dicta uelint contendere diui.
> (not to try to oppose his commands by force of arms.)
>
> 653 Ipse per aetherias caeli delabitur auras
> (He himself glides down through the upper air of heaven)
>
> 654 umbrosisque simul consedit montibus Idae.
> (and at once takes his seat on Ida’s shaded mountains.)

With daylight restored, authority over the coming battle passes firmly into the hands of Iouis, the Roman Jupiter, whom Homer calls Zeus. He is the ruler of the gods, but the other immortals have their own loyalties among the Greeks and Trojans. His warning is meant to prevent those loyalties from becoming armed intervention against his wishes.

The Latin reduces Homer’s divine assembly to a summons and a command. In the *Iliad*, Zeus explicitly forbids help to either army and threatens any disobedient god with a humiliating return to Olympus: “struck down, he will come back in disgrace” (8.12). Here the threat is left unspoken; the emphasis falls on the gods’ obligation to obey.

Iouis then leaves for Ida, the mountain range near Troy, where he can oversee the fighting. Homer describes his bronze-hoofed horses, their golden manes, and his golden clothing and whip before the journey through the sky. All this becomes a single effortless descent. The Latin also passes over his sanctuary and altar on Ida’s summit, bringing him directly to his seat among the shaded mountains.
