At dawn, when Titan reveals the world with his rays, Iouis summons the gods and forbids them to oppose his commands by force. He then descends to shaded Mount Ida to watch the battle. Holding up golden scales, he weighs the fates of the Trojans and Greeks; the doom of the Greeks outweighs their heavy arms, signalling their defeat.

Hector, called Priamides, “the sole glory of Phrygia,” and “the Trojan hero,” presses the attack in immense anger with all his forces. The Greeks fall into confusion, and their camp resounds with uproar. Atrides, Agamemnon, urges his comrades on from within the walls and restores the young men’s courage. Tydides, Diomedes, gleaming in blazing armour, charges through the enemy and kills Agelaus, who confronts him brandishing a huge weapon. Teucer, fighting under the protection of Aiax’s mighty armour, drives the Trojans back with arrows. He kills fierce Gorgythion and then the charioteer of “proud Hector.” Hector retaliates by striking Teucer with a stone, knocking him down and dislodging his bow. Teucer’s faithful comrades rescue and lift him from the battlefield.

Hector continues his furious assault, threatening the opposing ranks with his spear. Shaken by their losses, the Greeks retreat into their camp and bar its gates with an oak bolt. The Trojans surround them, posting sentries and watch-fires around the rampart. The book ends with the Greeks hemmed in and the remaining Trojan warriors reclining across the fields, drinking wine and relaxing their cares.

The Trojans are variously called Phryges, Phrygians, and the forces of Phrygia; the Greeks are called Achiui, Grai, and Danai, and their camp is described as Dorian. These names designate the same two opposing sides, not separate forces.
