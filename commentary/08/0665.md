### 665–670 (*Iliad* 8.253–260, 8.267–268)

> 665 Princeps Tydides fulgens ardentibus armis
> (Foremost Tydides, gleaming in blazing armour,)
>
> 666 per medios hostes immani pondere fertur.
> (charges through the midst of the enemy with tremendous force.)
>
> 667 Hic illi occurrit fatis Agelaus iniquis,
> (Here Agelaus, with fate against him, meets him,)
>
> 668 telum immane manu quatiens, quem maximus heros
> (brandishing a huge weapon in his hand; the mighty hero)
>
> 669 occupat et duro medium transuerberat ense.
> (strikes him first and pierces him through the middle with his hard sword.)
>
> 670 Hinc Phrygas Aiacis uastis protectus in armis
> (Then the Phrygians, sheltered in the vast arms of Ajax,)

Agamemnon’s rallying appeal is followed by renewed Greek resistance. Tydides is Diomedes, the son of Tydeus, who again takes the lead in battle. In Homer, no other Greek is quicker to drive his horses out across the defensive ditch and face the enemy. The Latin compresses this movement into a forceful charge: the horses and ditch disappear, leaving the blazing armour and the warrior’s overwhelming impact.

His victim, Agelaus, is a Trojan warrior, the son of Phradmon. The encounter changes substantially in the retelling. Homer’s Agelaus turns his horses to flee, and Diomedes drives a spear into his back between the shoulders and out through his chest; he falls from his chariot with his armour clattering around him (*Iliad* 8.257–260). Here Agelaus confronts Diomedes brandishing a formidable weapon, but Diomedes strikes before he can use it. A sword replaces the spear, and the killing becomes a direct encounter rather than an attack on a fleeing charioteer. “With fate against him” marks Agelaus as doomed even as he comes forward threateningly.

The unfinished sentence turns to Teucer, a Greek archer fighting under the protection of Ajax, his half-brother. The Phrygians here mean the Trojans. What the Latin calls Ajax’s “vast arms” is specifically his great shield in Homer: Ajax moves it aside to let Teucer shoot, then shelters him again. After Diomedes’ individual charge, the focus shifts to two warriors whose coordinated fighting helps sustain the Greek resistance.
