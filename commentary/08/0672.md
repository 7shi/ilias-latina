### 672 (*Iliad* 8.302–308)

> 672 Gorgythiona ferum letali uulnere fundit;
> (He strikes down fierce Gorgythion with a fatal wound.)

Teucer’s shower of arrows now claims a named victim: Gorgythion, one of the sons of Priam, the king of Troy. The Latin presents his death as a direct continuation of the archer’s success. In Homer, however, Teucer is aiming at Hector, the Trojans’ leading defender: his arrow misses its intended target and strikes Gorgythion in the chest (*Iliad* 8.302–303).

The Latin compresses the death into a single forceful line, leaving out the details that give Homer’s victim an identity beyond the battlefield. Homer recalls Gorgythion’s mother and her goddess-like beauty, then describes his dying movement: “As a poppy in a garden bows its head to one side, weighed down by its fruit and the spring rains, so his head drooped to one side beneath the weight of his helmet” (*Iliad* 8.306–308). That delicate image briefly slows the fighting and draws attention to the young man’s vulnerability. The Latin instead calls him “fierce” and keeps the emphasis on Teucer’s deadly effectiveness.
