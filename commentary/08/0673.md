### 673–674 (*Iliad* 8.312–315, 8.320–322)

> 673 mox alias acies petit aurigamque superbi
> (soon he attacks other ranks, and the charioteer of proud)
>
> 674 Hectoris obtruncat, quem saxo Troius heros
> (Hector he cuts down; him the Trojan hero with a stone)

After killing Gorgythion, Teucer continues his attack and strikes down Hector’s charioteer. In Homer, this man is named Archeptolemus: Teucer’s arrow hits him in the chest as he drives into battle. He falls from the chariot, his horses shy away, and “his life and strength were released there” (*Iliad* 8.312–315). The Latin leaves out his name and the movements of horses and dying man, reducing the episode to another swift demonstration of the archer’s deadly skill.

But the attack now brings Hector himself against Teucer. Hector is the “Trojan hero,” and the man he threatens with the stone is Teucer, not the fallen charioteer. Homer describes Hector leaping from his shining chariot with a terrible shout, taking a stone in his hand, and advancing straight towards the archer, intent on striking him (*Iliad* 8.320–322). The Latin compresses that furious response into a sentence left unfinished here: Teucer’s success has brought him into immediate danger.
