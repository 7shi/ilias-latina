### 677–678 (*Iliad* 8.335–342)

> 677 prostratumque leuant. Ruit undique turbidus Hector
> (And they lift him where he lies fallen. Hector charges furiously on every side)
>
> 678 aduersasque acies infesta cuspide terret.
> (and terrifies the opposing ranks with his threatening spear-point.)

As his companions lift the wounded Teucer, the Greek archer, the narrative turns straight back to Hector, the leading Trojan warrior. The rescue has saved one man, but it has not checked the Trojan advance. Hector now threatens the whole opposing line: the Greeks face the same spear that has just brought Teucer down.

Homer places this renewed assault within a larger movement of the battle. Zeus, the supreme god, again gives the Trojans strength, and they drive the Greeks toward the deep ditch protecting their camp, with Hector advancing at the front (*Iliad* 8.335–337). The Latin leaves out both the divine intervention and the movement toward the ditch, concentrating the attack in Hector alone. It also replaces an extended hunting comparison with the immediate threat of his spear. In Homer, Hector pursues like a dog snapping at a wild boar’s or lion’s hindquarters and watching for it to turn; he repeatedly kills the hindmost Greek as the others flee (*Iliad* 8.338–342). The Latin preserves his relentless aggression and the fear he inspires, but leaves the pursuit and its successive killings unstated.
