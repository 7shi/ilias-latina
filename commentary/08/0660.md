### 660–662 (*Iliad* 8.216, 8.213–215)

> 660 Priamides agit et totis grauis imminet armis
> (Priamides drives, and heavy bears down with all his forces,)
>
> 661 unum quippe decus Phrygiae. Turbantur Achiui
> (for he alone is the glory of Phrygia. The Achaeans fall into confusion,)
>
> 662 Doricaque ingenti complentur castra tumultu.
> (and the Dorian camp is filled with a mighty uproar.)

The sentence begun in the preceding passage now names the warrior driving the Greeks back. Priamides means “son of Priam,” the king of Troy: here it identifies Hector. He advances with the full weight of his forces, turning the defeat foretold by the scales into panic in the Greek camp. Achaeans and “Dorian” both designate the Greeks here; Phrygia stands for the Trojan homeland. Calling Hector its “sole glory” concentrates the hopes and honor of Troy upon its foremost defender.

Homer gives a more concrete picture of the pressure on the retreating army: the space between the ships and the defensive ditch is packed with horses and shield-bearing men, crowded together as Hector drives them back (*Iliad* 8.213–215). The Latin compresses this scene into confusion and uproar throughout the camp. It also shifts the emphasis of Hector’s prominence. Where Homer says that Zeus “gave him glory” (*Iliad* 8.216), the Latin praises him as the glory of his country. The preceding judgment of the scales still supplies the divine background, but the immediate focus is on Hector’s overwhelming assault and the fear it creates.
