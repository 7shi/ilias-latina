### 656–659 (*Iliad* 8.69–74, 8.215)

> 656 sustinet auratas aequato pondere lances
> (he holds up the golden scales, their pans evenly balanced,)
>
> 657 fataque dura Phrygum casusque expendit Achiuum
> (and weighs the harsh fate of the Phryges and the downfall of the Achiui,)
>
> 658 et Graium clades grauibus praeponderat armis.
> (and the doom of the Grai outweighs their heavy arms.)
>
> 659 Interea Danaos ingenti concitus ira
> (Meanwhile, roused by immense anger, against the Danai…)

The mighty hand of Iouis, introduced in the preceding verse, now holds the balance that reveals which army must suffer defeat. The Phryges are the Trojans, named here after a people of the region around Troy; Achiui, Grai, and Danai are different names for the Greeks, not separate armies. The scales begin level, but the Greek doom proves heavier. In this weighing, sinking means disaster rather than victory.

Homer makes the scene more explicit: the god places two portions of death in his golden scales, and the Greek side sinks toward the earth while the Trojan side rises toward heaven (*Iliad* 8.69–74). The Latin condenses that movement into the greater weight of Greek destruction. Its reference to heavy arms seems to contrast the soldiers’ military strength with a doom that their weapons cannot avert.

The unfinished final sentence turns from the god overlooking the battlefield to the warrior driving the Greeks back: Hector, the foremost Trojan defender. The Latin jumps over a substantial stretch of Homer’s fighting to a moment when the Greeks are being pressed toward their ships. Homer describes Hector there as a match for the swift war god; the Latin instead introduces him through his immense anger. The judgment shown in the scales is becoming a human reality on the battlefield.
