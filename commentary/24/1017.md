### 1017 (*Iliad* 22.405–406, 22.430–431)

> 1017 infelix Hecube saeuisque arat unguibus ora
> (unhappy Hecube, and she furrows her face with savage nails.)

The grieving woman is now named: **Hecube**, Hector’s mother, known in English as Hecuba. She is the wife of Priam, Troy’s king. Her name completes the sentence begun in the preceding verse, bringing the city’s shared mourning into focus through a mother’s anguish. “Furrows” makes her grief painfully physical: her nails tear across her skin as a plough cuts into the earth.

In Homer, Hecuba tears her hair and throws off her veil when she sees Hector’s body being dragged away (*Iliad* 22.405–406). Later she leads the Trojan women in lamentation, beginning, “My child, how wretched I am! Why should I live, after suffering so terribly?” (*Iliad* 22.430–431). The Latin brings these moments together, retaining her lament but replacing the gestures involving her hair and veil with the image of her scratching her face. Rather than letting her speak at length, it conveys the violence of her sorrow through what she does to herself.
