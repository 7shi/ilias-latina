### 1018–1021 (*Iliad* 22.466–470, 24.200–205)

> 1018 Andromacheque suas scindit de pectore uestes,
> (And Andromache tears the clothes from her breast,)
>
> 1019 heu tanto spoliata uiro. Ruit omnis in uno
> (alas, robbed of so great a husband. With one man falls)
>
> 1020 Hectore causa Phrygum, ruit hoc defensa senectus
> (the whole cause of the Phrygians—with Hector; down falls, sheltered by him,)
>
> 1021 afflicti miseranda patris, quem nec sua coniunx
> (the pitiable old age of his stricken father, whom not even his own wife …)

From Hector’s mother, Hecube, the mourning passes to his wife, **Andromache**. Her tearing of her clothes answers Hecube’s scratching of her face: both women turn their anguish into violent gestures. In Homer, Andromache’s response is more extended. Darkness covers her eyes, she falls backward and loses consciousness, and her headdress falls away, including the veil given to her by Aphrodite, the goddess of love (*Iliad* 22.466–470). The Latin replaces these details with a single familiar gesture of mourning, followed by the narrator’s cry over the husband she has lost.

Her loss is also Troy’s. The **Phrygians** here means the Trojans, whose survival depended on Hector, their foremost defender. The repeated “falls” makes his death seem to bring everyone down with him: first the whole Trojan cause, then the vulnerable old age of his father, **Priam**, Troy’s king. Hector had protected not only the city but also the father who must now face life without him. These declarations have no direct counterpart in the cited Homeric scene; they draw out the public and private consequences of his death.

The unfinished reference to Priam’s wife turns the narrative toward his attempt to recover Hector’s body from Achilles. His wife is Hecube, whom we have just seen mourning. In Homer she cries out against Priam’s intention to approach alone the man who has killed so many of his sons: “Your heart must be made of iron” (*Iliad* 24.200–205). The Latin thus moves swiftly from the family’s first grief toward the father’s dangerous resolve.
