### 1015–1016 (*Iliad* 22.405–411)

> 1015 Flent miseri amissum Phryges Hectora totaque maesto
> (The wretched Phryges weep for Hector, lost to them,)
>
> 1016 Troia sonat planctu; fundit miseranda querelas
> (and all Troia resounds with mournful lamentation; she pours out her laments pitifully.)

After the funeral honours for Patroclus and the games among the Greeks, the story turns back to the grief of their enemies. The **Phryges** are the Trojans, here named after the Phrygian people of Asia Minor; **Troia** is their city, Troy. They mourn Hector, their foremost defender, whom Achilles killed and whose body he still holds. His death is both a personal bereavement and a disaster for the community he protected.

The Latin draws here on an earlier moment in Homer’s story: the Trojans’ response when Achilles first drags Hector’s body away. Hector’s mother, Hecuba, tears her hair, casts away her veil and cries out at the sight of her son; his father, Priam, joins the lament, and their grief spreads throughout the city. Homer compares the uproar to what would be heard “if all lofty Ilios were burning from top to bottom” (*Iliad* 22.410–411). The Latin omits that image and the parents’ separate gestures, compressing the scene into a whole city resounding with mourning.

The closing words begin to move from this collective grief to a woman’s individual lament. Her identity is not supplied within these two verses, and the sentence continues beyond the excerpt; the emphasis here is on the pitiable manner of her crying.
