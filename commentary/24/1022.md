### 1022–1024 (*Iliad* 24.189–190)

> 1022 turbaque natorum nec magni gloria regni
> (and his throng of children, nor the glory of his great kingdom,)
>
> 1023 oblitum tenuit uitae, quin iret inermis
> (could hold him back, heedless of his life, from going unarmed)
>
> 1024 et solum inuicti castris se redderet hostis.
> (and placing himself alone in the camp of his undefeated enemy.)

The sentence continues from the preceding passage: neither his wife and surviving children nor the grandeur of his kingship can restrain **Priam**, Troy’s aged king. Determined to recover Hector’s body, he disregards his own safety. His “undefeated enemy” is **Achilles**, the Greek warrior who has killed Hector. Priam must approach his son’s killer not with the protection and authority of a king, but as an unarmed, vulnerable father.

The Latin replaces practical preparations with an account of the resolve behind them. In the corresponding Homeric lines, Priam orders his sons “to prepare a well-wheeled mule wagon and fasten a wicker body upon it” (*Iliad* 24.189–190). Here the wagon disappears, and the emphasis falls instead on everything that cannot keep Priam at home. Family, kingdom, and even the instinct to preserve his life yield to his duty toward his dead son. The contrast between the defenseless father and the undefeated warrior makes the intended encounter seem desperately unequal.
