### 1051–1070 (*Iliad* 24.787)

> 1051 Haec super ingenti gemitu componitur Hector:
> (Upon these things Hector is laid amid great groaning:)
>
> 1052 stant circum Iliades matres manibusque decoros
> (the Ilian mothers stand around, and with their hands their lovely)
>
> 1053 abrumpunt crines laniataque pectora plangunt:
> (hair loosen, and beat their torn breasts:)
>
> 1054 illo namque rogo natorum funera cernunt.
> (for in that pyre they see the funerals of their sons.)
>
> 1055 Tollitur et iuuenum magno cum murmure clamor
> (And amid a great murmuring the young men raise a cry)
>
> 1056 flebilis: ardebat flamma namque Ilion illa.
> (of grief: for Ilion was burning in that flame.)
>
> 1057 Inter quos gemitus laniato pectore coniunx
> (Amid these groans his wife, her breast torn,)
>
> 1058 prouolat Andromache mediosque immittere in ignes
> (Andromache, rushes forward, and into the midst of the flames to throw)
>
> 1059 se cupit Astyanacta tenens, quam iussa suarum
> (herself she longs, holding Astyanax; her, as ordered, of her own women)
>
> 1060 turba rapit. Contra tamen omnibus usque resistit,
> (the throng snatches away. Yet against them all she keeps resisting,)
>
> 1061 donec collapsae ceciderunt robora flammae
> (until the strength of the collapsing flame fell)
>
> 1062 inque leues abiit tantus dux ille fauillas.
> (and into weightless ashes that great leader passed.)
>
> 1063 Sed iam siste gradum finemque impone labori,
> (But now halt your steps and bring this labor to an end,)
>
> 1064 Calliope, uatisque tui moderare carinam,
> (Calliope, and steer your poet’s vessel,)
>
> 1065 Remis quem cernis stringentem litora paucis,
> (whom you see skirting the shores with just a few oars;)
>
> 1066 Iamque tenet portum metamque potentis Homeri.
> (and now he holds the harbor and the goal of mighty Homer.)
>
> 1067 Pieridum comitata cohors, summitte rudentes
> (Accompanying band of the Pierides, lower the sails,)
>
> 1068 Sanctaque uirgineos lauro redimita capillos
> (and, holy, your maiden hair crowned with laurel,)
>
> 1069 Ipsa tuas depone lyras. Ades, inclita Pallas,
> (yourself lay down your lyres. Be present, glorious Pallas,)
>
> 1070 Tuque faue cursu uatis iam, Phoebe, peracto.
> (and grant your favor, Phoebus, now that the poet’s course is finished.)

Hector is laid above the bodies, horses, and military equipment assembled on the pyre. The Latin takes its starting point from Homer’s brief statement that the Trojans “laid the dead man on the summit of the pyre and set fire to it” (*Iliad* 24.787), but expands that act into a scene of collective mourning. The Ilian mothers are the women of Troy, Ilion being another name for the city. Their loosened hair and wounded breasts make grief physically visible, while the young men’s cries join theirs. Hector’s death belongs not only to his household but to the whole community he defended.

The mothers see their own sons’ funerals in his burning. Their grief gathers past losses into the present ceremony and suggests the deaths still to come now that Troy has lost its strongest protector. “Ilion was burning in that flame” makes this identification explicit: Hector’s pyre becomes an image of the city’s destruction. Troy has not yet fallen in the story. The poem instead lets the burning of one body foreshadow the burning of the community whose safety depended upon him.

Andromache, Hector’s wife, gives this public catastrophe its most intimate expression. Holding their little son Astyanax, she tries to follow her husband into the fire and must be dragged away by her attendants. This is an addition to Homer’s funeral scene. In the *Iliad*, Andromache’s lament dwells on her widowhood, her child’s vulnerability, and the ruin threatening Troy; the Latin turns that despair into an immediate, dangerous action. With the child in her arms, her attempted leap threatens to extinguish Hector’s surviving family beside him. Her resistance lasts until the fire subsides and there is nothing left to follow. The contrast between the “great leader” and the “weightless ashes” measures how completely death has undone his strength. The description of the collapsing flame may also suggest the pyre’s heavy timbers falling as they burn.

Here the narrative ends. Homer continues with the extinguishing of the pyre, the gathering and burial of Hector’s bones, the raising of his mound, and a funeral feast. The Latin omits those acts of care and communal closure, leaving Hector reduced to ashes and Troy symbolically consumed with him. It then turns abruptly from the mourners to the poet and his divine helpers.

Calliope is the Muse especially associated with epic poetry; the Pierides are the Muses collectively, the goddesses who inspire song. The poet asks them to stop their music because his task is complete. The one crowned with laurel who is to lay down her own lyres may be Calliope herself or the whole band of the Muses; the Latin does not make it certain. His work becomes a small vessel, moving close to shore with only a few oars, and its completion becomes a safe arrival in harbor. Calling Homer “mighty” sets the modest scale of this Latin abridgement beside the greatness of its source. The Latin poet has reached the end of Hector’s story, and thus the same broad destination, by a much shorter voyage.

The final appeals are to Pallas, the goddess of wisdom and a powerful participant in the war, and Phoebus, Apollo, the god of poetry as well as a defender of the Trojans. The renewed address to Phoebus recalls the poet’s earlier invocation of him. These gods are now approached not as opposing powers on the battlefield but as patrons whose presence and favor can bless the finished poem. After the violence and grief of Hector’s burning, the last movement belongs to the poet bringing his vessel home.
