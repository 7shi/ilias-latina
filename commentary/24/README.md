The wretched Phryges, also called the Phrygians, mourn Hector, and all Troia resounds with lamentation. Unhappy Hecube claws her face, while Andromache tears the clothing from her breast, bereft of her great husband. Hector’s fall brings down the whole Phrygian cause and leaves his father’s pitiable old age without its protector. Neither his wife, his many children, nor the glory of his kingdom can restrain the stricken old king, Priam, also named Priamus: indifferent to his own safety, he goes alone and unarmed into the enemy camp.

The chieftains of the Danai and Achilles, called Aeacides, marvel at the wretched old man’s courage. Prostrate at Achilles’ knees, Priam addresses him as the bravest of the Graian people, the enemy of his kingdom, and the man whom the defeated Dardan youth fear. He begs Achilles to temper his cruelty with gentleness, pity a suppliant father, and accept gifts in exchange for his unhappy son’s body. If neither prayers nor gold can move Achilles, Priam asks to be killed himself, joining Hector in death. He urges the victor over Priam and the Dardan kingdom to remember the common human lot and the changing fortunes of commanders. Moved at last, Achilles raises the aged king and returns Hector’s lifeless body.

Priam brings Hector home and prepares the customary funeral ceremonies. A pyre is built with twelve Greek bodies, horses, chariots, trumpets, shields, helmets, and missiles; Hector is laid upon it amid lamentation. The Iliades mothers loosen their hair and beat their breasts, seeing their own sons’ funerals in his, while the young men cry out as though Ilion itself were burning. Andromache tries to throw herself into the flames holding Astyanacta, but her attendants drag her back. She struggles until the fire subsides and the great leader becomes ashes.

The narrative ends with Hector’s cremation. The poet then ends the work itself, asking Calliope to halt their labour and steer his vessel into harbour at the endpoint of mighty Homerus. He bids the accompanying Pierides lower the sails and the laurel-crowned goddess lay down her lyres, and invokes glorious Pallas and Phoebus for favour at the completion of his poetic course. This is the conclusion of the Ilias Latina, not a transition to another book.
