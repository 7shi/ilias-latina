### 1046 (*Iliad* 24.689–691)

> 1046 in patriam Priamus tristesque ex more suorum
> (Priamus to his homeland, and the mournful rites customary among his people…)

The sentence continues from the preceding passage: Priamus, Hector’s father and the king of Troy, carries his son’s body home. The closing words look ahead to the funeral rites. Recovering Hector has not ended his father’s sorrow, but it has made possible the proper burial that Achilles’ refusal to surrender the body had prevented.

In Homer, the return begins with another moment of danger and divine assistance. Priamus is still within the enemy camp. Roused by his divine guide, Hermes, the frightened old man wakes his herald; the god then harnesses their horses and mules and drives them swiftly through the camp without anyone noticing (*Iliad* 24.689–691). The Latin passes directly over these preparations and the secret departure. Its attention remains on the purpose of the journey: bringing Hector back to his own people, where mourning and customary funeral observances can replace the humiliation of his body at Achilles’ hands.
