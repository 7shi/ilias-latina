### 1047 (*Iliad* 24.777–783)

> 1047 apparat exsequias extremaque funera ducit.
> (he prepares the funeral rites and conducts the final ceremonies.)

Priamus, Hector’s father and the king of Troy, can now give his son the honours that had been impossible while Achilles held the body. The sentence begun in the preceding verse ends with this act of care: Hector has been brought home, and his father takes responsibility for the customary rites of mourning and burial.

In Homer, Priamus’ instructions turn the Trojans from lamentation to practical preparations. He orders them to bring timber into the city for Hector’s funeral pyre, assuring them that they need not fear a Greek ambush: Achilles has promised not to attack before the twelfth dawn. The people then harness oxen and mules to wagons and assemble outside the city (*Iliad* 24.777–783). The Latin compresses this communal activity into the king’s preparation and conduct of the funeral, passing over both the gathering of wood and the promise that makes it safe. Its closing words also look beyond these preparations to the ceremonies themselves. War has not ended, but Hector’s recovery has secured an interval in which his people can honour their dead.
