### 1048–1050 (*Iliad* 24.784–786, 23.175–176, 23.171–172)

> 1048 Tum pyra construitur, qua bis sex corpora Graium
> (Then a pyre is built, on which twelve bodies of Greeks)
>
> 1049 quadrupedesque adduntur equi currusque tubaeque
> (and four-footed horses are added, and chariots and trumpets,)
>
> 1050 et clipei galeaeque cauae argutaque tela.
> (and shields and hollow helmets and whistling missiles.)

The preparations ordered by Priam now take visible shape in Hector’s funeral pyre. Homer describes nine days spent gathering wood, followed on the tenth morning by the mourners carrying Hector out for burning. The Latin passes directly to the completed structure and the bodies, animals, and military equipment placed upon it. Horses and chariots, armor and weapons surround the dead defender of Troy with the objects of his life as a warrior.

The twelve Greek bodies, however, introduce a striking departure from Homer’s account of Hector’s funeral. They correspond to the twelve Trojan captives whom Achilles kills at the pyre of Patroclus, his beloved companion, whose death at Hector’s hands he has avenged. The horses likewise come from that earlier funeral, where Achilles throws four of them onto the flames. The Latin thus transfers offerings from Patroclus’ pyre to Hector’s, reversing the identity of the human dead: Trojans sacrificed by a Greek warrior become Greeks burned beside a Trojan. Here, however, the poem mentions only bodies; it does not say that Priam has prisoners killed or explain how these Greek dead were obtained. The chariots, trumpets, and accumulated arms further enlarge the ceremony beyond Homer’s account. What had been a funeral centered on the Trojans’ grief for Hector becomes a more imposing martial spectacle, incorporating the troubling human cost of another warrior’s burial.
