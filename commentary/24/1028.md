### 1028–1042 (*Iliad* 24.485–486, 24.503–504, 24.502)

> 1028 haec ait: "O Graiae gentis fortissime Achilles,
> (speaks thus: “O Achilles, bravest of the Greek race,)
>
> 1029 o regnis inimice meis, te Dardana solum
> (O enemy of my realms, you alone the Dardanian)
>
> 1030 uicta tremit pubes, te sensit nostra senectus
> (youth, conquered, trembles at; you our old age has felt)
>
> 1031 crudelem nimium. Nunc sis mitissimus oro
> (all too cruel. Now be most gentle, I pray,)
>
> 1032 et patris afflicti genibus miserere precantis
> (and pity a father cast down at your knees in supplication,)
>
> 1033 donaque quae porto miseri pro corpore nati
> (and the gifts I bring for the body of my unhappy son)
>
> 1034 accipias; si nec precibus nec flecteris auro,
> (accept; if neither prayers nor gold can move you,)
>
> 1035 in senis extremis tua dextera saeuiat annis:
> (let your right hand rage against an old man’s last years:)
>
> 1036 saltem saeua pater comitabor funera nati!
> (at least I, his father, shall join my son in his cruel death!)
>
> 1037 Nec uitam mihi nec magnos *concedere* honores,
> (Neither life to me nor great honors grant,)
>
> 1038 sed funus crudele meum! Miserere parentis
> (but my own cruel death! Pity a father,)
>
> 1039 et pater esse meo mitis de corpore disce.
> (and learn from my flesh to be a gentle father.)
>
> 1040 Hectoris interitu uicisti Dardana regna,
> (With Hector’s death you have conquered the Dardanian realms,)
>
> 1041 uicisti Priamum: sortis reminiscere uictor
> (you have conquered Priam: remember, as victor, the lot)
>
> 1042 humanae uariosque ducum tu respice casus."
> (of humankind, and consider the changing fortunes of commanders.”)

Still at Achilles’ knees, Priam asks for the return of Hector’s body so that his son can receive burial. The Dardanian youth are the young men of Troy, whose king is Priam and whose greatest defender was Hector. Priam begins by acknowledging Achilles’ overwhelming power. The young warriors of Troy fear him, and their aged king has suffered his cruelty. Yet that very contrast gives the plea its force: the man who has shown himself most terrible in battle is now asked to show himself most gentle.

The Latin substantially reshapes Priam’s speech in Homer. There the opening appeal is “Remember your own father, godlike Achilles” (*Iliad* 24.486). Priam asks Achilles to imagine Peleus, Achilles’ elderly father, and to recognize another father’s suffering through that bond. Later he says, “Respect the gods, Achilles, and pity me, remembering your father” (*Iliad* 24.503–504). The Latin retains the plea for pity and the ransom gifts, but, having already put the thought of Peleus into Hector’s dying words (986–987), replaces this direct appeal with praise of Achilles’ strength and an accusation of excessive cruelty. Priam approaches him not only as a grieving father but also as a defeated king addressing his conqueror.

The possibility of Priam’s own death then dominates the appeal. If neither pleading nor treasure can recover Hector’s body, Achilles may kill the father too. Priam is not bargaining for his own survival or for the honors appropriate to a king: without his son, he sees death as the last means of accompanying him. This extended alternative is an addition to Homer’s speech. It makes Priam’s helplessness especially stark, while also giving him the courage to speak without reserve. The appeal to learn gentleness from “my flesh” points to Hector, his child, as the poem elsewhere calls a son his parent’s flesh or body: the body Achilles holds should teach him what a son means to a father.

The closing words turn personal grief into a warning about victory. Troy has not yet fallen, but Priam speaks as though Hector’s death has already defeated both his kingdom and himself. Achilles therefore has nothing more to prove by withholding the body. Instead, he should remember that a conqueror remains human and that commanders’ fortunes can change. This explicit concluding admonition is the Latin poem’s addition, though shared mortality is central to the encounter in Homer too. Priam asks Achilles to look beyond the division between victor and vanquished and recognize the vulnerability common to them both.
