### 796–798 (*Iliad* 15.405–409, 15.415–416, 15.716–718)

> 796 atque inde aduersis propellunt uiribus hostem.
> (and from there drive back the enemy with opposing strength.)
>
> 797 Fit pugna ante rates, saeuit Mauortius Hector
> (Battle is joined before the ships; Mavortian Hector rages)
>
> 798 et poscit flammas totamque incendere classem
> (and calls for fire, and to burn the whole fleet)

The Greeks turn to resist at the ships where they have sought refuge. Their retreat has not become a complete collapse: they can still push back against the attackers. But the fighting now threatens the fleet itself, and Hector, the Trojans’ leading warrior, wants to destroy their means of escape. His epithet “Mavortian,” from Mavors, a name of Mars, the Roman god of war, presents him as a fighter of terrifying force.

The Latin compresses several widely separated moments in Homer into one swift movement from resistance to the threat of fire. In the first corresponding passage, Homer describes a stalemate: the Greeks cannot drive the Trojans away from the ships, although the attackers are fewer, and the Trojans cannot break through the Greek ranks (*Iliad* 15.405–409). The Latin gives the Greek resistance a more positive force, saying that they drive the enemy back, but immediately shows the battle continuing before the fleet.

Homer then sets Hector against Ajax, one of the Greeks’ strongest defenders, in a struggle over a single ship (*Iliad* 15.415–416). Here Ajax does not appear until Hector has called for fire, and Hector dominates the scene. Later in Homer, Hector grips a ship’s stern ornament and orders the Trojans to bring fire and raise a united battle cry (*Iliad* 15.716–718). The Latin omits that physical foothold and the collective shout, concentrating instead on his call for fire to burn the entire fleet. The danger is no longer merely that the Greeks will lose ground: they may lose their way home.
