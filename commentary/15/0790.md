### 790–791 (*Iliad* 15.306–307)

> 790 acrius insurgunt Troes ad Achaica bella
> (The Troes press on more fiercely in battle against the Achaeans.)
>
> 791 \<>
> (—)

The Troes are the Trojans, and the Achaeans are the Greeks besieging their city. The fighting now turns sharply in the Trojans’ favour. At the end of the preceding book, Hector, their foremost warrior, had been struck down by Aiax, and the Trojans were suffering heavy losses. Here they attack with renewed ferocity, but the Latin narrative leaves unexplained what has restored their strength.

In Homer’s fuller account, the reversal begins among the gods. Jove, the ruler of the gods, awakens and intervenes to give the Trojans the advantage. Neptune, the sea god who has been helping the Greeks, is made to withdraw, while Apollo revives Hector and gives him fresh strength for battle. Divine intervention thus reverses the gains the Greeks have just made.

The corresponding moment in Homer makes that divine support visible: “The Trojans advanced in a body, and Hector led them with long strides; before him went Phoebus Apollo” (*Iliad* 15.306–307). The Latin preserves the renewed Trojan assault but leaves out both the recovered Hector at its head and the god moving before him. What Homer presents as a coordinated advance under divine leadership becomes a brief announcement of fiercer fighting, carrying the story swiftly towards the next attack but obscuring the cause of the change.
