### 803–804 (*Iliad* 15.743–744, 16.109–111)

> 803 illinc ardentes taedas Phryges undique iactant;
> (From the other side the Phryges hurl blazing torches from every direction;)
>
> 804 per uastos sudor pugnantum defluit artus.
> (sweat streams down the mighty limbs of the fighters.)

The exchange continues across the battle line: the Greeks answer with spears, while the Phryges—the Trojans, here named after a people of Asia Minor—attack with fire. Their torches threaten the ships that offer the Greeks their only way home. Against Aiax’s defence at the stern, the attackers seem to press in from every direction.

The Latin turns Homer’s account of individual Trojans approaching the ships with fire at Hector’s urging into a collective volley of burning torches. It also broadens the picture of exhaustion. In Homer, it is Aiax who struggles for breath, sweat pouring from his limbs as he finds no respite from the assault (*Iliad* 16.109–111). Here that sweat belongs to the fighters generally. The focus widens from the solitary defender to the bodily strain of the whole struggle: even these powerfully built warriors are labouring under its intensity.
