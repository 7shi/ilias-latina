### 799–802 (*Iliad* 15.416–418, 15.674–677, 15.729–731, 15.742–746)

> 799 apparat. Huic ualidis obsistit uiribus Aiax,
> (he makes ready. Ajax opposes him with mighty strength,)
>
> 800 stans prima in puppi, clipeoque incendia saeua
> (standing at the very stern, and with his shield holds off the savage flames)
>
> 801 sustinet et solus defendit mille carinas.
> (and alone defends a thousand ships.)
>
> 802 Hinc iaciunt Danai robustae cuspidis hastas,
> (From here the Danaans hurl spears with sturdy points,)

Hector prepares to carry out his threat to burn the fleet, but now a defender stands against him: Ajax, one of the Greeks’ strongest fighters. With the ships behind him and the attackers bringing fire, his resistance protects the army’s means of returning home. The “thousand ships” gives the fleet an immense scale and makes its survival seem to depend on a single man.

The Latin gathers several moments of Homer’s battle into this compact picture. In Homer, Hector and Ajax struggle over one ship, neither able to dislodge the other. Ajax also strides across the ships’ decks wielding a long boarding spear, before being forced back to a raised bench, where he continues to repel the attackers. The Latin leaves out this movement and changing foothold, fixing him instead at the stern as the fleet’s solitary champion.

His weapon and the nature of his defence also change. Homer shows Ajax using his spear against Trojans carrying fire towards the ships; he wounds twelve men at close quarters. Here the shield takes prominence, and Ajax withstands the flames themselves. The separate attacks become one concentrated image of a warrior holding destruction at bay. Yet the last verse brings the other defenders back into view: the Danaans answer with a volley of their own, showing that Ajax’s extraordinary resistance remains part of a collective struggle.
