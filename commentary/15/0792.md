### 792–793 (*Iliad* 15.1–3)

> 792 pulsa metu uallumque et muros aggere saeptos
> (Driven back by fear, over the rampart and the walls protected by an embankment)
>
> 793 transiliunt, alii fossas uoluuntur in ipsas.
> (they leap; others tumble into the very ditches.)

The renewed Trojan assault gives way to a picture of panicked flight. In this sequence, the fugitives appear to be the Greeks, driven back towards the fortified camp that shelters their ships. Its defences become obstacles in their retreat: some manage to leap across, while others tumble into the ditches. The contrast makes the rout vivid without pausing over individual warriors.

The Latin here draws on an earlier moment in Homer’s narrative, before the divine intervention that restores the Trojans’ strength. At the opening of Book 15, it is the Trojans who have fled across the Greeks’ defensive ditch and stakes. Many have been killed by the Greeks, and the survivors stop beside their chariots (*Iliad* 15.1–3). That retreat belongs to the period when Hector is still disabled and his army is losing ground.

By placing this image of flight after the renewed Trojan advance, the Latin gives it a different apparent direction: the attackers now seem to be driving the Greeks back through their own defences. It also changes the emphasis. Homer mentions deaths in the pursuit and the survivors’ halt; the Latin concentrates on the obstacles themselves, adding the striking image of men tumbling into the ditches. The result is a compressed scene of confusion and fear, carrying the fighting towards the Greek ships.
