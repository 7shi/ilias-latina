### 756 (*Iliad* 11.369–370, 11.376–378)

> 756 nec Paris hostiles cessat prosternere turmas
> (Nor does Paris cease to lay low the enemy ranks.)

Paris, Hector’s brother, also presses the attack against the Greeks. Better known for carrying Helen away from her husband Menelaus—the act that brought the Greek expedition against Troy—he is also a dangerous archer. Here his activity reinforces the sense that the Trojans have gained the upper hand.

The Latin turns a single, sharply drawn encounter into a general picture of Paris striking down his enemies. In Homer, Paris aims his bow at Diomedes, one of the leading Greek warriors, and hits his right foot; the arrow passes through and lodges in the earth, and Paris laughs with delight (*Iliad* 11.369–370, 376–378). Neither the bow nor the wounded warrior appears in the Latin. Instead, Paris stands alongside Hector as another source of relentless pressure on the Greek ranks.
