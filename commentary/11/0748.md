### 748 (*Iliad* 11.101, 11.109)

> 748 Antiphon ingenti prostratum uulnere fundit
> (lays Antiphus low, struck down with a huge wound,)

The sentence begun in the preceding passage now reaches its violent conclusion: Agamemnon, the Greek commander, turns his seething anger against a named opponent. The crowded struggle between the armies gives way to an individual killing.

Antiphus is probably Homer’s Antiphus, a son of Priam, the king of Troy, though the identification is not certain. In Homer, Agamemnon attacks two of Priam’s sons, Isus and Antiphus, who share a chariot (*Iliad* 11.101). He strikes Antiphus beside the ear with his sword and knocks him from the chariot (*Iliad* 11.109). The Latin compresses this encounter into a single forceful line: it names only one victim and omits the chariot, the weapon, and the precise location of the wound. What remains is the direct expression of Agamemnon’s deadly power.
