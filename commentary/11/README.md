At daybreak the fighting resumes between the refreshed forces of the Dardanidae (the Trojans) and the Danai (the Greeks). Their leaders renew the struggle, and the dense battle lines clash amid flying missiles, ringing iron, sweat, and blood. Agamemnon, first called “the king of the Danai,” is roused by seething anger and strikes down Antiphon, Pisander, and Pisander’s brother Hippolochus as he rushes into battle. He then attacks Iphidamas with his sword. Iphidamas’s brother wounds Agamemnon in the right arm with a spear, but the pain makes Agamemnon fiercer: despite his wound, he pursues the fleeing attacker, identified as “the son of Antenor,” and exacts vengeance.

Hector then enters the battle in fierce anger. He is also called “Priamides,” marking him as a son of Priam, and drives the battered Greeks before him on every side. Paris likewise continues to strike down the enemy. The book ends with Paris drawing his bow and wounding Eurypylus in the thigh, leaving the Greeks under renewed Trojan pressure.
