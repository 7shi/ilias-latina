At daybreak the men return to their former wars, and the leaders of the Dardanians (the Trojans) and the Danaans (the Greeks) renew their spirits for the fight. Missiles fly from every side, iron rings against iron, the dense battle lines press on from both sides, and sweat flows mingled with blood. At last Agamemnon, called “the king of the Danaans,” roused by seething anger, lays low Antiphon, Pisander, and Pisander’s brother Hippolochus, rushing into war. He then attacks Iphidamas with his sword. Iphidamas’ brother strikes his right arm with a javelin, but the pain makes Agamemnon fiercer: wounded as he is, he pursues the fleeing attacker, “the son of Antenor,” and takes vengeance.

Hector then enters the battle, roused by fierce anger. Called “Priamides,” the son of Priam, he drives the stricken Greeks on every side. Paris too keeps laying low the enemy squadrons. The book ends with Paris drawing his bow and wounding Eurypylus in the thigh, leaving the Greeks under renewed Trojan pressure.
