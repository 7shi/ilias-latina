### 750–753 (*Iliad* 11.145–147, 11.221, 11.240, 11.248–261)

> 750 Hippolochum; post hos gladio petit Iphidamanta.
> (Hippolochus; after these he attacks Iphidamas with his sword.)
>
> 751 Hic frater dextram iaculo ferit; ille dolore
> (Here his brother strikes the right arm with a javelin; he, by the pain)
>
> 752 acrior accepto fugientem Antenore natum
> (received made fiercer, the fleeing son of Antenor)
>
> 753 persequitur traxitque ferox cum uulnere poenas.
> (pursues, and fiercely exacted vengeance, with his wound.)

Hippolochus completes the preceding sentence: he is the brother killed alongside Pisander. In Homer, he leaps from their chariot, only for Agamemnon to cut off his hands and head and send his body rolling through the crowd. The Latin passes over this mutilation and moves straight to another victim, Iphidamas, a son of the Trojan elder Antenor.

A second pair of brothers now confronts Agamemnon. The Latin barely pauses over Iphidamas’ death, which is implied rather than expressly narrated, before introducing his avenger. “His brother” is Coon, Antenor’s eldest son; he is also the unnamed “son of Antenor” whom Agamemnon pursues. Although Coon succeeds in wounding the Greek commander, the pain only makes Agamemnon more dangerous.

Homer gives this encounter a stronger emphasis on grief and loyalty. Overwhelmed by his brother’s death, Coon approaches unseen from the side and drives his spear through Agamemnon’s arm below the elbow. Agamemnon then attacks him while he is trying to drag Iphidamas’ body to safety and calling for help. He kills Coon and cuts off his head over his brother’s body (*Iliad* 11.248–261). The Latin replaces that attempted rescue with flight and omits the beheading. Its focus remains on Agamemnon’s relentless violence: even wounded, he pursues his attacker and takes revenge.
