### 757 (*Iliad* 11.581–584)

> 757 Eurypylique femur contento uulnerat arcu.
> (and with his bow drawn he wounds the thigh of Eurypylus.)

Paris now strikes Eurypylus, one of the Greek commanders. After the preceding verse’s broad picture of Paris laying low the enemy squadrons, this wound gives his attack a particular victim and brings his skill as an archer into focus.

In Homer, Paris catches Eurypylus stripping the armor from Apisaon, a Trojan warrior he has just killed. Paris draws his bow and shoots him in the right thigh; the arrow’s shaft breaks, leaving the wound to weigh painfully on his leg (*Iliad* 11.581–584). The Latin keeps the essential action—the drawn bow and the thigh wound—but omits the circumstances that expose Eurypylus to the shot and the broken shaft that makes the injury so vivid. The swift account sustains the pressure of the Trojan attack.
