### 743–747 (*Iliad* 11.15, 11.55–56, 11.67–71, 11.91–92)

> 743 Dardanidum Danaumque duces: uolat undique nubes
> (the leaders of the Dardanidae and the Danai. From every side flies a cloud)
>
> 744 telorum et ferro ferrum sonat, undique mixtis
> (of missiles, and iron rings against iron; on every side,)
>
> 745 inter se strident mucronibus: instat utrimque
> (blades clash as they meet: on both sides presses forward)
>
> 746 densa acies mixtusque fluit cum sanguine sudor.
> (the dense battle line, and sweat flows mingled with blood.)
>
> 747 Tandem feruenti Danaum rex concitus ira
> (At last the king of the Danai, roused by seething anger,)

The opening words complete the preceding sentence: it is the leaders of the two armies who renew their eagerness for battle. The Dardanidae are the Trojans, named after Dardanus, an ancestor of their royal house; the Danai are the Greeks. With both sides refreshed after the night, missiles fill the air and the armies close in hand-to-hand fighting. Neither side yet has the advantage.

Homer describes this encounter through a comparison with harvesting: opposing reapers work across a field of wheat or barley, and the cut stalks fall thickly. So the Trojans and Greeks strike one another down, neither thinking of flight (*Iliad* 11.67–71). The Latin leaves out the rural comparison but preserves the close-packed struggle and pressure from both sides. It gives the fighting a more immediate physical presence through the cloud of missiles, the repeated clash of iron, and the sweat mingling with blood. That last detail adds exhaustion and bodily effort to the picture of slaughter.

The final line begins a turn from the armies as a whole to one commander. The “king of the Danai” is Agamemnon, the chief leader of the Greek expedition against Troy. Homer likewise brings him forward from the general fighting as the first to spring into action (*Iliad* 11.91–92). Here his seething anger prepares the transition: the sentence remains unfinished, carrying the reader onward to what he will do.
