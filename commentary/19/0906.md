### 906–907 (*Iliad* 21.1–8)

> 906 confugit ad Xanthi rapidos perterrita fluctus
> (in terror flees for refuge to the rushing waters of Xanthus)
>
> 907 auxiliumque petit diuini fluminis; ille
> (and seeks the divine river’s aid; he)

The “Dardanian youth” of the preceding verse are the Trojans, now driven by Achilles to seek refuge at the river Xanthus, which flows across the plain near Troy. Their terror answers his thirst for Hector’s blood: the story turns from the warrior pursuing revenge to the men desperate to escape him. The river offers more than a physical refuge. As “the divine river,” it is also a power to whom they can appeal for protection.

Homer gives a fuller picture of the army’s retreat. At the river crossing, Achilles divides the fugitives: some he pursues across the plain towards Troy, while the others are hemmed into the deep river. The route towards the city is the same ground over which the Greeks fled the previous day when Hector dominated the fighting (*Iliad* 21.3–5). Now the fortunes of battle have reversed. Hera, a goddess supporting the Greeks, spreads a thick mist before the fleeing Trojans to hinder them (*Iliad* 21.6–7).

The Latin omits this divided retreat, Hera’s intervention, and the reminder of Hector’s earlier success. Instead, it concentrates the Trojans’ fear into a single movement towards the water. Homer already establishes the river’s divine nature by calling Xanthus a son of Zeus (*Iliad* 21.1–2); the Latin makes that divinity immediately important to the fugitives by adding their appeal for aid. They do not merely enter the river: they seek its protection against Achilles.
