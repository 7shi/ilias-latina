Equipped with his gifts, Achilles, called “the hero, son of Thetis,” and “Aeacides,” is borne into battle in a huge whirlwind. Juno and “chaste Pallas” give him strength and courage. Aeneas, “the Cytherean hero,” the son of Venus, goes to meet him, though he is no match for Aeacides; anger drives the young man to pit his weapons against invincible strength. Neptune, “the ruler of the mighty waters,” saves him, so that as an exile he might restore Troy in fertile fields and bring forth the Augustan line; otherwise “the founder of an illustrious race” would not have survived “for us,” the Romans.

Achilles then drives the Trojans, called “Teucrians” and “the Dardanian youth,” before his spear, laying low a huge number as he thirsts for Hector’s blood. In terror they flee to the rushing Xanthus and seek the divine river’s aid, but Achilles presses on and the battle is fought in its waters, his anger giving him strength. The book ends amid this river slaughter: the banks are streaked with blood, and scattered bodies roll through the waves. Hector remains Achilles’ intended target, but their encounter has not yet occurred.
