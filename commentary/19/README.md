Equipped with his gifts, Achilles, called “the hero born of Thetis” and “Aeacides,” rushes into battle like a whirlwind. Iuno and “chaste Pallas” strengthen him and give him courage. Aeneas, designated “the Cytherean hero” through his connection with Venus, confronts Achilles. Though no match for his opponent’s invincible strength, anger drives him to fight. Neptune, “the ruler of the mighty waters,” saves Aeneas, preserving him for his destiny: to rebuild Troy in fertile lands as an exile and establish the Augustan line. The narrator thus identifies him as “the founder of an illustrious race,” whose survival matters to the Romans.

Achilles then routs the Trojans, called “Teucrians” and “Dardanian young warriors,” slaughtering many as he thirsts for Hector’s blood. Terrified, they flee to the rushing Xanthus and appeal to the divine river for help. Achilles pursues them into its swirling waters and continues fighting, strengthened by anger. The book ends amid this river slaughter: the banks are streaked with blood, and bodies tumble everywhere through the waves. Hector remains Achilles’ intended target, but their encounter has not yet occurred.
