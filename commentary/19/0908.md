### 908–910 (*Iliad* 21.15–21)

> 908 instat et in mediis pugnatur gurgitis undis.
> (presses on, and battle is joined amid the waters of the eddy.)
>
> 909 Ira dabat uires; stringuntur sanguine ripae
> (Anger gave him strength; the banks are streaked with blood)
>
> 910 sparsaque per totos uoluuntur corpora fluctus.
> (and scattered bodies tumble through the waves everywhere.)

The pursuer is Achilles: the Trojans’ appeal to the divine river has not stopped him from carrying the slaughter into its waters. What they sought as a refuge becomes another battlefield. His anger, fuelled by the death of his companion Patroclus at Hector’s hands, gives him strength against the fugitives. The brief statement that “battle is joined” quickly yields to a picture of its consequences: blood along the banks and bodies rolling in the current.

Homer describes Achilles’ entry into the river more closely. He leaves his spear on the bank, leaning against tamarisk bushes, and leaps in with only his sword, “like a god,” intent on destruction (*Iliad* 21.17–19). He strikes in every direction; the victims groan, and “the water grew red with blood” (*Iliad* 21.20–21).

The Latin leaves out these movements and sounds, compressing the attack into three verses. In place of Homer’s description of Achilles’ destructive purpose, it explicitly names anger as the source of his strength. It also expands the final image: blood marks not only the scene of fighting in the water but the riverbanks, while scattered bodies fill the waves. The current that seemed to promise escape now carries the dead.
