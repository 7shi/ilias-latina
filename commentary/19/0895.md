### 895–898 (*Iliad* 20.79–85, 20.158–164)

> 895 dantque animos iuueni. Vidit Cythereius heros
> (and give the young man courage. The Cytherean hero saw him)
>
> 896 occurritque uiro, sed non cum uiribus aequis
> (and went to meet the warrior, but not with equal strength.)
>
> 897 Aeacidae nec compar erat, tamen ira coegit
> (Nor was he a match for Aeacides; yet anger drove)
>
> 898 conferre inuictis iuuenem cum uiribus arma.
> (the young man to pit his weapons against invincible strength.)

Iuno and Pallas continue their support of Achilles, giving him courage as well as strength. He now faces Aeneas, a leading Trojan warrior and the son of the goddess Venus. “Cytherean” identifies Aeneas through his mother, whose worship was associated with the island of Cythera. “Aeacides,” by contrast, means a descendant of Aeacus: here it names Achilles, Aeacus’ grandson.

In Homer, Aeneas also receives divine encouragement. Apollo, disguised as a son of the Trojan king Priam, challenges him to make good on his boasts that he would fight Achilles (*Iliad* 20.79–85). The Latin leaves out this intervention. Coming immediately after the goddesses’ aid to Achilles, Aeneas’ advance therefore looks like a mortal act of anger against a divinely strengthened opponent. Anger supplies the resolve that strength alone cannot justify.

The Latin also makes the inequality of the encounter explicit. Homer introduces the pair as “two men, by far the best,” advancing eagerly into the space between the armies (*Iliad* 20.158–160). Aeneas comes forward threateningly, his heavy helmet nodding, his shield before his chest and his spear shaking in his hand; Achilles approaches like a lion (*Iliad* 20.161–164). Those visible preparations disappear here. Instead, the repeated contrast between unequal and invincible strength makes Aeneas’ challenge seem desperate before the weapons have even met.
