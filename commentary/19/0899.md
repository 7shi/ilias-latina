### 899–902 (*Iliad* 20.291–293, 20.318–325, 20.302–308)

> 899 Quem nisi seruasset magnarum rector aquarum,
> (Had not the ruler of the mighty waters saved him,)
>
> 900 ut profugus laetis Troiam repararet in aruis
> (so that, an exile, he might restore Troy in fertile fields)
>
> 901 Augustumque genus claris submitteret astris,
> (and bring forth the Augustan line beneath the bright stars,)
>
> 902 non clarae gentis nobis mansisset origo.
> (the founder of an illustrious race would not have survived for us.)

Aeneas cannot overcome Achilles, but his death would destroy a future that reaches far beyond this battle. His rescuer is Neptune, god of the sea, called here “the ruler of the mighty waters.” The Latin passes directly from the unequal encounter to its larger significance, leaving out the action of the rescue. In Homer, Poseidon—the Greek name for Neptune—covers Achilles’ eyes with mist, pulls Achilles’ spear from Aeneas’ shield, and lifts Aeneas away from the battlefield (*Iliad* 20.318–325).

The reason for preserving Aeneas also changes. Homer’s sea god declares that Aeneas must escape so that the descendants of Dardanus, an early ancestor of the Trojans, do not disappear. Zeus has turned against the family of Priam, Troy’s reigning king; another branch of the Trojan royal house must survive. “Now mighty Aeneas will rule over the Trojans, and his children’s children who are born hereafter” (*Iliad* 20.307–308). The Latin keeps this concern for the survival of a dynasty, but directs it towards Rome rather than towards continued rule over the Trojans.

The “fertile fields” are those of Italy, where Roman tradition placed Aeneas’ new homeland after his flight from Troy. Restoring Troy means establishing a new home for its surviving people and traditions, not restoring the ruined city itself. Through his son Iulus, Aeneas was claimed as the ancestor of the Julian family, the family of Julius Caesar and, by adoption, Augustus. The “Augustan line” brings that imperial future into the Trojan story. Bringing it forth “beneath the bright stars” can describe its birth into the world, while also suggesting the glory and heavenly associations of the ruling family. Above all, “for us” briefly reveals the narrator’s Roman perspective: Neptune saves not merely a Trojan warrior, but the ancestral source of Rome’s illustrious dynasty.
