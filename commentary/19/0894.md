### 894 (*Iliad* 19.349–354)

> 894 cui uires praebet casta cum Pallade Iuno
> (to whom Iuno, together with chaste Pallas, gives strength.)

As Achilles rushes back into battle, divine support strengthens him as well as equipping him with armour. Iuno is the Roman counterpart of Hera, wife of Zeus; Pallas is Athena, goddess of wisdom and warfare, whose unmarried status is recalled by “chaste.” Both goddesses favour the Greeks against the Trojans.

In Homer, this help comes before Achilles puts on his armour. Grieving for Patroclus, he has refused food, and Zeus sends Athena to sustain him. She descends from heaven like a long-winged bird and pours nectar and ambrosia, the food and drink of the gods, into his chest, “so that painful hunger might not reach his knees” (*Iliad* 19.349–354). The Latin condenses this act of nourishment into a general gift of strength, omitting both Achilles’ fasting and the means by which he is sustained. It also gives Iuno a share in helping him, while leaving out Zeus’s command. Placed after his headlong return to battle, the divine aid now reinforces the impression of an irresistible warrior.
