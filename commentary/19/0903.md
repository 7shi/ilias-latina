### 903–905 (*Iliad* 20.381–383, 20.490–499)

> 903 Inde agit Aeacides infesta cuspide Teucros
> (Then Aeacides drives the Teucrians before his threatening spear,)
>
> 904 ingentemque modum prosternit caede uirorum,
> (and lays low a vast number of men in slaughter,)
>
> 905 sanguinis Hectorei sitiens. At Dardana pubes
> (thirsting for Hector’s blood. But the Dardanian young warriors)

With Aeneas rescued, the story returns from Rome’s distant future to the immediate violence of battle. Aeacides is Achilles, called here by a name identifying him as a descendant of his grandfather Aeacus. The Teucrians are the Trojans, as are the “Dardanian young warriors”: both names recall legendary ancestors of Troy. Achilles sweeps through their ranks, but his desire is fixed on one man. Hector, Troy’s chief defender, has killed Achilles’ beloved companion Patroclus; the slaughter around him serves a revenge that will not be satisfied until he reaches Hector himself.

The Latin condenses a long sequence of Homeric fighting into a single statement of overwhelming destruction. Homer first shows Achilles leaping among the Trojans with a terrible shout and killing a named leader, Iphition (*Iliad* 20.381–383). Later, his onslaught becomes a landscape-wide catastrophe: he rages like fire through dry mountain woodland, while “the dark earth flowed with blood” (*Iliad* 20.490–494). His horses then trample bodies and shields as oxen thresh grain beneath their feet (*Iliad* 20.495–499). The Latin leaves out both the individual victim and these extended comparisons, retaining the spear, the immense number of dead, and the relentless forward movement.

“Thirsting for Hector’s blood” gives this compressed account its personal focus. It is not a translation of the supplied Homeric battle descriptions, but makes explicit the purpose driving Achilles through them. The unfinished turn, “But the Dardanian young warriors,” begins to shift attention from the attacker to the Trojans facing his assault.
