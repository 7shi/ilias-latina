### 1008–1009 (*Iliad* 23.536–538, 23.700–701)

> 1008 Tydides *tyrsin* cursu pedibusque ferocem
> (Tydides … in the race, and fierce of foot)
>
> 1009 Merionem superat; luctando uincitur Aiax
> (Meriones defeats; at wrestling is beaten Ajax,)

The funeral games turn grief for Patroclus into competition among the warriors gathered to honor him. Tydides is Diomedes, named here as the son of Tydeus; Meriones is a Cretan commander. Their contest is a chariot race, despite the description of Meriones as “fierce of foot”; one word of the first verse is damaged beyond sure reading. In Homer, Diomedes finishes first and Meriones fourth among five competitors. The Latin preserves their relative success but makes the crowded race sound like a contest between just two men, leaving out its accidents, rivalries, and disputes over prizes.

The passage then moves straight to wrestling; the boxing match, which comes between them in Homer, follows only afterwards (1011). Ajax is the great Greek warrior, son of Telamon, whose opponent is Ulysses, called Odysseus in Greek. Their match pits formidable strength against resourcefulness and skill. Here the Latin gives Ajax a straightforward defeat, whereas Homer leaves the contest unresolved: each man has shown his ability without securing a decisive victory when Achilles stops them and awards both equal prizes (*Iliad* 23.735–739). The compressed account thus turns a balanced struggle into another brief result, carrying the reader rapidly through the honors paid to the dead.
