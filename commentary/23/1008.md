### 1008–1009 (*Iliad* 23.536–538, 23.700–701)

> 1008 Tydides *tyrsin* cursu pedibusque ferocem
> (Tydides [unclear] in the race)
>
> 1009 Merionem superat; luctando uincitur Aiax
> (defeats Meriones, fierce of foot; Aiax is beaten at wrestling.)

The funeral games turn grief for Patroclus into competition among the warriors gathered to honour him. Tydides is Diomedes, named here as the son of Tydeus; Meriones is a Cretan commander. Their contest is a chariot race, despite the description of Meriones as “fierce of foot.” In Homer, Diomedes finishes first and Meriones fourth among five competitors. The Latin preserves their relative success but makes the crowded race sound like a contest between just two men, leaving out its accidents, rivalries, and disputes over prizes.

The passage then moves straight to wrestling, passing over the boxing match that intervenes in Homer. Aiax is the great Greek warrior, son of Telamon, whose opponent is Ulysses, better known by his Greek name, Odysseus. Their match pits formidable strength against resourcefulness and skill. Here the Latin gives Aiax a straightforward defeat, whereas Homer leaves the contest unresolved: each man has shown his ability without securing a decisive victory when Achilles stops them and awards both equal prizes (*Iliad* 23.735–739). The compressed account thus turns a balanced struggle into another brief result, carrying the reader rapidly through the honours paid to the dead.
