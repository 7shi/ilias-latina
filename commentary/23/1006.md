### 1006 (*Iliad* 24.14–18)

> 1006 Tum circa tumulum miseros rapit Hectoris artus
> (Then he drags Hector’s pitiable limbs around the burial mound.)

Achilles turns from honouring Patroclus to dishonouring Hector, the Trojan champion who killed him. The mound is Patroclus’s: the funeral has not quieted Achilles’ grief or his anger against the dead enemy. Calling Hector’s limbs “pitiable” directs sympathy towards the body being abused, setting its treatment against the dignity just accorded to Patroclus.

The Latin moves straight from the funeral to this renewed violence, passing over the athletic contests that Achilles holds in his friend’s honour in the *Iliad*. Homer presents the dragging as a repeated act: Achilles harnesses his horses, ties Hector behind his chariot, and drags him three times around Patroclus’s mound before returning to his shelter and leaving the body stretched face down in the dust (*Iliad* 24.14–18). The Latin compresses that routine into one stark action, without specifying the three circuits. This is not another journey around Troy’s walls: Achilles now makes his friend’s grave the centre of his vengeance. The honour paid to one body and the humiliation inflicted on the other arise from the same grief.
