Ajax, with great strength, kills Acamas. Menelaus captures the huge Adrastus and drags him toward the ships with his hands bound behind his back, seeking the joy of triumph over his enemy. The Danaans press forward, while the young Trojan warriors retreat, shielding their exposed backs.

Mavortian Hector recognizes that the gods are fighting for the Danaans and that the “armed maiden,” the goddess also called “unwed Pallas” and Minerva, is draining his men’s strength. He immediately heads for the walls, orders Hecuba to be summoned, and urges that the goddess be appeased. The Iliades, the Trojan women, ascend to Pallas’s fortified citadel, decorate the altars with festive garlands, and sacrifice sheep according to custom. Hecuba, a suppliant at Minerva’s temple, pours out a mother’s prayers for her beloved children and husband.

Meanwhile, Glaucus prepares to fight Diomedes with drawn steel. Diomedes asks his name, lineage, and place of origin. As Glaucus attempts to cast his spear, Diomedes, called the “Aetolian hero,” challenges his rashness in confronting superior weapons. He identifies the arms Glaucus faces as those of a guest-friend who wounded Venus’s right hand and pierced Mars in battle, and tells him to restrain his anger and weapons. The book ends with the two abandoning their confrontation and exchanging shields; no outcome of Hecuba’s prayers is reported.
