### 553–562 (*Iliad* 6.119–127, 6.215, 5.336, 5.855–858, 6.226, 6.232–233)

> 553 interea Glaucus stricto decernere ferro
> (Meanwhile Glaucus prepares to fight with drawn steel)
>
> 554 cum Diomede parat nomenque genusque roganti
> (against Diomedes, who asks his name and lineage,)
>
> 555 qui sit et unde ferat, magnis cum uiribus hastam
> (who he is and whence he brings his arms. With all his strength)
>
> 556 mittere temptabat; temptanti Aetolius heros:
> (he was trying to hurl his spear; as he tried, the Aetolian hero)
>
> 557 "Quo ruis?" - exclamat - "quae te, scelerate, furentem
> (cries out: “Where are you rushing? Villain, what thought)

> (drives you in your fury to face me with weapons no match for mine?)

> (You see the arms of a guest-friend, who wounded Venus’s right hand)
>
> 560 perculit et summo pupugit certamine Martem.
> (and pierced Mars in the height of battle.)
>
> 561 Pone truces animos infestaque tela coerce."
> (Put aside your savage anger and hold back your hostile weapons.”)
>
> 562 Post haec inter se posito certamine pugnae
> (After this, once they had set aside their fight with one another,)

While Hecube prays inside Troy, the narrative returns to the battlefield. Glaucus is a Lycian warrior fighting on the Trojan side; Diomedes is one of the Greeks’ most formidable champions. His title “Aetolian hero” recalls his family’s origins in Aetolia, a region of Greece. Glaucus is already preparing to throw his spear when Diomedes interrupts him. What begins as a challenge ends with both men abandoning their fight.

The key to that reversal is “guest-friend.” This means more than an acquaintance: hospitality could establish a lasting bond between households, inherited by their descendants even when they found themselves on opposing sides of a war. In Homer, Diomedes’ question prompts a long account of Glaucus’ ancestry. Diomedes then recognizes that his grandfather Oeneus once entertained Glaucus’ grandfather Bellerophon, and welcomes Glaucus as “an ancient guest-friend of my father’s house” (*Iliad* 6.215). The Latin removes the family history and the explanation of how recognition occurs. It retains the decisive relationship, but its sudden appearance makes the change from hostility to friendship much more abrupt.

The tone changes too. Homer’s Diomedes is threatening, but begins with the respectful address “best of mortal men” (*Iliad* 6.123). Here he calls Glaucus a villain, stresses their unequal strength, and reinforces his command with a boast about wounding gods. Venus and Mars are the Roman counterparts of Aphrodite and Ares, whom Diomedes wounded earlier in the fighting. These exploits are brought forward from Book 5 and made part of his warning; Athena’s assistance in those encounters goes unmentioned. The Latin thus combines an inherited obligation of friendship with an assertion of overwhelming force. Instead of Homer’s mutual proposal to avoid one another’s spears, Diomedes orders Glaucus to restrain himself. Nevertheless, the closing line restores the mutual character of the settlement: both men put their conflict aside, and the sentence carries their reconciliation into the next passage.
