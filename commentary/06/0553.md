### 553–562 (*Iliad* 6.119–127, 6.215, 5.336, 5.855–858, 6.226, 6.232–233)

> 553 interea Glaucus stricto decernere ferro
> (meanwhile Glaucus, to fight it out with drawn steel)
>
> 554 cum Diomede parat nomenque genusque roganti
> (with Diomedes, prepares, and at him, asking his name and lineage,)
>
> 555 qui sit et unde ferat, magnis cum uiribus hastam
> (who he is and whence he bears his arms, with great strength his spear)
>
> 556 mittere temptabat; temptanti Aetolius heros:
> (he was trying to hurl; to him, as he tried, the Aetolian hero)
>
> 557 "Quo ruis?" - exclamat - "quae te, scelerate, furentem
> (cries out: “Where are you rushing? What, villain, you in your fury)
>
> 558 mens agit imparibus mecum concurrere telis?
> (—what mind drives you to clash with me with unequal weapons?)
>
> 559 Hospitis arma uides, Veneris qui uulnere dextram
> (You see the arms of a guest-friend, who with a wound Venus’s right hand)
>
> 560 perculit et summo pupugit certamine Martem.
> (struck, and pierced Mars at the height of the contest.)
>
> 561 Pone truces animos infestaque tela coerce."
> (Put aside your savage anger and hold back your hostile weapons.”)
>
> 562 Post haec inter se posito certamine pugnae
> (After this, once they had set aside their fight with one another,)

While Hecuba prays inside Troy, the narrative returns to the battlefield. Glaucus is a Lycian warrior fighting on the Trojan side; Diomedes is one of the Greeks’ most formidable champions. His title “Aetolian hero” recalls his family’s origins in Aetolia, a region of Greece. Glaucus is already preparing to throw his spear when Diomedes interrupts him. What begins as a challenge ends with both men abandoning their fight.

The key to that reversal is “guest-friend.” This means more than an acquaintance: hospitality could establish a lasting bond between households, inherited by their descendants even when they found themselves on opposing sides of a war. In Homer, Diomedes’ question prompts a long account of Glaucus’ ancestry. Diomedes then recognizes that his grandfather Oeneus once entertained Glaucus’ grandfather Bellerophon, and welcomes Glaucus as “an ancient guest-friend of my father’s house” (*Iliad* 6.215). The Latin removes the family history and the explanation of how recognition occurs. It retains the decisive relationship, but its sudden appearance makes the change from hostility to friendship much more abrupt.

The tone changes too. Homer’s Diomedes is threatening, but begins with the respectful address “best of mortal men” (*Iliad* 6.123). Here he calls Glaucus a villain, stresses their unequal strength, and reinforces his command with a boast about wounding gods. Venus and Mars are the Roman counterparts of Aphrodite and Ares. Diomedes wounded Venus earlier in the fighting; the wounding of Mars was there given to Pallas herself, but here Diomedes claims it for himself, as in Homer, where Athena drives his spear home. Her assistance goes unmentioned in his warning. The Latin thus combines an inherited obligation of friendship with an assertion of overwhelming force. Instead of Homer’s mutual proposal to avoid one another’s spears, Diomedes orders Glaucus to restrain himself. Nevertheless, the closing line restores the mutual character of the settlement: both men put their conflict aside, and the sentence carries their reconciliation into the next passage.
