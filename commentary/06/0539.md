### 539–541 (*Iliad* 6.37–38, 6.52–53)

> 539 interimit uastumque capit Menelaus Adrastum
> (slays, and Menelaus captures huge Adrastus)
>
> 540 et rapit ad classes manibus post terga reuinctis,
> (and drags him to the ships, his hands bound behind his back,)
>
> 541 ut ui deducat laetos ex hoste triumphos.
> (to win by force joyful triumphs over his enemy.)

The opening completes the preceding sentence: Ajax kills Acamas, the Thracian warrior fighting for Troy. The narrative then turns immediately to another Greek success. Menelaus, brother of the Greek commander Agamemnon, captures Adrastus, a warrior on the Trojan side, and takes him towards the Greek ships, where the invading army has its camp. Adrastus’ bound hands make his helplessness clear; the final line presents his capture as a source of victorious joy.

In Homer, this encounter develops into a darker scene. Adrastus is thrown from his chariot after his terrified horses run into a tree. Captured alive, he clasps Menelaus’ knees and offers a rich ransom. Menelaus is moved and is about to entrust him to an attendant to take back to the ships when Agamemnon intervenes, rebukes his brother’s mercy, and kills the prisoner (*Iliad* 6.37–65). The Latin keeps the capture and turns the proposed journey to the ships into an action, adding the detail of the bound hands. It omits both Adrastus’ appeal and Agamemnon’s intervention. The added language of joyful triumph thus closes as a straightforward victory what Homer makes a story of compassion briefly offered and violently withdrawn.
