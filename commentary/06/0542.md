### 542–546 (*Iliad* 6.73–76, 6.94–97, 6.86–87, 6.237)

> 542 Incumbunt Danai, cedit Troiana iuuentus
> (The Danai press forward; the young Trojan warriors give way)
>
> 543 tergaque nuda tegit. Sensit Mauortius Hector
> (and cover their exposed backs. Warlike Hector realized)
>
> 544 pro Danais pugnare deos ualidasque suorum
> (that the gods were fighting for the Danai and that his men’s mighty)
>
> 545 uirginis armigerae subduci numine uires
> (strength was being drained by the power of the armed maiden,)
>
> 546 continuoque petit muros Hecubamque uocari
> (and at once heads for the walls and orders Hecuba to be summoned,)

The individual Greek successes now become a general advance. The Danai are the Greeks, whose pressure forces the Trojan warriors to retreat, guarding their exposed backs—perhaps with their shields. Hector, Troy’s foremost defender, recognizes a danger beyond ordinary military defeat: the gods themselves are helping the enemy. The “armed maiden” is Minerva, the Roman counterpart of the Greek goddess Athena, a virgin goddess of warfare and a powerful supporter of the Greeks. Against her divine power, even the Trojans’ strength is failing. Hector therefore turns towards the city and sends for Hecuba, his mother and the queen of Troy.

In Homer, the Trojans would have fled into the city had Helenus, a son of King Priam and a skilled interpreter of omens, not intervened. He urges Hector and Aeneas, another leading Trojan warrior, to rally their forces. He then sends Hector into Troy to ask their mother Hecuba to gather the older women and make an appeal to Athena. Their immediate purpose is to persuade the goddess to hold back Diomedes, the Greek warrior whose attacks have made him especially terrifying (*Iliad* 6.73–97). The Latin omits Helenus and gives Hector himself the decisive recognition of divine hostility. It also broadens the danger: rather than singling out Diomedes, it presents the Trojan army’s strength as being sapped by the goddess. The movement towards the walls thus turns the story from battlefield resistance towards an appeal for divine mercy.
