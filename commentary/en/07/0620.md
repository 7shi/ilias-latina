### 620–627 (*Iliad* 7.287)

> 620 deponunt animos. Tunc bello maximus Hector:
> (lay aside their passion. Then Hector, greatest in war:)
>
> 621 "Quae te terra uirum, qui te genuere parentes?
> (“What land produced a man like you, what parents gave you birth?)
>
> 622 Viribus es proles generosa atque inclita" - dixit.
> (Your strength shows you to be a noble and illustrious son,” he said.)
>
> 623 At contra se ferre parat Telamonius Aiax:
> (But in turn Telamonian Ajax prepares to present himself:)
>
> 624 "Hesiona de matre uides Telamone creatum,
> (“From Hesione as mother you see one born of Telamon;)
>
> 625 nobilis est domus et fama generosa propago."
> (my house is noble, and my lineage renowned.”)
>
> 626 Hector, ut Hesionae nomen casusque recordans:
> (Hector, as one recalling Hesione’s name and misfortunes:)
>
> 627 "Absistamus" - ait - "sanguis communis utriquest",
> (“Let us desist,” he says, “the blood is common to us both,”)

The two warriors promptly set aside their passion, completing the response begun in the preceding passage. Hector’s admiration for his opponent’s strength now leads to a question about his origins. Ajax, the Greek champion, answers with his parents’ names: “Telamonian” means “son of Telamon.” What began as a deadly contest becomes an encounter between men who discover that they belong to the same family.

Hesione supplies the connection. She was a daughter of Laomedon, an earlier king of Troy, and sister of Priam, Hector’s father. Hercules rescued her after she had been exposed to a sea-monster. Later, when he captured Troy, he gave her in marriage to Telamon, who had been the first to scale the city’s wall. Her “misfortunes” thus connect an earlier Greek attack on Troy with the present war. If Ajax is her son, as he declares here, he and Hector are first cousins: their shared blood gives Hector a personal reason to stop fighting.

This recognition is an addition to Homer’s account, not a shortened version of it. In the *Iliad*, Hector praises Ajax’s strength and skill but proposes ending the duel because night has come; there is no inquiry about his parentage or discovery of kinship. The Latin also departs from the usual genealogy, in which Hesione is the mother of Teucer, Ajax’s half-brother, rather than of Ajax himself. By making her Ajax’s mother, the retelling turns the interruption by the men sent to part them into a reconciliation grounded in family ties. The opposing champions remain enemies in the larger war, but Hector now recognizes his own kin in the man he has been trying to kill.
