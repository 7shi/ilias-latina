### 572–574 (*Iliad* 6.475–478)

> 572 attollensque manus: "Precor, o pater optime" - dixit -
> (and raising his hands, he said: “I pray, O best of fathers,)
>
> 573 "ut meus hic, pro quo tua numina, natus, adoro,
> (that this son of mine, for whom I appeal to your divine power,)
>
> 574 uirtutes patrias primis imitetur ab annis."
> (may imitate his father’s virtues from his earliest years.”)

With his son now in his arms, Hector turns from comforting the child to praying for his future. His raised hands accompany an appeal to the divine father, Jupiter—the god called Zeus in Homer. Hector asks that the courage and excellence which guide his own life should take root in his son from childhood. The tenderness of the embrace thus leads back to the responsibilities of a warrior: he hopes that the child will grow into the qualities his father embodies.

The Latin compresses a more specific prayer. In Homer, Hector addresses Zeus and the other gods, asking that his son become “outstanding among the Trojans, as I am,” strong in battle and a powerful ruler of Troy (*Iliad* 6.475–478). Here the other gods and the hope of kingship disappear, while strength and public distinction are gathered into “his father’s virtues.” The addition “from his earliest years” brings that distant adult future closer to the infant before us. Hector’s prayer becomes a compact wish for continuity between father and son, with his own conduct offered as the child’s example.
