### 575–577 (*Iliad* 7.1–2, 7.55)

> 575 Haec ait et portis acies petit acer apertis,
> (So he spoke and eagerly made for the battle lines through the open gates,)
>
> 576 una deinde Paris. Postquam in certamina uentumst,
> (then Paris went with him. When they reached the fighting,)
>
> 577 protinus in medium procedit maximus Hector
> (mighty Hector at once advanced into the midst)

Hector turns immediately from his prayer for his son to the fighting outside Troy. Paris, his brother and a fellow Trojan prince, accompanies him. The transition brings Hector’s public responsibilities sharply back into view: having asked that his child inherit his virtues, he returns to the battlefield where those virtues are tested.

The Latin makes this departure more abrupt than it is in Homer. There, Hector first returns the child to his wife, Andromache, and speaks further words of comfort before they part; he subsequently meets Paris and exchanges words with him. Here “So he spoke” connects the departure directly to Hector’s prayer, leaving the family farewell behind without further description.

The return to battle is equally compressed. Homer describes renewed fighting and an agreement between the gods Athena and Apollo to bring about a challenge to single combat. The Latin passes straight to Hector’s advance. In Homer, Hector moves into the midst and begins holding back the Trojan ranks (*Iliad* 7.55); here the passage retains only the commanding movement of the warrior himself. This prepares for his challenge to an individual Greek opponent, shifting the action from the general fighting to a contest centred on Hector.
