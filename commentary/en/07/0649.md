### 649 (*Iliad* 7.436–441)

> 649 Tum renouant fossas et uallum robore cingunt.
> (Then they restore the trenches and surround the rampart with an oak palisade.)

After burning their dead, the Greeks turn to securing their camp beside the ships. The movement from funeral pyres to defensive works brings the army back from mourning to preparation for renewed fighting. The trenches and wooden barrier are intended to keep the enemy from reaching the ships, on which the Greeks depend for their eventual return home.

The Latin compresses Homer’s substantial building scene into a single line. In the *Iliad*, the Greeks construct a wall with high towers, “a defense for the ships and for themselves,” and gates wide enough for chariots; outside it they dig a broad, deep ditch and plant stakes (*Iliad* 7.436–441). Here the towers and gates disappear, leaving trenches and an oak palisade as the essentials of the defense. “Restore” also makes the work sound like the renewal of existing fortifications rather than the construction of the new barrier described by Homer.
