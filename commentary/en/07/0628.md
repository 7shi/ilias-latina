### 628–632 (*Iliad* 7.303–307)

> 628 et prior Aeaciden aurato munerat ense
> (and he is first to give Aeacides a gold-adorned sword,)
>
> 629 inque uicem, quo se bellator cinxerat Aiax,
> (and in return, that with which the warrior Ajax had girded himself,)
>
> 630 accipit insignem uario caelamine balteum.
> (he receives, a belt distinguished by its varied engraving.)
>
> 631 Post haec extemplo Graium Troumque cateruae
> (After this, at once the bands of Greeks and Trojans)
>
> 632 discedunt caelumque tegit nox atra tenebris.
> (depart, and black night covers the sky with darkness.)

Hector follows his declaration of shared blood with a gift, offering his former opponent a richly decorated sword. “Aeacides” means “descendant of Aeacus” and here names Ajax, whose father Telamon was Aeacus’s son. Ajax answers Hector’s generosity with his own belt. The exchange gives a tangible form to their reconciliation: weapons and military equipment become tokens of goodwill rather than instruments of violence. Neither man has defeated the other, and both can leave the encounter with honor.

The exchange itself comes from Homer, though the Latin changes the gifts’ appearance. In the *Iliad*, Hector gives a silver-studded sword together with its sheath and carrying strap, while Ajax’s belt gleams crimson (*Iliad* 7.303–305). Here the sword is adorned with gold and the belt with varied engravings. The Latin also broadens the departure: Homer follows each champion back to his own people, whereas here the assembled bands of Greeks and Trojans disperse together.

Black night closes the scene. In Homer, approaching night was the stated reason for ending the duel; in this retelling, the discovery of kinship has supplied the personal motive, and darkness now seals the pause in fighting. The champions have exchanged gifts, but their peoples remain on opposite sides of the war.
