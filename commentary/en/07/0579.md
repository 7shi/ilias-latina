### 579–584 (*Iliad* 7.161, 7.168, 7.165–166, 7.162, 7.164, 7.167, 7.163)

> 579 Nec mora: continuo fraudis commentor Vlixes
> (No delay: at once Ulysses, contriver of deceit,)

> 580 et ferus Idomeneus et notus gente paterna
> (and fierce Idomeneus, and, renowned for his father’s lineage,)
>
> 581 Meriones Graiumque simul dux acer Atrides
> (Meriones, and with them the Greeks’ valiant leader Atrides,)
>
> 582 Aiacesque duo \<et> claris speciosus in armis
> (and the two Ajaxes, and, splendid in shining arms,)
>
> 583 Eurypylus magnoque Thoas Andraemone natus
> (Eurypylus, and Thoas, son of great Andraemon,)
>
> 584 quique manum Veneris uiolauit uulnere tristi
> (and he who injured Venus’ hand with a grievous wound,)

Nine Greek champions answer Hector’s challenge. They are not preparing to attack him together: these are the men willing to face him, from whom a single opponent must be chosen. The opening “No delay” makes their response seem immediate and confident. In Homer, however, the challenge is followed by an uncomfortable silence. Menelaus volunteers, but his brother Agamemnon restrains him; only after the aged Nestor rebukes the Greeks do nine men rise. Homer introduces their response with “So the old man reproached them, and all nine stood up” (*Iliad* 7.161). The Latin passes over this hesitation and rebuke, moving directly from Hector’s challenge to the gathering of his possible opponents.

The catalogue brings together leaders distinguished by cunning, courage, ancestry, and appearance. Ulysses is Odysseus, whose familiar description as “contriver of deceit” again marks intelligence and trickery as his particular strengths. Idomeneus is the king of Crete, and Meriones his companion in arms. Atrides means “son of Atreus”: here it identifies Agamemnon, the chief commander of the Greek army, rather than his brother Menelaus. The two Ajaxes are the son of Telamon and the son of Oileus. Eurypylus is another Greek commander, while Thoas, son of Andraemon, leads the Aetolians.

The Latin retains all nine volunteers but changes their order and their presentation. Homer puts Agamemnon first and Odysseus last; here Ulysses heads the list, while its final place goes to a warrior identified not by name but by an extraordinary deed. This is Diomedes, who earlier wounded Venus—the goddess called Aphrodite by the Greeks—when she tried to rescue her son Aeneas from battle. The closing reminder makes him especially formidable: among the men ready to meet Hector stands one who has already dared to strike a goddess.
