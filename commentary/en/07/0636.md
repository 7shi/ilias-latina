### 636–640 (*Iliad* 7.345–346, 7.350–351, 7.363–364, 7.379)

> 636 in coetum uenere Phryges. Tunc maximus Hector
> (the Phrygians came together in assembly. Then mighty Hector,)
>
> 637 cum sociis memorans hesternae funera caedis
> (recalling with his companions the deaths in yesterday’s slaughter,)
>
> 638 suadet ut inuictis Helene reddatur Achiuis
> (urges that Helen be returned to the unconquered Achaeans,)
>
> 639 praedaque quae duros Menelai mulceat ignes
> (and plunder to soothe the harsh fires of Menelaus,)
>
> 640 idque placet cunctis. Tunc saeuo missus Atridae
> (and everyone approves. Then, sent to the fierce Atrides,)

The dawn announced in the preceding line brings the Trojans into council. Here they are called Phrygians, a name the Latin poet uses for the people of Troy; the Achaeans are the Greeks. After the previous day’s losses, Hector, Troy’s leading defender, proposes a way to end the conflict: restore Helen to the Greeks and offer compensation to Menelaus, her husband. Her departure with the Trojan prince Paris had brought the Greek army to Troy. The memory of the dead now gives Hector’s proposal an immediate urgency.

This council differs sharply from Homer’s. In the *Iliad*, the Trojans meet in a troubled assembly outside King Priam’s palace, and it is the elder Antenor, not Hector, who urges them to return Helen and her possessions. Paris refuses to surrender her, though he offers to give back everything he brought from her homeland and add wealth of his own. The Latin poet combines the demand for Helen’s return with the offer of plunder, assigns the proposal to Hector, and removes Paris’s resistance. A divided debate becomes a unanimous decision.

That change makes the Trojans appear ready to address the cause of the war, rather than merely bargain over property while keeping Helen. Hector’s concern for the losses also connects the council directly to the fighting just narrated. In Homer, this debate occurs before the night’s rest; here it belongs to the new morning.

The unfinished final sentence turns toward the delivery of the proposal to the Greeks. Atrides means “son of Atreus,” a title used of both Menelaus and his brother Agamemnon, the commander of the Greek army. The description “fierce” reminds us that the Trojans must still win acceptance from their enemies: agreement within their own council does not yet mean peace.
