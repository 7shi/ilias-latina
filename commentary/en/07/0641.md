### 641–643 (*Iliad* 7.381–386, 7.400–404)

> 641 pertulit Idaeus Troum mandata; neque ille
> (Idaeus delivered the message of the Trojans; but he)
>
> 642 aut animum praedae aut dictis accommodat aures,
> (takes no interest in the plunder and lends no ear to the words,)
>
> 643 ultro etiam castris Idaeum excedere iussit.
> (and even ordered Idaeus to leave the camp.)

The unanimous Trojan proposal meets a flat refusal. Idaeus, Troy’s herald, carries the Trojans’ message to the son of Atreus mentioned at the end of the preceding passage. Neither the offer of treasure nor the accompanying appeal receives a hearing. The abrupt dismissal closes the exchange: the Trojans’ willingness to return Helen has not brought peace any closer.

The Latin poet compresses a public debate into one leader’s response. In Homer, Idaeus finds the Greeks assembled beside Agamemnon’s ship and addresses the sons of Atreus and the other chiefs. The forceful rejection comes from Diomedes, a leading Greek warrior, who declares that they should accept neither Paris’s possessions nor Helen herself: even a fool can see that Troy’s destruction is near (*Iliad* 7.400–402). The army cheers his speech. Here the assembly, the speaker’s identity, and his confidence in Troy’s approaching defeat disappear; refusal is assigned instead to the Atrides whom Idaeus addresses. Vollmer’s index takes him as Agamemnon; Plessis’s lists the verse under Menelaus, though it has Agamemnon receive the message and dismiss the herald (641, 643).

The change in the preceding Trojan council also changes the meaning of this rejection. Homer’s Trojans offer treasure but refuse to surrender Helen, so Diomedes rejects more than they have actually offered. In the Latin narrative, her return is genuinely on offer, and the Greek refusal therefore blocks a possible settlement of the war’s original grievance. The order expelling Idaeus from the camp is an added touch of hostility. Homer’s Agamemnon endorses the rejection but also grants the herald’s separate request for a pause to burn the dead; the Latin passage ends instead with the envoy being sent away.
