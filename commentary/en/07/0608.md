### 608–610 (*Iliad* 7.262–264)

> 608 ensis et exiguo ceruicem uulnere libat.
> (the sword, and with a slight wound grazes his neck.)
>
> 609 Acrius impugnans rursus consurgit in hostem
> (Attacking more fiercely, again rises against the foe)
>
> 610 Priamides nec iam ferro Telamone creatum,
> (Priamides, and no longer with the steel Telamon’s son,)

The sword that slipped along Hector’s shield now reaches his neck, but only grazes it. Rather than checking him, the wound prompts a fiercer attack. Priamides means “son of Priam,” the king of Troy, and here names Hector; Telamon’s son is his opponent, Ajax. The passage ends mid-sentence, as Hector abandons the sword for another means of fighting.

In Homer, it is Ajax’s spear that cuts Hector’s neck, and “dark blood welled up” (*Iliad* 7.262). The Latin continues its reshaping of that spear-thrust into a sword-stroke, explicitly making the wound slight and omitting the blood. Both poems stress Hector’s refusal to yield, but the Latin makes his renewed aggression more emphatic: he attacks more fiercely than before. Homer then has Hector draw back and take up a stone in his powerful hand (*Iliad* 7.263–264). Here the unfinished sentence prepares that change of weapon without yet revealing what will replace the sword.
