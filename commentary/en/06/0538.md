### 538 (*Iliad* 6.5–8)

> 538 Interea magnis Acamantem uiribus Aiax
> (Meanwhile with great strength Acamas Ajax)

After the divine conflict that ended the preceding book, the narrative returns to the human battlefield. Ajax, son of Telamon, is one of the Greeks’ strongest warriors. His opponent, Acamas, is a leader of the Thracians, allies fighting on Troy’s side. The sentence runs over into the next verse, which gives its verb.

In Homer, Ajax’s attack breaks the Trojan formation and brings relief to his comrades. He is called the “bulwark of the Achaeans”—that is, the Greeks—and Acamas is introduced as the son of Eussorus, the finest warrior among the Thracians, “brave and tall” (*Iliad* 6.5–8). The Latin compresses that fuller introduction into a direct encounter between two named opponents. Its emphasis on Ajax’s great strength preserves the impression of overwhelming power, while leaving aside, in this line, both Acamas’ distinguished standing and the wider effect of the attack on the battle.
