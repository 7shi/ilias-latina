### 551–552 (*Iliad* 6.293–310)

> 551 Dumque preces Hecube supplex ad templa Mineruae
> (And while Hecuba, a suppliant, prayers at Minerva’s temple)
>
> 552 pro caris genetrix natis et coniuge fundit,
> (for her dear children and husband, a mother, pours out,)

The focus narrows from the women’s collective worship to the fears of the queen herself. Hecuba is the wife of Priam, Troy’s king, and mother of Hector. She appeals to Minerva, the Roman counterpart of Athena, for those closest to her. The word “mother” gives the public ceremony a personal urgency: Troy’s danger threatens her own children and husband.

In Homer, Hecuba brings the offering to the temple, but Theano, Athena’s priestess, formally addresses the goddess. The Latin transfers that prayer to Hecuba, keeping Hector’s mother at the center of the action. Its emphasis also changes. Theano asks Athena to break Diomedes’ spear and bring him down before Troy’s gates, then appeals for mercy on the city, its wives, and its young children (*Iliad* 6.305–310). Here the military request disappears, and the wider community’s peril is expressed through one mother’s concern for her family. Her prayer continues the attempt to win protection from the very goddess who has been helping the Greeks.
