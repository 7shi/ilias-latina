### 892–893 (*Iliad* 19.364–369)

> 892 Talibus ornatus donis Thetideius heros
> (Equipped with such gifts, the hero, son of Thetis,)
>
> 893 in medias acies immani turbine fertur,
> (is borne into the midst of the battle lines in a huge whirlwind,)

The hero is Achilles, son of the sea-goddess Thetis. “Such gifts” are the divine armor she has just brought him, forged by Vulcan. After the long description of its decorated shield, the story suddenly accelerates: Achilles, previously overwhelmed by grief for his dead companion Patroclus, now carries that grief back into battle as destructive fury. Hector, who killed Patroclus, is the intended target of his revenge.

The Latin compresses Homer’s account sharply. In the corresponding passage, Achilles is still arming among his comrades: his teeth chatter, his eyes shine like fire, and unbearable sorrow fills his heart as he puts on the god’s gifts (*Iliad* 19.364–369). The Latin retains the divine equipment but replaces these signs of inward anguish with the outward force of a whirlwind. Its second verse pushes beyond the act of arming and sends Achilles straight toward combat.

This swift transition also bypasses much of the larger story told in Homer’s nineteenth book, especially Achilles’ reconciliation with Agamemnon, the Greek commander whose quarrel with him had kept him away from the fighting. Here the essential connection is immediate: the loss of Patroclus, the arrival of new armor, and the return to battle form one continuous movement toward revenge.
