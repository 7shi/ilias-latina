### 960–967 (*Iliad* 22.289–291)

> 960 Exclamant Danai. Contra Priameius heros
> (The Danaans shout. In reply, the hero, Priam’s son,)
>
> 961 uibratum iaculum Vulcania torquet in arma.
> (hurls his brandished javelin against the armor of Vulcan.)
>
> 962 Nec successus adest: nam duro inflectitur auro
> (Nor is success at hand: for against the hard gold is bent)
>
> 963 dissiluitque mucro. Gemuerunt agmina Troum.
> (and the point shatters. The ranks of the Trojans groaned.)
>
> 964 Concurrunt iterum collatis fortiter armis
> (Again they charge bravely together, clashing their weapons,)
>
> 965 inque uicem duros euitant comminus enses.
> (and at close quarters each dodges the other’s hard sword.)
>
> 966 Nec sufferre ualet ultra sortemque supremam
> (Nor can he any longer bear both his final lot)
>
> 967 stantemque Aeaciden defectis uiribus Hector.
> (and Aeacides standing firm, his strength failing, Hector.)

Hector answers Achilles’ unsuccessful cast with a throw of his own, but the two men survive for different reasons. Hector has escaped through alertness and agility; Achilles is protected by extraordinary armor. “The hero, Priam’s son” is Hector, son of Priam, Troy’s king. His opponent is called Aeacides because Achilles is a descendant of Aeacus, his grandfather. The armor of Vulcan was made for Achilles by the divine craftsman and already described in the Latin poem as fashioned from gold. Its hard surface defeats Hector’s attack.

The cries surrounding this exchange make the duel a struggle watched by two peoples. The Danaans are the Greeks; the Trojans’ groan registers the failure of their champion’s weapon. These collective reactions are additions to the corresponding Homeric verses. In the *Iliad*, Hector’s spear strikes the middle of Achilles’ shield without missing, but rebounds far away, leaving Hector angry (*Iliad* 22.289–291). The Latin instead emphasizes the weapon’s failure against the armor and lets the Trojan ranks express the distress that Homer locates in Hector himself.

The point that bends and shatters on the gold makes this failure more destructive than Homer’s rebound; the Latin does not say plainly whether it is the point of the javelin or of a sword, though the javelin has just been thrown. The narrative then moves directly into another vigorous clash. Neither fighter immediately lands a wound: they exchange attacks at close range and evade one another’s swords. This sustained, mutual sword-fighting has no counterpart in the Homeric lines being retold. It briefly presents the opponents as evenly matched in courage and skill, even though Achilles’ armor has already exposed the inequality between them.

That appearance of balance cannot last. The final sentence brings Hector’s dwindling physical strength together with his approaching death. He faces both an armed opponent still standing before him and a fate he can no longer resist. The passage does not yet describe the fatal blow; it ends with the exhaustion that makes Hector’s earlier resourcefulness insufficient to save him.
