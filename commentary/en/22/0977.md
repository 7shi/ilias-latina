### 977–979 (*Iliad* 22.324–327, 22.337)

> 977 et medias rigida transfixit cuspide fauces.
> (and pierced him through the middle of the throat with the hard spear-point.)
>
> 978 Exsultant Danai, Troes sua uulnera deflent.
> (The Danaans exult; the Trojans lament their own wounds.)
>
> 979 Tunc sic amissis infelix uiribus Hector:
> (Then, his strength gone, ill-fated Hector spoke as follows:)

The spear cast by Achilles strikes home. Hector’s failing resistance succumbs to a mortal wound, but not yet to silence: the passage closes by preparing his last appeal to his conqueror. His strength is gone, yet he still has a voice.

The Latin condenses Homer’s precise account of the injury. In the *Iliad*, Achilles strikes the exposed throat where the collarbones meet the neck, a place “where destruction comes most swiftly to life” (*Iliad* 22.325). The spear passes through, but Homer then explains that it has not severed the windpipe, allowing Hector to speak (*Iliad* 22.328–329). The Latin keeps the decisive thrust through the throat without that anatomical qualification, moving directly from the blow to the response it provokes.

Between the wound and Hector’s speech, the Latin adds a brief contrast between the two peoples watching their champions: the Danaans are the Greeks, Achilles’ people, set against the Trojans, whom Hector has defended. The Greeks rejoice, while the Trojans mourn “their own wounds.” Hector’s injury is thus felt as theirs: the fall of their foremost defender is a disaster for the whole community. This single sentence widens the scene beyond the two fighters before returning to the exhausted Hector. Against the victors’ exultation, “ill-fated” directs sympathy toward the man who must now speak in defeat.
