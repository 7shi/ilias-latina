### 996–997 (*Iliad* 22.361–363, 22.395)

> 996 ore truci iactat, uitam miserabilis Hector
> (hurls from his savage mouth, pitiable Hector)
>
> 997 reddidit. Hunc animi nondum satiatus Achilles
> (gave up his life. Him Achilles, still unsatisfied at heart,)

Hector dies while Achilles is still speaking. The sentence continues directly from Achilles’ threat that animals will devour his enemy’s body and that Patroclus’ spirit may take pleasure in it. Against that savage speech, the description “pitiable Hector” briefly directs our sympathy toward the dying man. Troy’s defender, who has just begged for his body to be returned to his family, can no longer plead or resist.

The Latin compresses Homer’s account of death into the plain statement that Hector “gave up his life.” In the *Iliad*, death covers Hector, and his soul flies from his body toward the realm of the dead, “lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth behind” (*Iliad* 22.361–363). That farewell gives weight to everything his death cuts short. The Latin omits the soul’s departure and lament, but “pitiable” preserves something of their sorrow.

The narrative immediately turns back to Achilles. Hector’s death has not satisfied his anger over the loss of Patroclus, the companion Hector killed. Homer introduces the coming outrage by saying that Achilles “devised shameful deeds against noble Hector” (*Iliad* 22.395). Here the emphasis falls instead on the avenger’s unsatisfied heart. Killing his enemy has not ended his desire to punish him: the unfinished sentence carries that desire forward toward Hector’s helpless body.
