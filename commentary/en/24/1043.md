### 1043–1045 (*Iliad* 24.515–516, 24.589–590, 24.579)

> 1043 His tandem precibus grandaeuum motus Achilles
> (By these prayers at last moved, the aged man Achilles)
>
> 1044 alleuat a terra corpusque exsangue parenti
> (raises from the ground, and the lifeless body to the father)
>
> 1045 reddidit Hectoreum. Post haec sua dona reportat
> (gave back, Hector’s. After this his gifts he carries back)

Priam’s appeal at last moves Achilles to pity. The conqueror who has taken Hector’s life now helps Hector’s aged father rise from the ground and restores his son’s body to him. The physical gesture answers Priam’s helpless posture at Achilles’ knees: instead of striking the old man, as Priam had invited him to do in his despair, Achilles raises him up. His victory is no longer expressed through violence, but through mercy.

The Latin brings together actions separated by a substantial stretch of narrative in Homer. In the *Iliad*, Achilles rises from his seat and takes Priam by the hand, pitying his white hair and beard (*Iliad* 24.515–516). The return of Hector’s body comes later, after further conversation and preparations. Achilles himself lifts the body onto a bier, and his companions help place it on the wagon (*Iliad* 24.589–590). Here that fuller sequence becomes a swift response to Priam’s plea: Achilles relents, raises the father, and returns the son.

The “gifts” Priam carries back are Hector’s body, now granted to him for burial, not the treasure he brought as ransom. In Homer, Achilles’ attendants unload and retain that ransom (*Iliad* 24.579). The Latin’s closing phrase instead keeps attention on what Priam has gained: not wealth restored, but the chance to take his son home and give him the funeral he has begged to provide.
