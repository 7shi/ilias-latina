### 1025–1026 (*Iliad* 24.483–484)

> 1025 Mirantur Danaum proceres, miratur et ipse
> (The chieftains of the Danaans marvel, and marvels too, himself,)
>
> 1026 Aeacides animum miseri senis; ille trementes
> (Aeacides, at the courage of the wretched old man; he, his trembling)

Priam has now reached the enemy camp. The Latin moves directly from his determination to go there unarmed to the astonishment his presence inspires, passing over the preparations and journey. The **Danaans** are the Greeks, and **Aeacides**, “descendant of Aeacus,” means Achilles, Aeacus’ grandson. Even the warrior who killed Hector marvels at the courage of Hector’s aged father. The helplessness that made Priam’s undertaking seem so desperate now gives it an unexpected dignity.

In Homer, Achilles is astonished “at the sight of godlike Priam,” while the others are also astonished and look at one another (*Iliad* 24.483–484). The Latin preserves their shared amazement but makes its cause explicit: they admire the old man’s courage. Homer’s “godlike Priam” becomes the “wretched old man,” shifting attention from his royal stature to his suffering and vulnerability. This continues the preceding passage’s emphasis on a father whose concern for his dead son overcomes concern for his own life. Priam has no force with which to compel Achilles to return Hector’s body; what he brings instead is the extraordinary resolve required to stand before his son’s killer.
