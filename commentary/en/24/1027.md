### 1027 (*Iliad* 24.477–478)

> 1027 affusus genibus tendens ad sidera palmas
> (palms, prostrate at his knees, stretching toward the stars,)

Priam throws himself down at Achilles’ knees and raises his trembling hands—the gesture begun in the preceding verse. The king of Troy places himself entirely at the mercy of the man who killed his son. Clasping someone’s knees was a traditional act of supplication: it made a bodily appeal for protection or pity where the suppliant had no power to demand either.

In Homer, Priam enters unnoticed, approaches Achilles, clasps his knees, and kisses his hands (*Iliad* 24.477–478). The astonishment described in the preceding passage follows these gestures. The Latin reverses that order, presenting the onlookers’ amazement before showing Priam at Achilles’ knees. It also replaces the kiss upon Achilles’ hands with palms stretched toward the stars. This gives the appeal the appearance of prayer, though Achilles remains the person whose mercy Priam needs. The old man’s courage and helplessness are inseparable: he has dared to approach his enemy only to humble himself completely before him.
