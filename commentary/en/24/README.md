The wretched Phrygians mourn Hector, and all Troy resounds with lamentation. Unhappy Hecuba pours out her laments and furrows her face with her nails, while Andromache tears the clothing from her breast, bereft of her great husband. Hector’s fall brings down the whole Phrygian cause and leaves his father’s pitiable old age without its protector. Neither his wife, his many children, nor the glory of his kingdom can restrain the stricken old king, Priam: forgetful of his own life, he goes alone and unarmed into the enemy camp.

The chieftains of the Danaans and Achilles, called Aeacides, marvel at the wretched old man’s courage. Prostrate at Achilles’ knees, Priam addresses him as the bravest of the Greek race, the enemy of his realms, and the man at whom the conquered Dardanian youth tremble. He begs Achilles to temper his cruelty with gentleness, pity a suppliant father, and accept gifts in exchange for his unhappy son’s body. If neither prayers nor gold can move Achilles, Priam asks to be killed himself, joining Hector in death. He urges the victor over Priam and the Dardanian realms to remember the common human lot and the changing fortunes of commanders. Moved at last, Achilles raises the aged king and returns Hector’s lifeless body.

Priam carries back his gifts, that is, Hector’s body, to his homeland and prepares the customary funeral rites. A pyre is built with twelve Greek bodies, horses, chariots, trumpets, shields, helmets, and missiles; Hector is laid upon it amid lamentation. The Ilian mothers loosen their hair and beat their breasts, seeing their own sons’ funerals in his, while the young men cry out as though Ilion itself were burning. Andromache tries to throw herself into the flames holding Astyanax, but the throng of her women snatches her away. She struggles until the fire subsides and the great leader becomes ashes.

The narrative ends with Hector’s cremation. The poet then ends the work itself, asking Calliope to halt her steps, end the labor and steer his vessel, which now holds the harbor and the goal of mighty Homer. He bids the accompanying band of the Pierides lower the sails and, crowned with laurel, lay down the lyres (whether Calliope herself or the band is meant is not certain), and invokes glorious Pallas and Phoebus for favor at the completion of his poetic course. This is the conclusion of the *Ilias Latina*, not a transition to another book.
