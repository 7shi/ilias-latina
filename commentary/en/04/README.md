While the chiefs contend, the almighty ruler of Olympus holds a divine council. Pandarus breaks the treaty by shooting at Menelaus: his arrow penetrates the iron-scaled tunic and wounds him. Menelaus, called Atrides, withdraws groaning to the camp, where the young Podalirius, trained in his father’s healing art, treats him with Paeonian herbs. He then goes again, the victor, into the fighting. Agamemnon’s anger arms the brave Pelasgians, and shared grief drives the whole army into battle.

The fighting becomes general, with heavy losses among both Trojans and Danaans. Mavors, the war god, resounds everywhere amid showers of missiles. Antilochus kills Thalysiades with his sword. Telamonian Ajax strikes down Anthemion’s son, who is pressing the Greeks, piercing his breast. Antiphus then hurls his spear at Aeacides—the designation here for Ajax—but misses and strikes Leucus in the groin; Leucus dies biting the grass. “Tireless Atrides,” stirred by his friend’s death, kills Democoon with a massive spear through the temples and then draws his sword; his opponent falls on his back. Pirous Imbrasides has meanwhile killed Amaryncides with a stone. As Pirous prepares to strip the dead youth, Thoas casts a spear through his shoulders and breast, leaving him face-down, bleeding and twitching on his own arms.

The book ends with battle still raging across the blood-soaked Dardanian plains. The Greeks are also called Danaans, Pelasgians and Achaeans. Neither side has secured victory: courage grows now among the Trojans, now among the Achaeans, as both continue striving to prevail.
