### 357–359 (*Iliad* 4.450–451, 4.456)

> 357 inque uicem Troumque cadunt Danaumque cateruae
> (and in turn companies of Trojans and Danaans fall,)
>
> 358 nec requies datur ulla uiris: sonat undique Mauors
> (and the men are given no rest: Mavors resounds everywhere,)
>
> 359 telorumque uolant cunctis e partibus imbres.
> (and showers of missiles fly from every side.)

The fighting continues without pause, with losses among both the Trojans and the Danaans—the Greeks besieging Troy. The falling companies extend the preceding picture of bodies scattered across the fields: the dead are not merely the aftermath of battle, but the result of killing still going on. Missiles converge from every direction, leaving the men no respite.

Homer mingles the groans of the dying with the triumphant cries of their killers (*Iliad* 4.450–451), then sums up the encounter as “the uproar and toil” of the armies clashing (*Iliad* 4.456). The Latin retains the shared suffering and surrounding noise but gives no separate voice to victor or victim. Instead, Mavors, the Roman war god also called Mars, embodies the din of battle; this does not describe a god personally entering the fight. The final shower of missiles adds an image without a direct counterpart in the corresponding Homeric lines. War seems to fill the air like a violent storm, enclosing everyone on the battlefield.
