### 380–388 (*Iliad* 4.527–528, 4.531, 4.536, 4.451, 4.543–544)

> 380 desuper hasta uenit dextra librata Thoantis
> (From above comes a spear hurled by the right hand of Thoas)
>
> 381 perque uiri scapulas animosaque pectora transit;
> (and passes through the man’s shoulders and valiant breast;)

> (he falls on his face, and hot blood)

> 383 ore uomit stratusque super sua palpitat arma.

>
> 384 Sanguine Dardanii manabant undique campi,

> 386 immixtis ardens amborum exercitus armis
> (blazing, the army of both sides, their arms intermingled,)
>
> 387 et modo Troianis uirtus, modo crescit Achiuis
> (and now courage grows among the Trojans, now among the Achaeans,)
>
> 388 laetaque per uarios petitur uictoria casus.
> (and glad victory is sought through changing fortunes.)

Pirous has scarcely killed Diores and begun to reach for his spoils before he becomes a victim himself. Thoas, a Greek leader from Aetolia in western Greece, strikes him down. The sudden arrival of the spear interrupts the action carried over from the preceding passage: Pirous’ eagerness for plunder has left him no time to enjoy his success.

As with the death of Diores, the Latin reduces a sequence of wounds to one decisive blow. In Homer, Thoas strikes Pirous in the chest above the nipple, lodging the spear-point in his lung. He then approaches, pulls out the spear, and kills him with a sword-thrust to the belly. Here the spear passes through shoulders and breast, and the dying man collapses on his own arms, vomiting blood and twitching. The Latin omits the separate finishing stroke but supplies its own vivid account of the body’s last movements.

Homer continues the struggle over the dead: Thoas tries to strip Pirous, but the Thracians defend their comrade’s body and drive him back. Diores and Pirous are left lying beside one another. The Latin leaves out that attempted plundering and resistance, moving directly from Pirous’ death to a view of the whole battlefield. His blood becomes part of a landscape drenched in blood: the Dardanian plains are the plains around Troy, named for Dardanus, an ancestor of its royal house.

The closing lines gather the individual encounters into the larger, still undecided battle. The Achaeans are the Greeks. Their intermingled weapons suggest close fighting in which neither army has gained a clear advantage. Homer closes this part of the battle by stressing the many men of both sides stretched face down beside one another in the dust. The Latin instead ends with renewed energy and fluctuating courage. Both armies continue to seek a glad victory, though the deaths just narrated have shown how quickly a warrior’s success can turn into his destruction.
