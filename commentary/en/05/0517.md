### 517–518 (*Iliad* 5.541–542, 5.561)

> 517 desilit et ualido Crethona\<que> comminus ictu
> (leaps down, and with a mighty blow at close quarters Crethon)
>
> 518 Orsilochumque ferit, quorum post funera uictus
> (and Orsilochus he strikes; after whose deaths, overcome,)

Aeneas leaps from the chariot and resumes fighting on foot. The loss of his driver has not disabled him: he now kills Crethon and Orsilochus, two Greek warriors. The swift movement from his groan over the charioteer to this deadly attack shows how quickly grief gives way to action on the battlefield.

In Homer, Crethon and Orsilochus are brothers, sons of Diocles, and are introduced as outstanding Greek fighters (*Iliad* 5.541–542). Homer then pauses to describe their family and their youthful promise before returning to their deaths. The Latin omits that fuller account, reducing their encounter with Aeneas to a brief, forceful stroke of narrative. After borrowing the charioteer’s death from a later battle, it has returned to Aeneas’ exploits in *Iliad* 5.

The unfinished phrase “after whose deaths, overcome” begins another turn in the action. In Homer, Menelaus, the Greek king whose wife Helen was taken to Troy, sees the fallen brothers and feels pity for them (*Iliad* 5.561). Their deaths thus prepare a response from the Greek side, just as the driver’s death prompted Aeneas’ renewed attack.
