### 434–435 (*Iliad* 5.76, 5.79–80)

> 434 Eurypylus gladio uenientem Hypsenora fundit
> (Eurypylus strikes down the approaching Hypsenor with his sword)
>
> 435 et pariter uita iuuenem spoliauit et armis.
> (and strips the young man of life and armor alike.)

The fearsome warrior introduced at the end of the preceding passage is **Eurypylus**, another of the Greek commanders. His victim, **Hypsenor**, fights on the Trojan side. The sequence of brief Greek victories continues, with a single sword-stroke followed by the victor’s seizure of the fallen man’s armor.

In Homer, Hypsenor is the son of Dolopion, a priest of the river-god Scamander whom the Trojans honor like a god. The Latin again leaves out the family background that gives the dead warrior a place in Trojan society. It also changes the movement of the encounter: here Hypsenor approaches Eurypylus, whereas in Homer Eurypylus pursues him as he flees and strikes his shoulder, severing his arm (*Iliad* 5.77–83). That graphic wound disappears into the simpler statement that Eurypylus strikes him down. The final line then joins death and the loss of armor in one compact expression: the young man is deprived at once of his life and his warrior’s equipment.
