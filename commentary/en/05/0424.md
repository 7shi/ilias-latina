### 424–428 (*Iliad* 5.38–41)

> 424 Nec minus in Teucros armis furit alter Atrides
> (No less fiercely does the other Atrides rage in arms against the Teucrians,)
>
> 425 insequiturque acies et ferro funera miscet.
> (pursuing their ranks and wreaking carnage with his sword.)
>
> 426 Obuius huic fatis occurrit ductus iniquis
> (Driven by cruel fate, there comes into his path)
>
> 427 infelix Odius, quem uastae cuspidis ictu
> (unhappy Odius, whom, with a blow from his massive spearhead,)
>
> 428 sternit et ingenti scapulas transuerberat hasta.
> (he lays low, driving his huge spear through his shoulders.)

The fighting now shifts from Diomedes to **Agamemnon**, commander of the Greek army. He is “the other Atrides”: like his brother Menelaus, he is a son of Atreus. The **Teucrians** are the Trojans, whose ranks he pursues with a fury matching Diomedes’ own. His victim, **Odius**, is identified in Homer as the leader of the Halizones, a people fighting on the Trojan side. The Latin leaves out his command and origin, presenting him simply as an unfortunate warrior brought into Agamemnon’s path by a hostile fate.

The killing retains the force of Homer’s spear-thrust but condenses its circumstances. In the *Iliad*, Agamemnon knocks Odius from his chariot: as Odius turns to flee, the spear strikes him “in the back, between the shoulders,” and passes through his chest (*Iliad* 5.39–41). Here the chariot and the explicit flight disappear, though the thrust through the shoulders remains. Instead, the Latin adds a broader picture of Agamemnon spreading slaughter through the enemy ranks, then singles out the doomed man who crosses his path. After Idaeus’ narrow escape, Odius shows the other outcome of meeting an overpowering Greek warrior: he is struck down before any resistance can be described.
