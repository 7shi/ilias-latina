### 500–503 (*Iliad* 5.161–162)

> 500 Vt Libycus cum forte leo procul agmina uidit
> (Just as a Libyan lion, when by chance it sees in the distance)
>
> 501 laeta boum passim uirides errare per herbas,
> (sleek herds of cattle wandering here and there through the green grass,)
>
> 502 attollit ceruice iubas sitiensque cruoris
> (raises the mane on its neck and, thirsting for blood,)
>
> 503 in mediam erecto contendit pectore turbam,
> (with chest held high rushes into the midst of the herd,)

Agamemnon’s charge into the enemy ranks is now pictured as a lion’s rush among grazing cattle. “Libyan” places the animal in North Africa, a region familiar to Roman readers as a home of lions. The cattle are scattered and peacefully feeding; against their ease, the lion’s raised mane and thirst for blood make its approach especially threatening. The comparison presents the Greek commander as a powerful predator and the opposing soldiers as a herd exposed to his attack. These verses carry the lion into their midst but leave the outcome suspended.

The Latin draws this image from an earlier episode in Homer, where it describes Diomedes attacking two Trojan warriors rather than Agamemnon rallying his army. Homer’s lion leaps among cattle and “breaks the neck of a calf or cow” grazing in a wooded pasture (*Iliad* 5.161–162). Here the emphasis shifts from the killing itself to the predator’s approach: it spots the herd at a distance, raises its mane, and charges. The North African setting and these bodily gestures are additions to the Homeric comparison. They prolong the moment before contact while sustaining the fierce momentum of Agamemnon’s assault.
