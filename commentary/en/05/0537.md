### 537 (*Iliad* 5.888–898)

> 537 saucius et magni genitoris iurgia suffert.
> (wounded, and endures the rebukes of his mighty father.)

Mavors, the war-god Mars, receives a rebuke rather than the vindication he seeks. His “mighty father” is Jupiter, the king of heaven, whom Homer calls Zeus. Driven from battle by Pallas, Mars must now submit to his father’s anger: his physical injury is followed by humiliation within the divine household.

The Latin condenses a fierce but revealing speech into this single line. In Homer, Zeus tells Ares—the Greek name for Mars—to stop whining and declares, “You are the most hateful to me of the gods who inhabit Olympus,” because he always delights in strife, wars, and battles (*Iliad* 5.890–891). The god who promotes violence finds little sympathy when he suffers from it himself. Zeus also blames Ares’ uncontrollable temper on his mother Hera, the goddess the Romans call Juno, whose resistance to his authority he can scarcely overcome.

Yet Homer’s rebuke ends with a qualification that the Latin leaves unspoken. Zeus will not allow his son to remain in pain for long, precisely because he is his son; another god so destructive would long since have been cast beneath the heavenly gods (*Iliad* 5.895–898). The Latin retains the father–son relationship but omits this reluctant protection, leaving the emphasis on the wounded war-god’s submission to a greater power.
