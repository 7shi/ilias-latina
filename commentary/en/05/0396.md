### 396–404 (*Iliad* 5.136–143, 5.93–94, 5.9–12)

> 396 Ille, boum ueluti uiso grege saeua leaena,
> (He—as a fierce lioness, on sight of a herd of cattle,)
>
> 397 quam stimulat ieiuna fames, ruit agmina contra
> (driven by ravenous hunger, charges against their ranks)
>
> 398 et prostrata necat uesano corpora dente,
> (and kills her fallen victims with frenzied teeth,)
>
> 399 sic ruit in medios hostes Calydonius heros,
> (so rushes into the midst of the enemy the Calydonian hero,)
>
> 400 uirginis armigerae monitis et numine tutus.
> (protected by the counsel and divine power of the armed maiden.)
>
> 401 Conuersi dant terga Phryges, fugientibus ille
> (The Phrygians turn their backs; he presses on them as they flee)
>
> 402 instat et exstructos morientum calcat aceruos.
> (and tramples the heaped-up piles of dying men.)
>
> 403 Dumque ferit sternitque uiros, uidet ecce Daretis
> (And while he strikes and fells men, he sees—look—Dares’)
>
> 404 aduerso stantes furibundus in agmine natos,
> (sons standing in the opposing ranks, in his fury,)

With Pallas now sustaining him, Diomedes drives the enemy into flight. He is the **“Calydonian hero”** through his father Tydeus, who came from Calydon in Aetolia, a region of western Greece. The “armed maiden” is Pallas, whose counsel and divine protection reinforce the strength she has just given him. **Phrygians** here means the Trojans: the Latin uses the name of another people of Asia Minor for the defenders of Troy.

The lioness makes the inequality of the struggle stark. Diomedes faces not opponents capable of resisting him, but a herd exposed to a hungry predator. Homer also compares him to a lion, but gives the animal a different reason for its fury: a shepherd has wounded it without subduing it, only increasing its strength, and then retreats into shelter while the sheep scatter and huddle together (*Iliad* 5.136–143). That comparison belongs to Diomedes’ renewed attack after he himself has been wounded. The Latin brings it forward, before that wound, and substitutes hunger for the wound as the animal’s driving force. Its lioness attacks cattle rather than sheep, and the killing is more explicit. The added picture of Diomedes trampling heaps of dying men carries that savagery directly onto the battlefield.

The narrative now narrows from the rout of an army to an encounter with two individual opponents. **Dares** is their father; Homer introduces him as a wealthy Trojan priest of the smith-god Hephaestus and names his sons Phegeus and Idaeus, both skilled fighters (*Iliad* 5.9–12). Their appearance here shows how freely the abridgment rearranges Homer’s sequence: an encounter from the opening of Diomedes’ great battle episode now follows images drawn from his later fighting. Rather than pausing to introduce the family, the Latin lets Diomedes catch sight of the brothers amid the slaughter, poised for his next attack.
