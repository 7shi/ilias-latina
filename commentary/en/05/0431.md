### 431 (*Iliad* 5.49)

> 431 et Strophio genitum Stygias demittit ad umbras.
> (and sends the son of Strophius down to the Stygian shades.)

Still rejoicing after killing Phaestus, **Idomeneus** claims another victim. The unnamed “son of Strophius” is **Scamandrius**, a warrior on the Trojan side. In Homer, however, it is Menelaus, not Idomeneus, who kills him. The Latin gathers the two deaths into a single commander’s victorious advance, dropping Scamandrius’s own name and Homer’s description of him as “skilled in hunting” (*Iliad* 5.49).

The “Stygian shades” are the shadowy realm of the dead, associated with the Styx, a river of the underworld. Sending someone there is an elevated way of saying that he is killed: the Latin passes directly from the first victory to the next death, without describing the blow or the victim’s fall.
