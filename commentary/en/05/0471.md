### 471 (*Iliad* 5.370–374)

> 471 atque ibi sidereae queritur sua uulnera matri.
> (and there complains of her wounds to her heavenly mother.)

Having fled the battlefield, Venus turns to her mother Dione, a goddess whom Homer names as the mother of Aphrodite, the Greek counterpart of Venus. The warrior’s victim is now a daughter seeking comfort: the scene moves from the violence of battle to the shelter of a divine family.

The Latin condenses a tender encounter into a single report of Venus’s complaint. In Homer, Aphrodite falls into her mother’s lap; Dione embraces her, strokes her with her hand, and asks who has hurt her (*Iliad* 5.370–374). These gestures disappear, but the mother’s presence preserves the sense of refuge. Dione’s question also carries a revealing assumption: she asks which of the heavenly gods has mistreated her daughter. The reader knows that the attacker was instead the mortal Diomedes, whose assault has crossed the boundary that Dione takes for granted.
