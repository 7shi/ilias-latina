### 460–463 (*Iliad* 5.302–305, 5.309–310)

> 460 saxum ingens medio quod forte iacebat in agro,
> (a huge rock that happened to lie in the middle of the field,)
>
> 461 bis seni quod uix iuuenes tellure mouerent,
> (which twelve young men could scarcely move from the ground,)
>
> 462 sustulit et magno conamine misit in hostem.
> (he lifted and with great effort hurled at his enemy.)
>
> 463 Ille ruit prostratus humi cum fortibus armis,
> (The other fell, laid low on the ground with his stout armor,)

Unable to find an opening for his sword, Diomedes turns to a weapon lying ready on the battlefield. The sentence begun in the preceding passage now reaches its decisive action: he lifts the enormous stone and throws it at Aeneas. The evenly matched exchange of blows ends abruptly in a display of overwhelming strength. Aeneas falls despite the armor that had helped him withstand his opponent.

The Latin keeps Homer’s basic action but greatly enlarges the stone. In the *Iliad*, two men “such as mortals are now” could not carry it, yet Diomedes handles it easily, alone (*Iliad* 5.302–304). Homer contrasts the strength of the ancient heroes with that of people in his own day. The Latin drops that explicit contrast and instead measures the feat against twelve young men. Its Diomedes performs an even more extraordinary feat, though “with great effort” rather than Homer’s ease.

Aeneas’ collapse is also compressed. Homer specifies a blow to the hip, then shows him sinking to one knee and bracing himself against the earth with one hand as darkness covers his eyes (*Iliad* 5.305, 5.309–310). The Latin reduces this sequence to a single image: the fully armed warrior brought crashing to the ground. He has been struck down, not declared dead.
