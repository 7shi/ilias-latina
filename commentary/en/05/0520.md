### 520–522 (*Iliad* 5.580, 5.629–630)

> 520 Antilochique Mydon. Post hos Iouis inclita proles
> (and Mydon by Antilochus’. After these, Jupiter’s renowned son)
>
> 521 Sarpedon bellum funestaque proelia miscet.
> (Sarpedon stirs up war and deadly battles.)
>
> 522 Quem contra infelix non aequis dimicat armis
> (Against him, ill-fated, with unequal arms fights)

Mydon’s death completes the brief account of Greek success begun in the preceding verse. He is the charioteer of the Paphlagonian commander whom Menelaus has just killed; his killer, Antilochus, is a Greek warrior and a son of Nestor, the army’s elderly counsellor. Homer gives a vivid account of Antilochus striking Mydon with a stone and then attacking him with his sword. The Latin reduces that struggle to two names, borrowing the verb of falling and the arms from the preceding verse.

The focus then shifts to Sarpedon, a son of Jupiter—the god whom Homer calls Zeus—and commander of the Lycians, allies of Troy from southwestern Asia Minor. His divine parentage announces a formidable opponent. The Latin passes over the intervening fighting, including the Greeks’ retreat before Hector and the war god, to bring him directly into action.

The ill-fated warrior who faces Sarpedon is Tlepolemus, a Greek leader and son of Hercules, whose name comes in the next verse. Homer says that “mighty fate drove him against godlike Sarpedon” (*Iliad* 5.629). The Latin preserves that sense of impending disaster through “ill-fated” and the “unequal arms.” These words need not imply inferior equipment: they present the encounter as ominously uneven before its course has been described.
