### 737–740 (*Iliad* 10.532, 10.540–545, 10.575–579)

> 737 ad classes redeunt, quos Nestoris accipit aetas
> (fleet they return; aged Nestor hears them)
>
> 738 ac recipit portis. Postquam sua castra tenebant,
> (and welcomes them through the gates. Once they were in their own camp,)
>
> 739 facta duci referunt: laudat Pelopeius heros,
> (they report their deeds to the leader; the Pelopeian hero praises,)
>
> 740 fessaque iucundae tradunt sua membra quieti.
> (and they surrender their weary limbs to welcome rest.)

Ulysses and Diomedes return safely to the Greek ships with the captured horses. The gates mark their passage out of enemy territory and back into the security of their own camp. Nestor, the elderly Greek king and counsellor, is the first to hear them approaching. In Homer, he recognizes the sound of horses’ hooves before the returning warriors come into view (*Iliad* 10.532). The Latin preserves his alertness but makes him the central figure in their welcome, condensing the joyful greetings and handclasps of the assembled Greeks.

Calling him “aged Nestor” emphasizes the meeting between an older generation and the warriors whose exploits it welcomes and honors. The report then passes to the supreme Greek commander, Agamemnon. He is the “Pelopeian hero,” so called because he is a grandson of Pelops. Where Homer has Nestor question Odysseus about the extraordinary horses, the Latin moves directly from the warriors’ report to their commander’s praise. Their dangerous private expedition thus ends in public recognition.

The final rest closes the night’s action with a simple contrast between exertion and ease. Homer gives the return a fuller conclusion: the two men wash away sweat, bathe, anoint themselves with oil, and sit down to a meal, pouring a wine offering to Athena, the goddess who has supported their mission (*Iliad* 10.575–579). The Latin replaces these details of refreshment and thanksgiving with the welcome repose of exhausted bodies.
