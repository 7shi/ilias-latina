### 730–733 (*Iliad* 10.471, 10.494–497, 10.483–488, 10.498–501)

> 730 intrant atque ipsum somno uinoque sepultum
> (they enter, and him, buried in sleep and wine,)
>
> 731 obtruncant spoliantque uirum fusosque per herbam
> (they cut down and strip the man, and, stretched out on the grass,)
>
> 732 exanimant socios. Tum tristi caede peracta
> (his companions they kill. Then, when the grim slaughter is done,)
>
> 733 praeda umeros onerant, multo et candore nitentes
> (they load their shoulders with plunder, and, gleaming with much whiteness,)

Ulysses and Diomedes enter the tents of Rhesus, the Thracian king newly arrived to help defend Troy. The information extracted from Dolon now enables them to attack men who have no chance to resist. Rhesus is “buried in sleep and wine”: the phrase presents him as utterly insensible before the killing begins. Homer describes the Thracians as exhausted; the Latin adds wine to the explanation of their helplessness.

The Latin also changes the order and responsibility for the slaughter. In Homer, Diomedes kills twelve Thracians before reaching Rhesus, his thirteenth victim, while Odysseus assists him and secures the horses. Here the king dies first, his companions follow, and both Greeks are presented as killers. The account leaves out Homer’s comparison of Diomedes to a lion attacking unguarded sheep or goats, as well as the victims’ groans and the blood reddening the ground. It also omits Rhesus’ terrifying dream, in which his approaching killer looms over him through the intervention of Athena, the goddess supporting the raid. What remains is a swift succession of killing, stripping, and carrying off booty.

“Grim slaughter” briefly colors the otherwise brisk account. The Greeks’ success depends on the complete vulnerability of their sleeping enemies. They then turn from bodies to prizes: plunder burdens their shoulders, while the unfinished phrase about brilliant whiteness looks ahead to Rhesus’ horses, the most conspicuous spoil of the expedition.
