Late in the night, with a third of it still remaining, Diomedes, called “the Aetolian hero,” leaves the Greek camp at the command of the Danaans. He chooses Ulysses, “the companion of Diomedes,” to go with him and search out the Trojans’ confidence, their plans, and the forces they are preparing for battle.

On their way, they meet Dolon, whom the Trojan youth have sent to examine the strength of the Danaans and report the counsels of their leaders and common people. Ulysses sees him from afar, and the two Greeks hide behind thick bushes until he has run past them, so that he cannot easily return to his own camp. They then spring out, seize him as he tries to escape, and threaten him with steel and hand. Dolon, called “the Trojan Eumediades,” begs for his life. He explains that mighty Troy has promised him Achilles’ chariot if it captures the Greeks’ wealth. Invoking the gods, the sea, and the waves of dark Dis, he offers to set out the plan of King Priam and the affairs of the Phrygian people in exchange for his life. Once the Greeks have learned what Troy is preparing, however, they sever his throat.

Diomedes and Ulysses next enter the tents of Rhesus. They kill and strip the man, who lies buried in sleep and wine, and kill his companions stretched out on the grass. They load themselves with plunder and seize the gleaming white Thracian horses, which neither Eurus, the east wind, nor a flying arrow could outrun.

At first light they return to the Argive fleet. Aged Nestor hears them and receives them at the gates. Back in their own camp, they report their deeds to their leader, and “the Pelopeian hero,” Agamemnon, praises them. The book ends with the two warriors giving their weary limbs over to welcome rest.
