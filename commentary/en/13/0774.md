### 774 (*Iliad* 13.384–393)

> 774 Idomenei dextra cadit Asius; Hector atrocem
> (Asius falls by the right hand of Idomeneus; Hector the fierce)

The renewed Greek resistance now takes a concrete form: Idomeneus, the leader of the Greek forces from Crete, kills Asius, a commander fighting on the Trojan side. “By the right hand” identifies Idomeneus as the warrior who strikes the fatal blow.

The Latin reduces a vivid encounter to a single brief statement. In Homer, Asius approaches on foot, with his chariot and horses close behind him, intending to kill Idomeneus. But Idomeneus strikes first, driving his spear through Asius’ throat beneath the chin. Asius falls like a great tree felled by woodcutters for shipbuilding, and lies stretched before his horses, groaning and clutching the bloodstained dust (*Iliad* 13.384–393). The abridgment preserves the victor and the victim but leaves out the threatened attack, the wound, and the image that gives the fallen warrior such imposing stature.

The unfinished clause turns immediately to Hector, Troy’s foremost defender. Its final adjective, “fierce,” belongs to Amphimachus, the Greek warrior whose death at Hector’s hands is told in the continuation. Homer places that death earlier than Asius’ fall. By rearranging the encounters, the Latin moves directly from a Greek success to a Trojan one, sustaining the preceding picture of a battle fiercely contested on both sides.
