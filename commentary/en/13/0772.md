### 772–773 (*Iliad* 13.43–45)

> 772 Neptunus uires Danais animumque ministrat:
> (Neptune supplies the Danaans with strength and courage:)
>
> 773 pugna ingens oritur, furit istinc hostis et illinc.
> (A great battle breaks out; on this side and on that the enemy rages.)

After the Greeks’ panicked retreat to their ships, divine help restores their capacity to resist. Neptune, the Roman name for the sea god whom the Greeks called Poseidon, strengthens the Danaans—the Greeks—in both body and spirit. Their situation remains dangerous: the Trojans have breached the camp’s defenses and are pressing toward the ships. But the fighting is now fierce on both sides, rather than simply a triumphant advance by the attackers.

The Latin compresses the god’s intervention into its immediate effect. In the *Iliad*, Poseidon comes from the depths of the sea and encourages the Greeks, assuming the appearance and tireless voice of Calchas, a seer accompanying their army (*Iliad* 13.43–45). Here neither his arrival nor his disguise is described; the reader is told directly who renews the defenders’ strength. The following verse adds a broad picture of battle without a direct counterpart in those Homeric lines. Its balanced description makes both armies threatening: each is the raging enemy seen from the other side. Divine support has renewed Greek resistance, but it has not yet settled the struggle.
