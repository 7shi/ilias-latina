### 792–793 (*Iliad* 15.1–3)

> 792 pulsa metu uallumque et muros aggere saeptos
> (Driven by fear, over both the rampart and the walls fenced with an embankment)
>
> 793 transiliunt, alii fossas uoluuntur in ipsas.
> (they leap, others roll into the very ditches.)

Before the renewed Trojan assault, the battlefield breaks into panicked flight. Who flees is not stated: the words “driven by fear” probably describe a noun that stood in the lost verse. Since the Greeks flee to their ships in the verses that follow, the fugitives may be Greeks, driven back toward the fortified camp that shelters their ships. Plessis prints a verse in the gap, *Instaurantque manus, cedit Pelopea juventus*, “and they renew the fight; the youth of Pelops gives way,” and both his index and Vollmer’s take *Pelopea iuventus* as the Greeks. The defences become obstacles in the retreat: some manage to leap across, while others roll into the ditches. The contrast makes the rout vivid without pausing over individual warriors.

The Latin here draws on an earlier moment in Homer’s narrative, before the divine intervention that restores the Trojans’ strength. At the opening of Book 15, it is the Trojans who have fled across the Greeks’ defensive ditch and stakes. Many have been killed by the Greeks, and the survivors stop beside their chariots (*Iliad* 15.1–3). That retreat belongs to the period when Hector is still disabled and his army is losing ground.

Placed after the renewed Trojan advance, this image of flight may have a different direction: if the fugitives are Greeks, the attackers are now driving them back through their own defences. It also changes the emphasis. Homer mentions deaths in the pursuit and the survivors’ halt; the Latin concentrates on the obstacles themselves, adding the striking image of men rolling into the ditches. The result is a compressed scene of confusion and fear, carrying the fighting toward the Greek ships.
