### 937–938 (*Iliad* 22.136–138)

> 937 pertimuit clausisque fugit sua moenia circum
> (He was terrified and, with them shut, flees around his own walls,)
>
> 938 infelix portis, sequitur Nereius heros:
> (ill-fated, the gates; the Nereian hero follows:)

Hector’s determination gives way to terror. The man who would not be persuaded to retreat now runs beneath the walls of the city he has defended. The shut gates sharpen his isolation: Troy is close, but he remains outside, exposed to his pursuer. Calling him “ill-fated” casts pity over his flight and points toward its fatal outcome.

The Latin keeps Homer’s sudden reversal from resolve to panic, but compresses the action. In the *Iliad*, Hector cannot bear to stand his ground and leaves the gates behind him; Achilles springs after him, trusting in his speed. Here the gates are explicitly shut, and Hector is already fleeing around the walls, anticipating the extended pursuit that follows in Homer.

The “Nereian hero” is Achilles. The title recalls his mother Thetis, a sea-goddess and daughter of the sea-god Nereus. It identifies the relentless pursuer through his divine ancestry, contrasting with the brief, compassionate description of Hector as “ill-fated.”
