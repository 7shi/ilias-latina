### 939–943 (*Iliad* 22.199–201)

> 939 in somnis ueluti, cum pectora terruit ira,
> (As in dreams, when anger has terrified the heart,)
>
> 940 hic cursu super insequitur, fugere ille uidetur,
> (one races in pursuit, the other seems to flee,)
>
> 941 festinantque ambo, gressum labor ipse moratur,
> (and both hurry, but their very effort slows their steps,)
>
> 942 alternis poterant insistere coepta periclis,
> (they could press on in the race, each in turn in mortal danger,)
>
> 943 nec requies aderat, timor undique concitat iras.
> (and there was no rest: fear on every side rouses their anger.)

Achilles pursues Hector around Troy’s walls, but the chase seems unable to reach its conclusion. The dream comparison captures a familiar helplessness: pursuer and fugitive strain to run, yet their bodies will not answer their urgency. Their effort becomes an obstacle rather than a means of escape or victory. Neither can rest, and neither can end the pursuit.

The Latin preserves Homer’s central image while giving it a more agitated emotional character. In the *Iliad*, the comparison turns on the frustration shared by both runners: “As in a dream one cannot catch someone fleeing, and the fugitive cannot escape his pursuer, so Achilles could not catch Hector on foot, nor could Hector escape” (*Iliad* 22.199–201). Here the runners’ exertion itself slows them, while anger and fear surround the action. The swift pursuit described just before this passage thus takes on the oppressive quality of a nightmare.

The closing lines move beyond Homer’s comparison to present the chase as a danger to both men. Hector fears being caught and killed; Achilles, too, is drawn into a contest in which his opponent threatens his life. This does not make their positions equal—Hector remains the fugitive—but it binds them together in mounting distress. Fear does not merely drive one man away from the other: it feeds their anger and keeps the deadly contest going.
