### 836–838 (*Iliad* 17.132–137)

> 836 Vindicat exstincti corpus Telamonius Aiax
> (Telamonian Ajax defends the dead man’s body)
>
> 837 oppositoque tegit clipeo. Priameia pubes
> (and covers it with his shield held before it. The youth of Priam)
>
> 838 laetitia exsultat, Danai sua uulnera maerent.
> (exult with joy; the Danaans mourn their wounds.)

Patroclus is dead, but his body remains on the battlefield and needs protection. Ajax, the son of Telamon and one of the strongest Greek warriors, steps forward to defend it. He is not Achilles, whose armor Patroclus wore into battle, but another champion of the same army. His intervention shifts the story from Patroclus’ fatal encounter with Hector to the struggle over the fallen man’s body. Hector’s victory has not left the Trojans in undisputed possession of the field.

The Latin preserves the central image of Homer’s account: Ajax standing over Patroclus behind a broad, protective shield. It leaves out the extended comparison that gives this stance its emotional force. In the *Iliad*, Ajax resembles a lion guarding its cubs when hunters encounter it in the woods, gathering its strength and lowering its brow threateningly (*Iliad* 17.132–137). Here, that fierce protectiveness is compressed into two actions: defending and covering.

In place of the lion’s threatening watchfulness, the passage closes with the opposing emotions of the armies. “The youth of Priam” are the Trojan warriors, named after Priam, their king and Hector’s father; the Danaans are the Greeks. This contrast between Trojan rejoicing and Greek suffering has no direct counterpart in the Homeric lines rendered here. It broadens the scene from one warrior’s defense of a body to the consequences of Patroclus’ death for both sides.
