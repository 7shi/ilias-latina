### 65–66 (*Iliad* 1.309–312, 1.430–439)

> 65 multaque dona super. Quam cunctis notus Vlixes
> (and many gifts besides. Her Ulysses, known to all,)
>
> 66 impositam puppi patrias deuexit ad arces
> (put aboard a ship, carried to her native city,)

The return announced in the preceding lines now receives its brief account of the journey. Agamemnon authorizes Chryseis’ release, but Ulysses—the Latin form of the name Odysseus—takes her home. He is one of the leading Greek commanders, especially distinguished by his intelligence and resourcefulness. Here, however, “known to all” emphasizes his fame rather than the cunning suggested by Homer’s description of him as “Odysseus of many counsels” (*Iliad* 1.311).

The “many gifts besides” complete the account of what Agamemnon sends with Chryseis. In Homer, the ship carries a hecatomb, a substantial sacrificial offering for Apollo, as well as the rescued daughter. The Latin leaves the gifts unspecified, but they belong to the same effort to repair the offense against the god and end the plague.

Chryseis’ destination is Chryse, the home of her father, Apollo’s priest. Homer describes the expedition in practical detail: twenty rowers are chosen, the passengers and offerings embark, and at their destination the crew lower the sails and mast, row into harbor, and secure the ship before unloading. All this becomes a single swift movement from captivity to home. The Latin retains the essential human action—the woman’s safe return—and names the commander entrusted with carrying it out.
