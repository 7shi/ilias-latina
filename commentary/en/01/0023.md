### 23–26 (*Iliad* 1.22–25, 1.32, 1.112–115)

> 23 Myrmidones reddique patri Chryseida censent.
> (the Myrmidons urge that Chryseis be returned to her father.)
>
> 24 Sed negat Atrides Chrysenque excedere castris
> (But Atrides refuses, and Chryses to leave the camp)
>
> 25 despecta pietate iubet: ferus ossibus imis
> (he orders, scorning sacred duty: fierce, deep within his bones,)
>
> 26 haeret amor spernitque preces damnosa libido.
> (love clings, and ruinous lust spurns the prayers.)

The soldiers won over by Chryses’ tears now support his request: his captive daughter, Chryseis, should be restored to him. The Latin calls them Myrmidons. The name belongs properly to the men of Achilles, but in Homer it is all the other Greeks who approve the priest’s appeal and urge acceptance of his ransom, and the poet seems to use it here for the Greek army at large, as he does again later for Greeks who are not Achilles’ men. The essential contrast remains the same: the father finds sympathy among the soldiers, but not in Atrides—Agamemnon—the king who holds his daughter.

Agamemnon’s dismissal is sharply compressed. Homer gives him a threatening speech, warning Chryses not to return and declaring that his daughter will grow old in captivity. Here the command to leave the camp stands for the whole rejection. The narrator’s judgment replaces much of the king’s speech: Agamemnon disregards the obligations that should restrain him. He shows neither compassion for a father nor respect for a priest.

The final image explains that refusal through desire. Love has lodged in the king’s very bones, too deeply for the father’s prayers to dislodge it. This develops the earlier description of Apollo sending a destructive passion into his heart. It also brings forward an explanation that Homer reserves for a later confrontation, when Agamemnon admits that he wants to keep the captive at home and prefers her even to his wife, Clytemnestra (*Iliad* 1.112–115). The Latin turns that admission into an immediate verdict on his conduct. His attachment is not presented as tender affection but as a consuming, ruinous appetite: the king’s determination to possess one woman overrides another person’s suffering and exposes his army to disaster.
