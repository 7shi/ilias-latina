### 88–92 (*Iliad* 1.502–505, 1.508, 1.506–507)

> 88 "Pro nato ueni genetrix en ad tua supplex
> (“For my son I have come, his mother, here as a suppliant before your)
>
> 89 numina, summe parens; ulciscere meque meumque
> (divine power, supreme father; avenge me and my own)
>
> 90 corpus ab Atrida, quodsi permittitur illi
> (flesh upon Atrides. For if he is allowed)
>
> 91 ut flammas impune mei uiolarit Achillis,
> (to violate my Achilles’ love with impunity,)
>
> 92 turpiter occiderit superata libidine uirtus."
> (valor will have fallen shamefully, overcome by lust.”)

Still at Jupiter’s knees, Thetis presents her son’s injury as her own. Achilles is her “own flesh”: the demand to avenge mother and son makes their bond almost bodily. “Atrides,” meaning “son of Atreus,” is Agamemnon, the commander of the Greek army. By taking Briseis, the captive woman awarded to Achilles as a prize, he has insulted the warrior whose prowess has helped sustain the Greek war effort.

The Latin makes this appeal more directly a demand for vengeance than it is in Homer. There Thetis reminds Zeus, the Greek counterpart of Jupiter, of her past services and asks him to “honor my son,” whose life is destined to be exceptionally short (*Iliad* 1.503–505). Here those grounds for the appeal disappear. The mother’s distress and her identification with her son carry the plea instead.

The language of Achilles’ “love”—literally his “flames”—also shifts attention toward his attachment to Briseis. Homer’s Thetis stresses the dishonor of losing his prize; the Latin gives that loss an emotional and erotic coloring. Its final claim, that valor will fall before lust, has no counterpart in Homer’s speech. Agamemnon’s seizure becomes more than an affront to one warrior: allowing it to stand would let appetite triumph over courage. Yet the Latin leaves the remedy unstated. Homer’s Thetis specifically asks Zeus to give the Trojans the upper hand until the Greeks restore her son’s honor (*Iliad* 1.509–510). Here the appeal ends instead with a stark declaration of what is at stake.
