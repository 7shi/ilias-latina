### 86–87 (*Iliad* 1.497–501)

> 86 emicat aetherias et in aurea sidera fertur.
> (of heaven she darts, and is borne to the golden stars.)
>
> 87 Tunc genibus regis sparsis affusa capillis:
> (Then, with her hair disheveled, she throws herself at the king’s knees:)

Thetis reaches the divine king, Jupiter, to plead for her son Achilles. Her flight continues the swift movement begun in the preceding line: the Latin replaces Homer’s arrival in “the great heaven and Olympus” (*Iliad* 1.497) with an ascent toward the golden stars. The destination remains the home of the gods, but the mountain itself disappears from view.

Homer places Zeus—Jupiter in the Latin poem—alone on the highest peak of Olympus. Thetis sits before him, clasping his knees with her left hand and reaching beneath his chin with her right (*Iliad* 1.498–501). These gestures establish her as a suppliant, asking the more powerful god to hear and honor her request. The Latin condenses that carefully described encounter into a single image of the mother at the king’s knees. It leaves out the gesture toward his chin and adds her disheveled hair, making her distress immediately visible. Achilles has withdrawn from battle after being dishonored; his mother now humbles herself before the ruler of the gods to seek redress for him. The closing colon leads directly into her appeal.
