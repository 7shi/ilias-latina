The book opens by invoking the Goddess to sing the wrath of Achilles, called proud Pelides, which brought grievous deaths upon the Greeks, sent heroes’ souls to Orcus, and left their bodies unburied for dogs and birds. These disasters fulfil the will of Jupiter, the “highest king,” and arise from the quarrel between scepter-bearing Atrides, Agamemnon, and Achilles, famed in war. The Greeks are also called Danaans, Achaeans, and the Argive peoples; Agamemnon is also called king of the Pelasgians.

The immediate cause is Agamemnon’s refusal to return Chryseis to her father Chryses. Chryses is a priest of Apollo, wearing a sacred fillet, and is repeatedly characterized through his grief, age, and devotion. He comes to the Greek camp, falls at Agamemnon’s knees, offers gifts, and begs for his daughter, on whom he says his life depends. The Myrmidons are moved by his tears and urge her return, but Agamemnon dismisses him, scorning sacred duty because of his consuming passion for the girl. Back at Apollo’s temples, Chryses laments violently and appeals to the god, asking why his lifelong worship has brought no protection. He offers himself to the god’s arrows rather than have his daughter suffer for any unwitting fault of his. Apollo—called Phoebus, the Delphic god, the god of prophecy, and the son of Latona and mighty Jupiter—answers by sending a devastating plague among the Greeks.

After nine nights, Achilles summons the Greek leaders on the tenth day to discover the plague’s cause. The seer Calchas, also called Thestorides, learns both cause and remedy but fears to speak until Achilles guarantees his safety. He declares that angry Phoebus must be appeased by returning chaste Chryseis to her devout father. Agamemnon denounces Calchas as a liar and exchanges insults with Achilles amid a general uproar. Eventually he yields: Chryseis is returned untouched, together with many gifts. Ulysses, “known to all,” takes her by ship to her native city and sails back to the Greek fleet. Apollo is appeased, and the Greeks recover their strength.

Still mourning Chryseis, Agamemnon seizes Briseis from Achilles to soothe his passion with another man’s beloved. Achilles—also called great Achilles, the undefeated leader, fierce Aeacides, and Pelides—draws his sword and threatens Agamemnon with death unless his rewards for honorable service are restored. Agamemnon prepares to defend himself, but chaste Pallas restrains Achilles, preventing bloodshed and disgrace. Achilles then appeals for vengeance to his sea-dwelling mother, Thetis, calling Agamemnon Plisthenides. Thetis comes to the nearby Myrmidon camp and urges her son to abstain from battle before ascending to heaven.

Thetis supplicates Jupiter, the supreme father, supreme king, Thunderer, and father of Olympus, asking him to avenge the dishonor done to her son and his love. Jupiter promises to take care of the matter and tells her to comfort Achilles; she returns to the sea. Juno angrily protests that Thetis, whom she calls Doris’ daughter, and Achilles should wield such influence that Jupiter would destroy her beloved Greeks and restore Trojan strength. She invokes her own standing as Jupiter’s wife and sister, and the divine couple exchange abuse. The unnamed fire god intervenes and settles their quarrel. Jupiter dismisses the assembly. The book ends at sunset on Olympus: the gods enjoy a lavish feast, then retire to their chambers and sleep.
