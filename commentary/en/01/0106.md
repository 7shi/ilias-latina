### 106–107 (*Iliad* 1.571–600, 1.606)

> 106 Tandem interposito lis Ignipotente resedit
> (At last, with Ignipotens’ intervention, the quarrel subsided,)
>
> 107 conciliumque simul genitor dimittit Olympi.
> (and at once the father of Olympus dismisses the assembly.)

The quarrel between Jupiter and Juno ends through the intervention of Vulcan, the divine craftsman and god of fire, here called Ignipotens, “the master of fire.” He is the Latin counterpart of Homer’s Hephaestus and appears here as the son mediating between his parents. Peace returns to Olympus, the home of the gods, but Jupiter’s promise to honor Achilles remains unchanged.

The Latin compresses a lively domestic scene into a few words. In Homer, Hephaestus urges his mother, Hera, to speak gently to Zeus rather than spoil the gods’ feast with a quarrel over mortals. His appeal carries a warning: he could not protect her against Zeus’s violence. Once before, when he tried to defend her, Zeus seized him by the foot and hurled him from Olympus; he fell all day before landing on the island of Lemnos. Hephaestus then places a cup in Hera’s hand, and she smiles. As he serves nectar to the other gods, their laughter completes the change of mood. The Latin keeps only the outcome of this mixture of persuasion, remembered violence, and conviviality: the dispute subsides.

The closing action gives Jupiter, “the father of Olympus,” firm control over the gathering. In Homer, the gods eventually go home separately to sleep (*Iliad* 1.606); here Jupiter formally dismisses their assembly. The compressed ending leaves his authority unmistakable. The gods’ disagreement has been settled, while the Greeks must still face the consequences of his decision.
