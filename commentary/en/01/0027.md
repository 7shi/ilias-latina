### 27–31 (*Iliad* 1.33–36)

> 27 Contemptus repetit Phoebeia templa sacerdos
> (Rejected, the priest returns to the temples of Phoebus)
>
> 28 squalidaque infestis maerens secat unguibus ora
> (and in grief tears his unkempt face with savage nails,)
>
> 29 dilaceratque comas annosaque tempora plangit.
> (rips his hair and beats his aged temples.)
>
> 30 Mox ubi depositi gemitus lacrimaeque quierunt,
> (Then, when his groans have subsided and his tears have ceased,)
>
> 31 Fatidici his sacras compellat uocibus aures:
> (he addresses these words to the sacred ears of the god of prophecy:)

Driven away by Agamemnon, Chryses turns from the king who has rejected him to the god whom he serves. Phoebus is Apollo, here also called “the god of prophecy” because he reveals the future through oracles. The priest’s return to Apollo’s temples gives his withdrawal a destination: denied a hearing in the Greek camp, he seeks one in a sacred place.

The Latin greatly changes the manner of that withdrawal. In Homer, the old man is frightened into obedience and walks silently beside the sounding sea; only when he is some distance away does he pray to Apollo (*Iliad* 1.33–36). Here fear and silence give way to an extended display of grief. Chryses claws his face, tears his hair, and strikes his head—gestures of mourning that make his anguish physically visible. His aged temples emphasize the vulnerability of the father whom Agamemnon has dismissed. Although his daughter is alive, his inability to recover her draws from him the gestures of someone mourning a death.

This added scene also creates a pause before the prayer. Only after the groans and tears have quieted can Chryses put his appeal into words. The “sacred ears” of Apollo offer a pointed contrast with the king’s refusal to listen: human authority has failed the suppliant, but divine authority remains. The passage ends at the moment when the rejected father speaks again, now as a priest appealing to his god.
