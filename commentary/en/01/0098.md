### 98–105 (*Iliad* 1.536–539, 1.555–559, 4.60–61, 1.540–543, 1.560–569)

> 98 Offensa est Iuno: "Tantum"que ait, "optime coniunx,
> (Juno took offense and said, “So much, my excellent husband,)
>
> 99 Doride nata ualet, tantum debetur Achilli,
> (does Doris’ daughter prevail, is so much owed to Achilles,)
>
> 100 ut mihi quae coniunx dicor tua quaeque sororis
> (that—though I am called your wife and a sister’s)
>
> 101 dulce fero nomen, dilectos fundere Achiuos
> (sweet name bear—the Achaeans I love to slaughter)
>
> 102 et Troum renouare uelis in proelia uires?
> (and to renew the Trojans’ strength for battle you should wish?)
>
> 103 Haec ita dona refers nobis? sic diligor a te?"
> (Is this how you repay me with gifts? Is this how you love me?”)
>
> 104 Talibus incusat dictis irata Tonantem
> (With such words she angrily reproaches the Thunderer)
>
> 105 inque uicem summi patitur conuicia regis.
> (and in turn endures the abuse of the supreme king.)

Thetis’ successful appeal immediately provokes a quarrel among the gods. Juno, Jupiter’s wife and sister, favors the Achaeans—the Greeks fighting against Troy—and understands what his promise to Achilles will cost them. To restore Achilles’ honor, Jupiter must let the Greeks suffer in his absence, giving renewed strength to their Trojan enemies. Thetis has obtained protection for her son’s standing at the expense of the army with which he came to war.

“Doris’ daughter” is Thetis: Doris is her mother, a sea goddess, and Nereus her father. Juno’s question sets the influence of this visiting petitioner against her own claims as Jupiter’s wife and sister. Her address, “my excellent husband,” is pointed rather than affectionate. She treats his willingness to sacrifice the Greeks she loves as an injury to their marriage: what sort of return is this for her love and position?

The Latin brings together parts of a longer confrontation in Homer. There, Hera, as Homer calls Juno, first complains that Zeus makes secret plans without her, then voices her fear that he has promised Thetis to honor Achilles by destroying many Greeks beside their ships (*Iliad* 1.540–559). Here secrecy drops out, and the consequences of his promise become the immediate accusation. Her appeal to her standing as wife and sister also draws on a later dispute over Troy, where she insists on the dignity of her birth and marriage (*Iliad* 4.60–61).

Jupiter’s answer is compressed even more sharply. Homer gives him a threatening speech: he orders Hera to sit silently and warns that all the gods together could not protect her if he laid hands on her; she submits in fear (*Iliad* 1.560–569). The Latin reduces this to the abuse she receives in return. Its closing titles, “Thunderer” and “supreme king,” nevertheless keep his overwhelming power before us: Juno can protest, but she cannot overturn his decision.
