### 68–69 (*Iliad* 1.457, 1.472–474, 1.456)

> 68 Protinus infesti placantur numina Phoebi
> (At once the angry god Phoebus is appeased,)
>
> 69 et prope consumptae uires redduntur Achiuis.
> (and their almost exhausted strength is restored to the Achaeans.)

The return of Chryseis brings relief to the Greek army. Phoebus is Apollo, whose plague had punished the Greeks for Agamemnon’s treatment of his priest and the priest’s daughter. The Achaeans are the same Greeks just called the Danaans. Their strength has been nearly spent by the disease; with Apollo reconciled, they can recover.

The Latin places this resolution after Ulysses’ return and makes it immediate: “At once” the god is appeased and the army restored. Homer develops the reconciliation through prayer and song before the homeward voyage. Chryses asks Apollo to “ward off the shameful destruction from the Danaans” (*Iliad* 1.456), and the god hears him. The young Greeks then spend the whole day singing a paean—a hymn of praise to Apollo—and he takes pleasure in their song. These two Latin lines condense that sustained act of worship into its successful outcome. They also explicitly describe the recovery that Homer leaves implicit in the god’s acceptance of the prayer. The divine anger has subsided, though the quarrel between Agamemnon and Achilles remains.
