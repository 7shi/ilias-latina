### 70–73 (*Iliad* 1.318–325, 1.345–348)

> 70 Non tamen Atridae Chryseidis excidit ardor:
> (Yet Atrides has not lost his passion for Chryseis:)
>
> 71 maeret et amissos deceptus luget amores.
> (he grieves and, feeling cheated, mourns his lost love.)
>
> 72 Mox rapta magnum Briseide priuat Achillem
> (Soon he seizes Briseis, depriving great Achilles of her,)
>
> 73 solaturque suos alienis ignibus ignes.
> (and soothes his own flames with another’s flames.)

Apollo’s anger has been appeased, but Agamemnon’s resentment remains. Here he is called Atrides, “son of Atreus.” He has surrendered Chryseis to her father and secured relief for the army, yet he experiences that necessary surrender as a personal loss. He compensates himself by taking Briseis, the captive woman allotted to Achilles as a prize of war. Despite their similar names, Chryseis and Briseis are different women: restoring one to her father leads to the seizure of the other.

The Latin makes frustrated desire the immediate cause of Agamemnon’s action. The closing image of “flames” presents love as a consuming fire: Agamemnon tries to ease his own passion by appropriating the object of another man’s love. His consolation thus comes at Achilles’ expense. Homer’s account places greater emphasis on authority and the division of prizes. Agamemnon insists that he must not be left without a prize while Achilles keeps his; taking Briseis asserts his superior power and publicly dishonors Achilles.

The retelling also rearranges and compresses events. In Homer, Agamemnon orders Briseis’ seizure before Chryseis has been returned and Apollo reconciled. He sends two heralds to Achilles’ shelter, threatening to come himself with more men if Achilles refuses. Achilles allows her to be handed over, and his companion Patroclus brings her out. Homer briefly registers her own response: “the woman went unwillingly with them” (*Iliad* 1.348). The Latin omits the messengers, the handover, and Briseis’ reluctance, concentrating instead on Agamemnon’s desire and the injury he inflicts. The army’s recovery is therefore followed immediately by a fresh breach between its commander and its greatest warrior.
