At dawn, when Titan reveals the world with his rays, Jupiter summons the gods and warns them not to contend in arms against his words. He then descends to the shaded mountains of Ida and watches the Ilian battle lines. Holding up golden scales, he weighs the fates of the Phrygians, that is the Trojans, and of the Achaeans; the ruin of the Greeks weighs down with heavy arms, signalling their defeat.

Hector, called Priamides, “the glory of Phrygia,” and “the Trojan hero,” drives the Danaans in immense anger and bears down with all his forces. The Achaeans fall into confusion, and their Doric camp resounds with uproar. Atrides, Agamemnon, shut within the walls, urges on his comrades and steels the young men’s spirits. Tydides, Diomedes, gleaming in blazing armor, charges through the enemy and kills Agelaus, who meets him brandishing a huge weapon. Teucer, sheltered in the vast arms of Ajax, drives the Phrygians and scatters arrows into their backs. He kills fierce Gorgythion and then the charioteer of proud Hector. Hector forestalls Teucer with a stone, knocking his bow away and laying him low. Teucer’s faithful comrades snatch him from the slaughter and lift him up.

Hector rushes wildly on every side, terrifying the opposing ranks with his spear. Shaken by their losses, the Danaans flee back into their camp and bar its gates with an oak bolt. The Phrygians besiege the Greeks shut in by the rampart, beset the walls with sentries and ring them with watch-fires. The book ends with the Greeks hemmed in and the rest of the Trojan warriors lying across the fields, indulging in wine and relaxing their cares.

The Greeks are also called Achaeans and Danaans, and the Trojans Phrygians.
