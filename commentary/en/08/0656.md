### 656–659 (*Iliad* 8.69–74, 8.215)

> 656 sustinet auratas aequato pondere lances
> (he holds up the golden scales, their pans evenly balanced,)
>
> 657 fataque dura Phrygum casusque expendit Achiuum
> (and weighs the harsh fate of the Phrygians and the fall of the Achaeans,)
>
> 658 et Graium clades grauibus praeponderat armis.
> (and the ruin of the Greeks weighs down with heavy arms.)
>
> 659 Interea Danaos ingenti concitus ira
> (Meanwhile the Danaans, roused by immense anger,)

The mighty hand of Jupiter, introduced in the preceding verse, now holds the balance that reveals which army must suffer defeat. The Phrygians are the Trojans, named after a people of the region around Troy; Achaeans, Greeks, and Danaans are names of the same army, not separate ones. The scales begin level, but the Greek ruin proves heavier. In this weighing, sinking means disaster rather than victory.

Homer makes the scene more explicit: the god places two portions of death in his golden scales, and the Greek side sinks toward the earth while the Trojan side rises toward heaven (*Iliad* 8.69–74). The Latin condenses that movement into the greater weight of Greek destruction. How the heavy arms are meant is not said: they may be the weight that bears the Greek pan down.

The unfinished final sentence turns from the god overlooking the battlefield to the warrior driving the Greeks back: Hector, the foremost Trojan defender. The Latin jumps over a substantial stretch of Homer’s fighting to a moment when the Greeks are being pressed toward their ships. Homer describes Hector there as a match for the swift war god; the Latin instead introduces him through his immense anger. The judgment shown in the scales is becoming a human reality on the battlefield.
