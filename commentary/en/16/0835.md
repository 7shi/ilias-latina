### 835 (*Iliad* 17.125)

> 835 traicit et uictor Vulcania detrahit arma.
> (runs through, and as victor strips off the armor of Vulcan.)

Hector completes the killing stroke and takes the armor that Patroclus had borrowed from Achilles. The equipment that allowed Patroclus to appear as the Greeks’ greatest warrior now becomes the victor’s prize. Its loss compounds the disaster: Achilles has lost both his companion and the armor in which that companion went out to fight.

The Latin moves directly from the fatal blow to the stripping of the body. Homer separates these moments with Patroclus’ dying words and the opening struggle over his corpse; only later does he say that Hector “had stripped Patroclus of his glorious armor” (*Iliad* 17.125).

“Vulcan’s armor” identifies the equipment as the work of the divine smith, the Roman counterpart of Hephaestus. Homer does not attribute this particular suit to that god: it was a gift of the gods to Achilles’ father, Peleus, which he passed on to his son (*Iliad* 17.194–197). The armor Homer describes Hephaestus making for Achilles comes later, to replace the equipment now lost to Hector.
