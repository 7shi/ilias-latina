### 876–877 (*Iliad* 18.490, 18.497–500)

> 876 fluminaque et montes cumque altis oppida muris,
> (and rivers and mountains, and towns with lofty walls,)
>
> 877 in quibus exercent leges annosaque iura
> (in which they practice the laws and time-honored rights,)

The earth depicted on Achilles’ shield bears not only forests and wild beasts, but also rivers, mountains, and human settlements. The towns’ lofty walls mark out protected spaces within the landscape; inside them, established laws govern communal life, and the people who practice them are named in the next verse as contending parties. “Time-honored” gives this justice a history: the communities appear to possess inherited institutions, not merely buildings.

Homer introduces “two cities of mortal men” (*Iliad* 18.490), then makes civic justice visible through a particular dispute. A crowd gathers in the public square, where two men argue over compensation for a man’s death (*Iliad* 18.497–500). Such compensation offered a means of settling a killing without continuing the violence through revenge. The Latin condenses this vivid, contested encounter into a general statement about the administration of justice. Instead of watching individual speakers before an assembled community, we see towns characterized by enduring legal order. On a shield made for battle, this brief glimpse of peaceful civic life places warfare within a much larger human world.
