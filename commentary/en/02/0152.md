### 152–153 (*Iliad* 2.322, 2.328–330)

> 152 in decimo labor est, Calchas quem dixerat, anno,
> (Our appointed toil comes in the tenth year, as Calchas foretold,)
>
> 153 quo caderet Danaum uictricibus Ilion armis."
> (when Ilion would fall to the victorious arms of the Danaans.”)

Nestor completes his recollection by explaining what the omen promised. Calchas, the Greek army’s diviner, had read the nine birds killed by the serpent as a sign of nine years of warfare, with victory to follow in the tenth. Ilion is Troy, and the Danaans are the Greeks besieging it. Their long struggle is therefore not evidence that the expedition has failed: it is the hardship foretold before its success.

Homer distinguishes the nine years of fighting from the tenth year, when the city will be captured, and has Odysseus declare that the prophecy is now coming true. The Latin compresses this into a single sentence, placing the appointed toil and the promised victory together in the tenth year. Here Nestor delivers the reminder. His argument turns the army’s weariness into a reason to persevere: after so much suffering, the Greeks must not abandon the campaign when its promised goal is near.
