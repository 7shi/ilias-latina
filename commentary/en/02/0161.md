### 161–166 (*Iliad* 2.484, 2.487, 2.485, 2.493)

> 161 Vos mihi nunc, Musae - quid enim non ordine nostis? -,
> (Now, Muses—for what do you not know in its proper order?—)
>
> 162 nomina clara ducum clarosque referte parentes
> (recount for me the famous names of the leaders, their renowned parents,)
>
> 163 et dulces patrias: nam sunt haec munera uestra.
> (and their dear homelands: for these tasks are yours.)
>
> 164 Dicamus quot quisque rates ad Pergama duxit
> (Let us tell how many ships each brought to Pergama)
>
> 165 et coeptum peragamus opus, sitque auctor Apollo
> (and complete the work begun; may Apollo inspire it)
>
> 166 aspiretque libens operi per singula nostro.
> (and willingly favor our work in every detail.)

As the army advances toward battle, the narrator pauses to introduce the leaders and the forces they have brought. Until now, the Greek expedition has largely been represented by the commanders speaking in the assembly. The coming catalogue will reveal its wider scope: many separate contingents, each with its own leader and homeland, have sailed against the same enemy. Pergama is another name for Troy, especially its citadel.

The Muses are goddesses who give poets the knowledge and skill needed to tell their stories. The appeal to them follows Homer’s introduction to his catalogue of the Greek ships. There, their authority rests on their divine presence and complete knowledge: “You are goddesses, you are present, and you know all things” (*Iliad* 2.485). The Latin compresses this assurance into the question “what do you not know?” and emphasizes their ability to set everything out in order. It also expressly asks for the leaders’ parents and “dear homelands,” making the list a record of ancestry and belonging as well as military strength.

The closing appeal to Apollo, a god associated with poetry, music, and prophecy, is an addition to Homer’s invocation here. Alongside the Muses’ knowledge, the poet seeks divine inspiration and favor to carry his undertaking through. For a moment, the labor of telling the story comes into view: the army must be marshalled for battle, and its many names and ships must likewise be marshalled into verse.
