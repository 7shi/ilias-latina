### 226–235 (*Iliad* 2.816–818, 2.809–810)

> 226 Priamides Hector totamque in proelia pubem
> (Priamides Hector, and all the young men into battle)
>
> 227 festinare iubet portisque agit agmen apertis.
> (he orders to hurry, and drives the column through the open gates.)
>
> 228 Cui fulgens auro cassis iuuenile tegebat
> (A helmet gleaming with gold covered his youthful)
>
> 229 omni parte caput, munibat pectora thorax
> (head on every side; a breastplate guarded his chest,)
>
> 230 et clipeus laeuam, dextram decorauerat hasta
> (and a shield his left hand; a spear graced his right,)
>
> 231 ornabatque latus mucro; simul alta nitentes
> (and a sword adorned his side; at the same time gleaming)
>
> 232 crura tegunt ocreae, quales decet Hectoris esse.
> (greaves cover his long legs, such as befit Hector.)
>
> 233 Hunc sequitur forma melior, tunc fortis in armis,
> (After him comes one fairer in looks, now brave in arms,)
>
> 234 belli causa Paris, patriae funesta ruina,
> (Paris, the cause of the war, his country’s deadly ruin,)
>
> 235 Deiphobusque Helenusque simul fortisque Polites
> (and with him Deiphobus and Helenus and brave Polites)

Hector’s name completes the sentence begun in the preceding passage: it is he who takes up arms at his father’s command. “Priamides” means “son of Priam,” identifying him through the king whose authority he now carries into action. Hector orders Troy’s fighting youth to make haste and drives the army through the gates to confront the Greeks. The response to Iris’s warning has become a general advance.

The Latin brings together two moments from Homer. In the *Iliad*, the gates open and the troops pour out, on foot and in chariots, amid a great uproar; Hector is then named as the leader of the largest and finest Trojan contingent. Here both movements are placed directly under his command. The narrative then pauses over his appearance. Homer’s brief introduction calls him “great Hector of the flashing helmet” (*Iliad* 2.816); the Latin expands that martial brilliance into a full display of armor. Helmet, breastplate, shield, spear, sword, and greaves—the metal guards worn over the shins—present a warrior protected and equipped from head to foot. His equipment both guards and adorns him: its splendor is appropriate to Troy’s foremost defender.

Paris follows, and the description changes sharply from admiration to warning. He is presented as handsomer than Hector and, at this moment, brave in arms. But his beauty and readiness for battle cannot cancel his responsibility for the conflict. Paris had taken Helen, wife of the Greek king Menelaus, to Troy, prompting the Greek expedition to recover her. Calling him his country’s “deadly ruin” looks ahead to the destruction that this war will eventually bring upon Troy. The contrast sets the brother who defends the city beside the brother whose actions have endangered it.

Deiphobus, Helenus, and Polites are also sons of Priam. Their appearance gathers the royal brothers behind Hector rather than reproducing Homer’s broader survey of the Trojan forces and their allies. For the moment, Troy’s defense is embodied in the king’s own family, with Hector at its head.
