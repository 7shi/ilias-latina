### 780–781 (*Iliad* 14.409–413, 14.418)

> 780 quem saxo ingenti percussum maximus Aiax
> (him, struck with a huge stone, mighty Ajax)
>
> 781 depulit et toto prostratum corpore fudit.
> (drove back and laid him low, prostrate at full length.)

Hector’s furious advance is abruptly checked. Ajax, the Greek champion who is the son of Telamon, brings down Troy’s leading defender with a stone rather than a spear or sword. The man who seemed to rage everywhere across the battlefield now lies stretched on the ground. This is a disabling blow, not Hector’s death, but it removes the Trojans’ strongest fighter from the immediate struggle.

The Latin keeps the decisive action while stripping away Homer’s detailed account. In the *Iliad*, Ajax strikes Hector as he is withdrawing. He picks up one of the many stones used to prop up the Greek ships, a reminder that the fighting has reached the Greeks’ camp beside the sea. The stone hits Hector in the chest, just above the shield’s rim and close to his neck, sending him spinning “like a top” (*Iliad* 14.409–413). The Latin omits the stone’s source, the precise wound, and the spinning motion. Its emphasis falls instead on the huge missile, Ajax’s strength, and the completeness of Hector’s collapse.
