### 783–785 (*Iliad* 14.433–437, 14.440–441)

> 783 sanguineos fluctus Xanthi lauere fluentis.
> (streams of blood, they washed in the flowing waters of Xanthus.)
>
> 784 Inde iterum ad pugnam redeunt; fit maxima caedes
> (Then again they return to the fight; there is very great slaughter)
>
> 785 amborum et manat tellus infecta cruore.
> (on both sides, and the earth runs, stained with gore.)

The Trojans bring the wounded Hector to Xanthus, the river of the Trojan plain, and wash him in its waters. The “streams of blood” complete the description of his vomiting begun in the preceding verse: their champion remains helpless after the blow dealt by Ajax. In Homer, the rescue unfolds more slowly. Hector’s companions reach a river crossing, lower him from his chariot to the ground, and pour water over him. He revives, opens his eyes, and rises onto his knees before vomiting dark blood (*Iliad* 14.433–437). The Latin draws these actions together into one stark image of the wounded man being washed while blood pours from his body.

The rescuers then return to the fighting; the passage does not say that Hector himself is ready to fight again. Homer makes his removal the occasion for a renewed Greek assault: seeing Hector leave the battlefield, the Greeks attack the Trojans more fiercely (*Iliad* 14.440–441). The Latin instead moves directly from the rescue to a general picture of slaughter on both sides. Its closing image of the blood-soaked earth has no direct counterpart in the lines being abridged. Hector’s individual suffering gives way to the wider devastation of the battlefield, where the bloodshed continues without him.
