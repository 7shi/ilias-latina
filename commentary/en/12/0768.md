### 768–769 (*Iliad* 12.469–471)

> 768 ascenduntque aditus et portis uiribus instant.
> (and climb the approaches and press against the gates with force.)
>
> 769 Turbati fugiunt omnes iam castra Pelasgi
> (In panic all the Pelasgians now flee the camp)

The Trojans carry their assault up to the gates, and the defenders break and run. The Pelasgians here are the Greeks, whose fortified camp protects their ships on the shore. The movement follows directly from the preceding picture of the Trojans advancing beneath overlapping shields: their organized pressure now gives way to the enemy’s panic.

The Latin has already told how Hector smashed a gate with a rock and how the Trojans burst in; it now returns to the moment of the breakthrough, which Homer describes: “At once some crossed over the wall, while others poured through the constructed gates; the Danaans fled in terror among the hollow ships, and an unceasing din arose” (*Iliad* 12.469–471).

The Latin preserves the assault and rout but makes the action less explicit: instead of showing separate groups crossing the wall and streaming through the gates, it describes the attackers climbing and pressing forward. The ships toward which Homer’s Greeks retreat come in the next verse. What remains is a swift reversal: the Trojans advance together, while all the Greeks abandon the camp in confusion.
