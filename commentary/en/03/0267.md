### 267–271 (*Iliad* 3.52, 3.69, 3.68, 3.73, 3.70, 3.58)

> 267 Aequius aduersis tecum concurrat in armis
> (It would be fairer that against you in battle should fight)
>
> 268 impiger Atrides, spectet Danaumque Phrygumque
> (the tireless Atrides; let the Danaans’ and the Phrygians’)
>
> 269 depositis populus telis. Vos, foedere iuncto,
> (army watch, with weapons laid down. You two, with a pact agreed,)
>
> 270 aduersas conferte manus, decernite ferro."
> (engage in combat and settle the matter with the sword.”)
>
> 271 Dixit. Quem contra paucis Priameius heros:
> (He spoke. The hero, Priam’s son, answered him briefly:)

Hector turns his reproach into a proposal: instead of letting others bleed for his love affair, Paris should face the husband he has wronged. Atrides means “son of Atreus” and here names Menelaus, Helen’s husband. The Danaans are the Greeks, and the Phrygians are the Trojans. Both armies are to lay down their weapons and watch while the two rivals fight. “Fairer” carries the force of Hector’s accusation forward: the danger should fall on Paris himself, not on everyone defending him.

This proposal changes who takes the initiative. In Homer, Hector challenges Paris to stand against Menelaus, but Paris himself then proposes the duel, asking that the armies sit down while the two men fight over Helen and her possessions (*Iliad* 3.67–70). The Latin gives that plan to Hector, making it a further demand imposed on his disgraced brother. It also compresses the arrangements: an agreement is to govern the encounter, but the terms and stakes are not spelled out here. The closing line finally gives Paris his turn to speak. He is the “hero, Priam’s son”—a conventional designation for a warrior of royal birth, not praise for the courage he has just failed to show.
