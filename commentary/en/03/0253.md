### 253 (*Iliad* 3.16)

> 253 cum Paris, exitium Troiae funestaque flamma,
> (when Paris, the ruin of Troy and its deadly flame,)

Against the poised battle lines, attention turns to Paris, a Trojan prince whose taking of Helen, wife of the Greek king Menelaus, brought the Greek expedition against Troy. Before describing his actions, the Latin names him as the disaster he brings upon his own city: not merely the cause of its ruin, but its ruin embodied.

This is a darker introduction than Homer’s. In the *Iliad*, “godlike Alexander”—Alexander is another name for Paris—takes his place at the front of the Trojan army (*Iliad* 3.16). The Latin replaces that admiring description of his appearance with a judgment on his fatal role in the story. The “deadly flame” also recalls a dream attributed to his mother, Hecuba: she dreamed that she gave birth to a firebrand that would set Troy and the surrounding lands ablaze. Paris thus enters the scene beneath the shadow of the destruction his birth was believed to foreshadow.
