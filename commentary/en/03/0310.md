### 310–312 (*Iliad* 3.371–376)

> 310 fortia rupisset laxatis uincula nodis,
> (broken the strong bonds by loosening their knots,)
>
> 311 ultimus ille dies Paridi foret. Abstrahit auro
> (that would have been Paris’s last day. Drags away, with gold)
>
> 312 fulgentem galeam secum Menelaus et ardens
> (gleaming, the helmet Menelaus, taking it with him, and, burning with anger,)

The rescue is completed: without Cytherea’s intervention, Paris would have died. The “strong bonds” are the helmet’s fastening beneath his chin, which the goddess releases so that Menelaus pulls away the helmet without its wearer. His apparent victory leaves him holding only a piece of splendid armor. The unfinished phrase carries his anger forward: deprived of his opponent, he has not abandoned the fight.

Homer makes the physical danger more explicit. As Menelaus drags Paris away, the helmet’s strap chokes his soft throat; Aphrodite then breaks the oxhide strap, and “the empty helmet followed his heavy hand” (*Iliad* 3.376). The Latin condenses this struggle, combining the breaking of the fastening with the loosening of its knots. It also sharpens the threatened outcome. Where Homer says that Menelaus would have dragged Paris away and won immense glory, the Latin declares that this would have been Paris’s last day. His survival depends not on his own resistance but on his divine protector.
