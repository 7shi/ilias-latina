### 281–282 (*Iliad* 3.340–341, 3.335–338)

> 281 Interea toto procedit ab agmine Troum
> (Meanwhile, from the whole host of the Trojans comes forward)
>
> 282 pulcher Alexander, clipeoque insignis et hasta.
> (handsome Alexander, conspicuous with his shield and spear.)

Into the field cleared by the two armies steps Alexander, another name for Paris, the Trojan prince whose taking of Helen from her husband Menelaus has brought the opposing forces to Troy. The treaty has prepared the way for their duel; now attention narrows from the assembled soldiers to a single champion. His beauty and his weapons make him an imposing figure, though his appearance alone cannot establish his courage or fighting skill.

The Latin replaces Homer’s detailed preparations with a brief portrait. In the *Iliad*, Paris takes up a large, sturdy shield, puts on a horsehair-crested helmet, and chooses a spear suited to his grip (*Iliad* 3.335–338). Here the shield and spear stand for his full equipment: he appears already armed and advancing. Homer then brings both champions into view together: “They stepped into the space between the Trojans and Achaeans” (*Iliad* 3.341). The Latin instead gives Alexander the first entrance, holding the reader’s gaze on the man who must now fight to keep Helen.
