### 307–309 (*Iliad* 3.369–370, 3.374, 3.380–381)

> 307 et iuuenem arrepta prosternit casside uictor
> (and, seizing his helmet, he throws the youth down in triumph,)
>
> 308 ad socios traheretque, et, ni caligine caeca
> (and would have dragged him to his comrades, had not, in blinding mist,)
>
> 309 texisset Cytherea uirum subiectaque mento
> (Cytherea shrouded the man, and, fastened beneath his chin,)

Even without his sword, Menelaus overpowers Paris. Grasping his opponent’s helmet, he throws him down and nearly drags him away to the Greek ranks. The word “in triumph” makes the reversal especially sharp: Menelaus appears victorious, but divine intervention prevents him from securing his prize. Cytherea is Venus, the Roman counterpart of Homer’s Aphrodite, goddess of love and Paris’s protector. Her title comes from Cythera, an island associated with her worship. The man she conceals in mist is Paris.

The Latin brings together moments that Homer narrates separately. In the *Iliad*, Menelaus seizes Paris’s helmet and drags him toward the Greeks, while the helmet’s chinstrap chokes its wearer. Aphrodite first saves Paris by breaking that strap, leaving Menelaus holding an empty helmet. Only when Menelaus attacks again with his spear does she carry Paris away and conceal him in mist, “very easily, as a goddess can” (*Iliad* 3.381). Here the mist already interrupts the threatened capture, bringing the goddess’s rescue to the foreground. The unfinished phrase at the end turns toward the strap beneath Paris’s chin, whose part in his escape remains to be told. Menelaus’s strength has proved greater than Paris’s, but strength alone cannot settle a contest in which a goddess intervenes.
