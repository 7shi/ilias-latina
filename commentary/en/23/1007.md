### 1007 (*Iliad* 23.257–261)

> 1007 et uapido cineri ludorum indicit honores.
> (and for the spent ashes he proclaims the honor of games.)

The ashes are those of Patroclus, Achilles’ beloved companion, whose body has been burned and buried beneath the mound. Achilles honors him not only with a funeral but with athletic contests: the assembled warriors will compete for prizes, making their strength and skill part of a public tribute to the dead.

After the image of Hector’s body being dragged around the grave, the Latin turns back to these funeral games. In Homer, they follow the building of Patroclus’s mound and precede the renewed abuse of Hector. Achilles keeps the army gathered, seats the men in a broad assembly, and brings prizes from the ships: cauldrons, tripods, horses, mules, cattle, women held captive, and iron (*Iliad* 23.257–261). The Latin reduces the long account of the contests to this single announcement. Set beside the preceding line, it brings Achilles’ contrasting actions into sharp relief: he honors his dead friend while denying honor to the enemy who killed him.
