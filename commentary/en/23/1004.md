### 1004–1005 (*Iliad* 23.108–110, 23.161–165)

> 1004 Interea uictor defleti corpus amici
> (Meanwhile the victor, of his mourned friend, the body)
>
> 1005 funerat Aeacides pompasque ad funera ducit.
> (buries, Aeacides, and leads solemn processions to the funeral rites.)

Aeacides is Achilles, named here as a descendant of Aeacus, his grandfather. The friend he mourns is Patroclus, whose death at Hector’s hands drove Achilles to seek revenge. Having killed Hector and dragged away his body, Achilles now turns to honoring the companion he has lost. Calling him “the victor” holds triumph and bereavement together: his victory has not ended his grief. The care shown to Patroclus’s body also contrasts sharply with his treatment of Hector’s.

These two lines compress an extended account of mourning and funeral preparations. In the corresponding passage of the *Iliad*, dawn finds the Greeks weeping around Patroclus’s body (*Iliad* 23.108–110). Later, Agamemnon, the Greek commander, dismisses the troops to their ships while those responsible for the funeral remain. They pile up wood, build an enormous funeral pyre, and place the body on top with grieving hearts (*Iliad* 23.161–165). Patroclus’s body is therefore to be cremated, rather than simply buried.

The Latin leaves out these practical arrangements and the division of responsibilities, making Achilles the central figure throughout. Its solemn processions convey the public dignity of the funeral: the friend whose death prompted such violence now receives an ordered, communal expression of grief.
