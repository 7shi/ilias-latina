### 749 (*Iliad* 11.122–123, 11.143–144)

> 749 Pisandrumque simul fratremque ad bella ruentem
> (and Pisander at the same time, and his brother rushing into war,)

Agamemnon’s onslaught continues with another pair of victims. Pisander and his brother Hippolochus are Trojan warriors, sons of Antimachus. The sentence carries forward the killing begun in the preceding line, making their fall part of the Greek commander’s relentless advance.

In Homer, the brothers are caught together in a chariot and plead for their lives, offering a rich ransom. Agamemnon refuses and strikes Pisander in the chest with his spear, throwing him to the ground (*Iliad* 11.143–144). The Latin leaves out the appeal for mercy and the details of Pisander’s death. Its description of the brother “rushing into war” instead keeps the emphasis on combat: the men appear not as helpless captives bargaining for survival, but as opponents swept away by Agamemnon’s attack.
