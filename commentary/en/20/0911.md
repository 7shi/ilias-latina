### 911–914 (*Iliad* 21.233–240)

> 911 At Venus et Phrygiae gentis tutator Apollo
> (But Venus and Apollo, protector of the Phrygian people,)
>
> 912 cogunt in Danaos Xanthi consurgere fluctus,
> (force the waves of Xanthus to rise against the Danaans,)
>
> 913 ut fera terribili miscentem proelia dextra
> (so that the man stirring up savage battles with his fearsome right hand,)
>
> 914 obruat Aeaciden; qui protinus undique totis
> (Aeacides, it may overwhelm; and it at once, on every side, with all)

The river into which the Trojans fled now becomes a weapon against their pursuer. Achilles—here called Aeacides, “descendant of Aeacus,” his grandfather—has been slaughtering them in its waters. His devastating strength remains in view through the description of his “fearsome right hand,” but a rising river presents a danger unlike any human opponent. The Danaans are the Greeks, against whom the flood rises; Achilles is its particular target. Xanthus, also called Scamander, is the river of the Trojan plain, understood both as a watercourse and as a divine being.

The Latin retelling makes Venus and Apollo responsible for the attack. Both support Troy: Venus is the mother of the Trojan Aeneas, while Apollo is explicitly named the protector of the “Phrygian people,” a designation here for the Trojans that associates them with the wider region of Phrygia in Asia Minor. Their intervention continues the struggle between divine powers supporting the opposing armies.

In Homer, the river rises in its own anger, after reproaching Apollo for not helping the Trojans as Zeus had bidden him (*Iliad* 21.228–232). It churns up its waters, throws the bodies of Achilles’ victims onto dry land, and shelters the living in its deep eddies before raising a menacing wave around him (*Iliad* 21.234–240). The Latin preserves the central danger but compresses this vivid action into a divine command and its purpose: to overwhelm Achilles. Instead of dwelling on the river’s efforts to separate the dead from the living, it concentrates on the gods’ attempt to stop the killer. The sentence runs on into the next verses, where the river’s attack and Achilles’ resistance are told.
