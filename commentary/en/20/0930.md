### 930 (*Iliad* 21.606–611)

> 930 confugiunt portasque obiecto robore firmant.
> (flee for refuge, and make the gates fast with timber set against them.)

The Trojans take shelter inside Troy, completing the flight described in the preceding lines. Their last defense is now the city’s gates: instead of confronting Achilles in the open, they put a wooden barrier between themselves and their pursuer.

Homer dwells on the disorder of their escape. The city fills with fugitives, and no one dares remain outside the walls to wait for companions or discover who has survived and who has died. They pour inside, “all whose feet and knees had saved them” (*Iliad* 21.610–611). The Latin condenses this crowded, panicked retreat into a single action and ends it with the securing of the gates. In Homer, Priam had ordered the gatekeepers to hold the gates open for the fugitives and to close them again once they were inside, for fear that Achilles might leap within the walls (*Iliad* 21.531–536); the Latin shows the closing done. This gives the flight a decisive ending: the survivors are within the walls, but their safety depends on shutting the enemy out.
