### 278–280 (*Iliad* 3.116–117, 3.259–261, 3.271–301, 3.114–115)

> 278 Protinus accitur Priamus sacrisque peractis
> (At once Priam is summoned, and when the sacrifices are completed,)
>
> 279 foedera iunguntur. Post haec discedit uterque
> (a treaty is made. After this, both armies draw back,)
>
> 280 depositis populus telis campusque patescit.
> (having laid down their weapons, and the field lies open.)

The Greeks’ acceptance is now given the force of a solemn agreement. Priam, king of Troy and father of Hector and Paris, is summoned from the city to take part. The sacrifices bind the two sides before the gods: the proposed duel between Paris and Menelaus is to settle the dispute over Helen and bring the war to an end. As the armies withdraw, the battlefield becomes an arena for the two champions.

The Latin reduces a long and ominous ceremony to a few words. In Homer, Agamemnon, the Greek commander and Menelaus’s brother, calls on the gods to witness the agreement and punish anyone who breaks it. He specifies not only who will keep Helen but also the return of possessions and the compensation owed to the Greeks if Menelaus wins. Lambs are slaughtered, and both sides pour out wine, praying that oath-breakers’ brains may spill upon the ground in the same way (*Iliad* 3.271–301). The Latin preserves the sacrifices and treaty but leaves their terms and threatened penalties unspoken.

It also changes the sequence. In Homer, the soldiers take off their armour before Priam is summoned, laying it on the ground with only a little space between the opposing sides (*Iliad* 3.114–115). Here their withdrawal follows the treaty. That order gives the passage a clear forward movement: agreement, ritual confirmation, and finally an open field ready for the duel.
