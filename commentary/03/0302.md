### 302–306 (*Iliad* 3.361–368)

> 302 Dardaniumque premit iuuenem. Mox ense rigente
> (and presses hard upon the Dardanian youth. Soon, with his unyielding sword,)
>
> 303 cedentem retro dum desuper appetit hostem,
> (as he strikes from above at his retreating enemy,)
>
> 304 splendidus extremas galeae percussus ad oras
> (his gleaming blade, struck against the helmet’s outer rim,)
>
> 305 dissiluit mucro; gemuerunt agmina Graium.
> (shattered; the ranks of the Greeks groaned.)
>
> 306 Tum uero ardescit, quamuis manus ense carebat,
> (Then his anger truly blazes, though his hand held no sword,)

Menelaus continues the attack begun at the end of the preceding passage, driving Paris backward. The “Dardanian youth” is Paris: the name identifies him as a Trojan through Dardanus, a legendary ancestor of Troy’s royal family. The evenly matched struggle has given way to retreat, and Menelaus seems close to avenging the theft of Helen. But his downward sword-stroke ends in another frustration: the blade breaks against Paris’s helmet. The groan from the watching Greeks makes their disappointment part of the action. Their champion’s apparent advantage has suddenly been checked.

The broken sword comes directly from Homer, where Menelaus raises his weapon, strikes the helmet’s crest, and sees the blade shatter “into three and four pieces” and fall from his hand (*Iliad* 3.361–363). The Latin omits those particulars and changes whose distress we hear. In Homer, Menelaus himself groans, looks toward heaven, and bitterly accuses Zeus: “Father Zeus, no other god is more destructive than you” (*Iliad* 3.365). His complaint recalls both the failed spear-cast and the broken sword. Here that speech disappears. The Greeks groan instead, while Menelaus’s response is renewed fury. Losing his weapon does not extinguish his determination, and the unfinished final sentence carries that anger forward.
