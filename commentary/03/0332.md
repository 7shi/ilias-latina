### 332–335 (*Iliad* 3.437–440)

> 332 Tristis Alexander: "Non me superauit Atrides,
> (Downcast Alexander: “It was not Atrides who defeated me,)
>
> 333 o meus ardor" - ait - "sed castae Palladis ira.
> (my flame,” he says, “but the anger of chaste Pallas.)
>
> 334 Mox illum nostris succumbere turpiter armis
> (Soon him, yielding shamefully to my weapons,)
>
> 335 aspicies aderitque meo Cytherea labori."
> (you will see, and Cytherea will aid me in my struggle.”)

Alexander is another name for Paris. Though downcast after Helen’s reproach, he refuses to credit Atrides—Menelaus—with his defeat. Instead, he blames Pallas, the goddess Athena, whose support for Menelaus makes the unequal outcome easier to explain away. His answering address, “my flame,” echoes Helen’s affectionate greeting in the preceding passage. Their exchange remains a lovers’ conversation even as Paris tries to restore his standing as a warrior.

The Latin sharpens his excuse. In Homer, Paris first asks Helen not to wound him with harsh reproaches, then admits that “Menelaus has now won with Athena’s help” (*Iliad* 3.438–439). Here the appeal against her criticism disappears, and victory with divine assistance becomes victory attributable to the goddess rather than the man. Paris also promises a more emphatic reversal: Helen will soon witness Menelaus’s disgrace.

His confidence rests on Cytherea, a name for Venus, the goddess of love, associated with the island of Cythera. She is the goddess who has just rescued him from the duel. Homer’s Paris says more generally that “we too have gods beside us” (*Iliad* 3.440); the Latin names his particular protector. Her rescue now becomes, in his telling, grounds for expecting victory in another fight—an assurance that answers Helen’s fear without accepting her warning.
