### 339–340 (*Iliad* 3.449–450)

> 339 Interea toto Menelaus in agmine Troum
> (Meanwhile Menelaus, throughout the Trojan ranks,)
>
> 340 quaerit Alexandrum uictorque huc fertur et illuc.
> (searches for Alexander and, victorious, ranges here and there.)

While Paris lies with Helen, Menelaus is still searching the battlefield for him. Alexander is another name for Paris, whom Venus has rescued from the duel and carried away to his chamber. Menelaus has defeated his opponent but cannot find him to complete that victory. The contrast with the preceding embrace is sharp: Helen’s husband searches among the soldiers while her lover enjoys the protection of the goddess and the privacy of his home.

The Latin keeps Homer’s picture of a restless search but changes its emphasis. In the *Iliad*, Menelaus moves through the crowd “like a wild beast,” looking for “godlike Alexander” (*Iliad* 3.449–450). Here both descriptions disappear, and Menelaus is instead explicitly called “victorious.” That word makes the military outcome clear even as his movement “here and there” shows his frustration. Paris’s escape has left the duel without the settlement it was meant to secure: Menelaus has won the fight, but Helen has not been returned to him.
