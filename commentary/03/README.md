The Greek and Trojan armies stand ready for battle when Paris sees Menelaus in the opposing ranks and retreats in terror. Hector rebukes his brother as a disgrace to their homeland and family: Paris dared to seduce his host’s wife but now fears the husband whose wrong has brought war upon them. Hector contrasts Paris’s beauty, athletic prowess, and indulgence in love with the courage demanded by Mars, the war god. He proposes that Paris and Menelaus fight alone while both armies watch unarmed. Paris agrees, insisting that valor matters more to him than pleasure, provided that Helen belongs peacefully to the victor. Hector conveys the proposal to the Greeks; Priam is summoned, sacrifices are performed, and a treaty is concluded. Both armies lay down their weapons and clear the field.

Paris, also called Alexander, Priam’s son, the Dardanian youth, and the Phrygian robber, advances against Menelaus. Menelaus is called Atrides, “son of Atreus,” and invokes Jupiter before attacking. They exchange blows and spear-casts, then fight closely with swords and shields, like bulls competing for a mate. Paris’s armor saves him from Menelaus’s spear. Menelaus presses his attack, remembering his stolen wife, but his sword shatters against Paris’s helmet. He then seizes Paris by the helmet and throws him down. Paris would be dragged away and killed, but Venus—also called Cytherea and described as Paris’s own protecting goddess—conceals him in mist and releases the helmet’s fastenings. Menelaus throws the empty helmet among the Greek chiefs and renews his attack, but Venus carries Paris to his richly furnished chamber.

Venus brings Helen from the walls to Paris. Helen reproaches her defeated lover, yet speaks with affection and describes her terror that Menelaus would kill him and end their love. She warns him not to challenge her former husband again. Paris blames his defeat on the anger of chaste Pallas, Athena, and promises that with Venus’s help he will yet overcome Menelaus. They embrace and make love. Helen is also called Cygneis, the swan’s daughter; the closing erotic image makes Paris both her flame and the flame that will destroy Troy.

The Greeks are called Danai and Grai, and the Trojans Phryges or Phrygians. The book ends with the victorious Menelaus searching unsuccessfully for Paris among the Trojans. His brother, Agamemnon, unnamed here, urges on the Greek troops, rebukes the defeated Trojans, and demands fulfillment of the treaty and the return of Helen. The demand remains unanswered at the close.
