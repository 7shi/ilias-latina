### 336–338 (*Iliad* 3.441, 3.447–448)

> 336 Post haec amplexus per mutua corpora iunctis
> (After this, embracing her, bodies entwined, upon the joined)
>
> 337 incubuit membris Cygneidos; illa soluto
> (limbs of Cygneis he lay; she, with loosened)
>
> 338 accepit flammas gremio Troiaeque suasque.
> (lap, received both Troy’s flames and her own.)

Paris’s promise of victory gives way immediately to an embrace. The warrior who has just defended his honor returns to his role as Helen’s lover, and her reproaches give way to acceptance. Cygneis, “swan-daughter,” is Helen: the name recalls the story that Jupiter, taking the form of a swan, approached her mother Leda.

The Latin compresses the couple’s return to bed into a single physical encounter. In Homer, Paris invites Helen to “turn to love and lie together” (*Iliad* 3.441); he then leads the way to bed, his wife follows, and they lie down together (*Iliad* 3.447–448). Here the invitation and movement through the house disappear. Entwined limbs and Helen’s loosened lap make their reunion more immediate and bodily.

The closing “flames” turn this private moment into a warning about Troy’s future. The flames are Paris himself: Helen has just called him “my flame,” and at his first appearance in this book he was called Troy’s “deadly flame.” Receiving him as her lover, she also takes into her embrace the fire that will destroy the city. That double meaning is an addition to Homer’s account of the couple lying together. It also answers Paris’s confidence in the preceding passage: he expects the goddess of love to secure his success, while the narrator points instead to the catastrophe bound up with his passion.
