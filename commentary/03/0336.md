### 336–338 (*Iliad* 3.441, 3.447–448)

> 336 Post haec amplexus per mutua corpora iunctis
> (After this he embraced her, their bodies joined limb to limb,)
>
> 337 incubuit membris Cygneidos; illa soluto
> (and lay upon Cygneis; opening her lap,)
>
> 338 accepit flammas gremio Troiaeque suasque.
> (she received both Troy’s flames and his own.)

Paris’s promise of victory gives way immediately to an embrace. The warrior who has just defended his honour returns to his role as Helen’s lover, and her reproaches give way to acceptance. Cygneis, “swan-daughter,” is Helen: the name recalls the story that Jove, taking the form of a swan, approached her mother Leda.

The Latin compresses the couple’s return to bed into a single physical encounter. In Homer, Paris invites Helen to “turn to love and lie together” (*Iliad* 3.441); he then leads the way to bed, his wife follows, and they lie down together (*Iliad* 3.447–448). Here the invitation and movement through the house disappear. Entwined limbs and Helen’s open lap make their reunion more immediate and bodily.

The closing “flames” turn this private moment into a warning about Troy’s future. They are Paris’s flames of desire, but also the destructive fire his love will bring upon the city. Helen receives him as a lover and, figuratively, takes Troy’s coming ruin into her embrace. That double meaning is an addition to Homer’s account of the couple lying together. It also answers Paris’s confidence in the preceding passage: he expects the goddess of love to secure his success, while the narrator points instead to the catastrophe bound up with his passion.
