### 317–319 (*Iliad* 3.383–384, 3.424–427)

> 317 Ipsa dehinc Helenam muris accersit ab altis
> (Then she herself summons Helen from the high walls)
>
> 318 Dardanioque suos Paridi deducit amores.
> (and leads his beloved to Dardanian Paris.)
>
> 319 Quem tali postquam conspexit uoce locutast:
> (After she had seen him, she spoke these words:)

Having rescued Paris, Venus now brings Helen to him. Helen has been watching the duel from Troy’s walls; the goddess draws her away from that public scene into the private chamber where Paris has found safety. “Dardanian” means Trojan, recalling Dardanus, an ancestor of Troy’s royal family. Calling Helen Paris’s “beloved” presents the reunion in the language of love, though the words she is about to speak will hardly be affectionate.

The Latin compresses a long and troubled encounter into a simple summons and escort. In Homer, Aphrodite—Venus’s Greek name—approaches Helen disguised as an elderly woman who once worked wool for her. Helen recognizes the goddess and refuses to go to Paris, ashamed at the prospect of returning to his bed after his defeat. Only when Aphrodite angrily threatens her does Helen obey (*Iliad* 3.383–420). By leaving out this resistance and intimidation, the Latin makes Venus’s control of the reunion appear effortless and gives no indication here of Helen’s reluctance.

Homer also has the goddess place a chair opposite Paris for Helen. Helen sits down, turns her eyes away, and rebukes her husband (*Iliad* 3.424–427). The Latin omits these gestures and moves directly from her arrival to her speech. Its neutral introduction leaves Helen’s coming words to reveal what she thinks of the man Venus has saved.
