Paris retreats among his companions, but his brother Hector will not let him escape disgrace. Hector, a leading defender of Troy, sees this cowardice as a stain on both their family and their homeland. His accusation turns on the contrast between Paris’s earlier boldness and his present fear. Menelaus had received Paris as a guest; Paris then took his wife, Helen. He dared to violate the household that sheltered him, but now refuses to face the man he wronged.

The Latin gives this contrast a pointed military image: to seduce Helen was to “storm” her marriage bed. Paris could pursue an erotic conquest, winning over another man’s wife, but cannot stand his ground in an actual battle. Homer’s Hector likewise recalls Paris bringing a beautiful wife from abroad and challenges him to discover what sort of warrior her husband is (*Iliad* 3.46–49, 3.52–53). The Latin leaves out the voyage and Paris’s companions on that expedition, concentrating instead on the betrayal of hospitality and the confrontation he now avoids.

Hector’s appeal to Paris’s former prowess in athletic contests is an addition to the corresponding Homeric speech. It sharpens the distinction between strength displayed in games and courage proved under threat of death. Beauty is equally useless here. Homer names Paris’s lyre, the gifts of the love goddess, his hair, and his handsome appearance, imagining how little they will help when he lies in the dust (*Iliad* 3.54–55). The Latin compresses these attractions into “fine looks” and sets against them Mars, the Roman god of war, who demands toughness rather than charm.

The closing reproach is also added. Hector bitterly assigns Paris a life of idle pleasure while the rest of the Trojans fight and bleed. “Of course” makes the resentment unmistakable: this is no willing offer to protect his brother’s happiness. Paris enjoys the love that caused the war, while others must pay for it with their lives.
