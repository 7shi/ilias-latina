### 290–301 (*Iliad* 3.346–349, 3.355–359)

> 290 hastam deinde iacit, quam deuitauit Atrides
> (spear he then hurls, which Atrides avoided,)
>
> 291 inque uicem misso fixisset corpora telo
> (and with a spear thrown in return he would have pierced the body)
>
> 292 praedonis Phrygii, ni uastum ferrea pectus
> (of the Phrygian robber, had not the broad chest an iron)
>
> 293 texisset lorica uiri septemplice tergo.
> (corslet protected, the man’s, with seven layers of hide.)
>
> 294 Insequitur iuxta clamor; tum aduersus uterque
> (A shout follows close by; then each, facing the other,)
>
> 295 constitit et galeam galea terit et pede plantam
> (takes his stand, rubs helmet against helmet, and sole to foot)
>
> 296 coniungit stridetque mucro mucrone corusco;
> (joins, and blade clashes shrilly against flashing blade;)
>
> 297 corpus collectum tegitur fulgentibus armis.
> (each draws his body together behind the shelter of his gleaming shield.)
>
> 298 Non aliter fortes nitida de coniuge tauri
> (Just so do mighty bulls, fighting over a sleek mate,)
>
> 299 bella gerunt uastisque replent mugitibus auras.
> (wage war and fill the air with mighty bellows.)
>
> 300 Atque diu rigido captabant corpora ferro,
> (And long they sought an opening to wound each other with unyielding steel,)
>
> 301 cum memor Atrides raptae sibi coniugis instat
> (when Atrides, remembering the wife stolen from him, presses on)

The spear-cast completes Paris’s movement away from Menelaus at the end of the preceding passage. Atrides—“son of Atreus,” here meaning Menelaus—avoids the weapon and answers with a throw that nearly kills his opponent. Calling Paris the “Phrygian robber” keeps the stolen Helen at the center of the fight. “Phrygian” loosely identifies him with the peoples of Asia Minor, where Troy stands; “robber” presents his taking of another man’s wife as the defining wrong behind their quarrel.

The exchange preserves Homer’s sequence of Paris throwing first and Menelaus replying, but changes what saves each fighter. In the *Iliad*, Paris’s spear strikes Menelaus’s shield without breaking through it: “the point bent back in the strong shield” (*Iliad* 3.348–349). Menelaus’s answering spear penetrates Paris’s shield and corslet and cuts through his tunic; Paris escapes death by swerving aside (*Iliad* 3.355–360). Here Menelaus avoids the first spear, while Paris survives because his armor holds. The iron corslet, reinforced with seven layers of hide, becomes a formidable barrier rather than something Menelaus’s weapon can pierce.

The Latin then adds an extended struggle at close quarters. Helmets touch, feet press together, and swords clash as both men keep their bodies sheltered behind their shields. Their nearness makes the duel feel locked and desperate: neither can easily strike without exposing himself. The comparison with two bulls fighting over a mate turns their human rivalry into a spectacle of animal strength and possessiveness. The bellowing bulls also echo the noise surrounding the combat.

For a time the fighters seem evenly matched, each searching for an opening. But the passage ends by returning to Menelaus’s particular grievance. Helen is not merely the prize of a contest: she is the wife taken from him. Remembering that loss renews his attack and carries the action into the next passage.
