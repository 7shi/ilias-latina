Menelaus now takes his place opposite Alexander, or Paris. He is Helen’s husband, and his challenge makes their quarrel intensely personal: the woman Paris has taken remains, in Menelaus’s words, “my wife.” His threat imagines Helen mourning the loss of Paris, turning his rival’s present happiness into approaching grief. Yet the confidence depends on a condition. Menelaus needs the support of Jupiter, the Roman name for Zeus, the chief god.

The Latin preserves Homer’s balance between the two armed champions but changes the direction of Menelaus’s speech. In the *Iliad*, he addresses the god directly: “Lord Zeus, grant that I punish the man who first wronged me, noble Alexander, and bring him down beneath my hands” (*Iliad* 3.351–352). Here that prayer becomes a challenge addressed to Paris, with divine help invoked only at the end. The added reference to Helen’s anticipated grief sharpens the taunt: Menelaus threatens not merely to defeat Paris but to end his enjoyment of their stolen marriage.

The fighting also begins differently. Homer first places the champions close together “in the measured space” (*Iliad* 3.344), and Paris makes the first spear-cast. The Latin instead gives Menelaus an immediate, fierce rush, answered by a forceful blow from Paris. For the moment, Paris proves capable of resisting him; his quick retreat creates distance for the next exchange, a spear thrown from afar, whose whistling evokes the sound of a weapon cutting through the air.
