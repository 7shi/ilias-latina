### 314–316 (*Iliad* 3.379–382)

> 314 et magnam ualidis contorsit uiribus hastam
> (and hurled his great spear with all his might)
>
> 315 in cladem Phrygii, sua quem Venus eripit hosti
> (to destroy the Phrygian, whom his own Venus snatches from his foe)
>
> 316 et secum in thalamos defert testudine cultos.
> (and carries with her to chambers adorned with tortoiseshell.)

Menelaus renews his attack, but Venus again saves Paris. “The Phrygian” means Paris: the Latin uses the name of a people of Asia Minor for the Trojan prince. Venus, the goddess of love, is “his own” because he is devoted to her and enjoys her favour. Her protection overrides the apparent outcome of the duel: Menelaus has gained the upper hand, yet cannot finish off his opponent.

The Latin makes the attack more definite than Homer does. In the *Iliad*, Menelaus rushes forward, eager to kill Paris with his bronze spear; here he actually hurls it. Homer then describes Aphrodite—the Greek name for Venus—rescuing Paris with divine ease, hiding him in thick mist, and setting him down in his fragrant bedchamber (*Iliad* 3.379–382). The Latin omits the concealing mist and moves straight from mortal danger to the shelter of the bedroom.

The tortoiseshell decoration is an addition. In place of Homer’s fragrance, it gives the chamber a costly material splendour, recalling the luxury of wealthy Roman interiors rather than the furnishings specified in Homer’s scene. Paris is whisked away from the violence of the battlefield into comfort, while Menelaus’s force and determination are once more frustrated by a goddess.
