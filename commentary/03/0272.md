### 272–276 (*Iliad* 3.59, 3.64–75)

> 275 nec uires temptare uiri dextramque recuso,

> 276 dummodo uictorem coniunx cum pace sequatur."

Paris accepts the challenge Hector has proposed, but pushes back against his brother’s reproach. Calling Hector the “glory of our homeland” acknowledges his standing as Troy’s defender, while Paris insists that he too values a warrior’s honor. To “test” his opponent’s strength and right hand means to discover through combat how formidable he is. The opponent is Menelaus, whose wife Helen Paris has taken to Troy. Paris calls her his own wife here, but agrees that she should go with whichever man wins.

The reply differs sharply from Paris’s answer in Homer. There he begins by admitting that Hector has rebuked him “justly, and not beyond what is just” (*Iliad* 3.59). He then defends the lovely gifts of Aphrodite, the goddess of love: gifts freely bestowed by the gods should not be despised (*Iliad* 3.64–66). The Latin replaces that defense with a claim that neither his wife nor a life of corrupt pleasure comes before valor. Paris thus answers Hector on Hector’s own terms, asserting the very priorities his conduct has called into question.

His final condition makes the duel a possible settlement of the war, not merely a private contest. Homer spells out the arrangements: the winner takes Helen and her possessions home, the armies swear peace, the Trojans remain in Troy, and the Greeks return to their country (*Iliad* 3.71–75). Here those provisions shrink to the wife, the victor, and peace. With Hector already having proposed the duel, Paris’s role is to accept it and stipulate that its outcome should end the wider conflict.
