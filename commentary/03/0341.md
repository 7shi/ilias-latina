### 341–343 (*Iliad* 3.455–459)

> 341 Quem frater socias acuens in bella cateruas
> (Him his brother, spurring his comrades’ bands to battle,)
>
> 342 adiuuat et forti pulsos Phrygas increpat ore
> (aids, and with a forceful voice rebukes the defeated Phrygians,)
>
> 343 seruarique iubet leges Helenamque reposcit.
> (orders that the terms be kept and demands Helen back.)

Menelaus’s brother Agamemnon, commander of the Greek expedition, now supports his claim to victory. The “Phrygians” are the Trojans, here named after another people of Asia Minor. Before the duel, both sides had sworn to accept its outcome: the winner would take Helen and her possessions, and the fighting would end. Paris’s disappearance does not cancel that agreement. Agamemnon therefore demands that the Trojans honor its terms and return Helen to Menelaus.

The Latin turns Homer’s formal address into a brief, forceful intervention. In the *Iliad*, Agamemnon calls on the Trojans and their allies to listen, declares Menelaus the victor, and demands Helen, her possessions, and appropriate compensation (*Iliad* 3.455–459). Here the possessions and compensation disappear, leaving Helen herself at the center of the demand. The description of the Trojans as “defeated” extends Paris’s individual loss to his whole side, while Agamemnon’s encouragement of his troops adds a threat of renewed fighting absent from Homer’s speech. The agreed settlement remains the stated goal, but military pressure now stands behind it.
