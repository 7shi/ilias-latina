### 320–331 (*Iliad* 3.428–429, 3.432–433, 3.430–431, 3.434–436)

> 331 Dixit. Tum largis perfudit fletibus ora.
> (She spoke. Then she drenched her face with copious tears.)

Helen’s rebuke mingles shame at Paris’s defeat with fear for his life. Her “former husband” is Menelaus, whom she left for Paris. He is also the man called Atrides, “son of Atreus”; although that name can describe his brother Agamemnon too, here it means Paris’s opponent in the duel. “Doric” is a poetic word for Greek, after the Dorians, one of the Greek peoples, while the “Ilian dust” is the dust of Troy. Helen recalls the humiliating sight of Paris being dragged away by his enemy, but what most frightens her is the possibility that his death might end their intimacy. The warrior’s endangered life becomes, in her language, their endangered kisses.

This is a striking change from Homer’s encounter. There Helen begins: “You have come back from battle. If only you had died there, overcome by the strong man who was once my husband” (*Iliad* 3.428–429). The Latin keeps his defeat and her former marriage at the opening, but replaces the wish for Paris’s death with the affectionate address “my flame.” It then adds her account of watching the fight, her terror, and the loss of colour and strength in her body. Her shame remains, but the surrounding language makes it part of a distressed lover’s reproach rather than an expression of contempt alone.

The warning against another encounter also reshapes Homer. His Helen reminds Paris that he once boasted of being stronger than Menelaus, then challenges him to fight again before withdrawing the challenge and warning that Menelaus may kill him (*Iliad* 3.430–436). Here the boast disappears. Instead, Helen asks who encouraged such a dangerous contest and whether Paris has heard of his opponent’s courage. Her warning not to place his fate again in Menelaus’s hand, in a fight he cannot win, is more directly protective. The closing tears, also added in the Latin, leave the reunion suspended between reproach and attachment: she is ashamed of Paris, yet terrified of losing him.
